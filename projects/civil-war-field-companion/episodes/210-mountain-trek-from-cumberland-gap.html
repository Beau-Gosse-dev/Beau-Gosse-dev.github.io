MOUNTAIN TREK FROM CUMBERLAND GAP

Successful United States evacuation. Brig. Gen. George W. Morgan surrendered the strategic Cumberland Gap and destroyed stores and fixed defenses that he could not remove, but brought roughly 7,000 soldiers, about 500 wagons, every mobile gun, and the army’s baggage to the Ohio River after a 219-mile, sixteen-day march. Confederate forces occupied the Gap and harassed the column but failed to trap or destroy the Seventh Division
Episode scopeThe siege, evacuation, and mountain retreat from Cumberland GapThe episode first surveys Confederate pressure on Cincinnati and Louisville, then follows George W. Morgan’s isolated division from the September 14 evacuation decision to its October 3 arrival at the Ohio River.
Strategic settingTwo Confederate armies were operating in KentuckyBraxton Bragg moved northeast from Munfordville while Edmund Kirby Smith occupied Lexington and sent detachments toward Cincinnati and Louisville. Their movements isolated the United States garrison at Cumberland Gap.
Cincinnati alarmHenry Heth remained south of the city until September 18Heth probed the defenses with about 6,000 Confederates but did not assault. Thousands of civilians prepared defenses on both sides of the Ohio River.
The Black BrigadeMore than 700 Black Cincinnatians built defensesAfter an initial forced roundup, the men were permitted to volunteer and organized as the Black Brigade of Cincinnati. They worked on fortifications in northern Kentucky without arms or uniforms.
Emergency command at LouisvilleCapt. Charles C. Gilbert became an acting major generalDepartment commander Horatio G. Wright promoted the forty-year-old Regular Army captain without statutory authority and placed him over the city’s defenses. More than 20,000 United States troops were present by mid-September.
GarrisonAbout 7,000 soldiers in the Seventh DivisionGeorge W. Morgan’s division comprised four brigades under Samuel P. Carter, James G. Spears, John F. De Courcy, and Absalom Baird. Many soldiers in the Tennessee regiments were East Tennessee Unionists.
A three-state fortressKentucky, Tennessee, and Virginia met at the GapFortifications crowned the heights above the Wilderness Road. Depending on his post, a soldier could be standing in any one of the three states.
InvestmentCarter L. Stevenson’s division watched the GapKirby Smith bypassed the fortress during his advance into Kentucky and left roughly 9,000 men under Stevenson. The Confederate ring was strongest south of the Gap and too weak for a direct assault.
CommunicationsCouriers replaced the telegraphThe isolated garrison communicated with Cincinnati over lightly guarded mountain roads. Delays made both relief and reliable strategic direction difficult.
Supply crisisBread gone September 6; animal forage nearly gone September 12A 150-wagon convoy had arrived on August 19, but Morgan immediately imposed half rations. Loss of the horses and mules would have immobilized the artillery and wagon train.
Decision to evacuateCouncil of war, September 14Morgan met Baird, Spears, Carter, and chief engineer Lt. William P. Craighill. All accepted Craighill’s conclusion that the post should be abandoned.
The chosen routeNorth through the mountains rather than west toward LexingtonThe Wilderness Road led toward Kirby Smith’s main army. The alternative promised about 200 miles of poor roads, scarce food and forage, and easily blocked defiles, but it led away from the largest Confederate concentrations.
Evacuation orderMove on September 17; destroy what could not be carriedAn advance wagon train left on the evening of September 16. The main body departed at 8 p.m. the next day, with Spears’s East Tennessee regiments among the last troops to leave.
Rear guardAbout 200 selected soldiers under Col. George W. GallupGallup’s detachment held back Confederate probes and burned post buildings. The fires illuminated the valley as the division moved north.
DemolitionFixed guns, forts, magazines, and the south road disabledFour heavy cannon were spiked and pushed over a cliff. Craighill’s engineers exploded magazines and fortifications and blocked the road through the pass to delay pursuit.
First rendezvousManchester, September 19The entire division and approximately 500 wagons gathered after a sixty-mile first leg. The column paused briefly to reorganize before turning toward the Kentucky River.
Kirby Smith’s wrong assumptionHe expected a move toward Richmond and LexingtonSmith sent Heth toward Mount Sterling and John Hunt Morgan toward Irvine, concentrating northwest of the actual route. This left the critical Kentucky River crossing at Proctor unopposed.
Proctor crossingSeptember 21The bridge over the Kentucky River was intact even though John Hunt Morgan’s cavalry had passed through the previous night. George Morgan sent a brigade and battery across before resting the column.
Two roads to Hazel GreenThe division split between the Ridge and North Fork roadsGeorge Morgan’s published account places De Courcy and Spears on the dry Ridge Road and Baird and Carter with the wagon train on the damaged but water-rich North Fork Road.
Hazel GreenReached September 23The half-famished troops and animals halted for a full day before continuing twelve miles toward West Liberty.
Rear-guard attackSix United States soldiers killed before West LibertyConfederate cavalry struck the Sixth Tennessee Infantry at the rear and scattered roughly eighty to one hundred cattle before being driven away.
West Liberty to GraysonForty-three miles through a cavalry gauntletJohn Hunt Morgan’s horsemen moved ahead of the column, felled trees, rolled rocks into defiles, destroyed forage, and prepared ambushes.
Road-clearing forceAbout 1,000 soldiers with tools and block and tackleThe pioneers cleared repeated obstructions. At one blockade they cut four miles of new road through forest to bypass a one-mile section.
Final stagesGrayson October 1; Greenupsburg October 3John Hunt Morgan was recalled after the column reached Grayson. Two days later the Seventh Division crossed the Ohio River to safety.
March total219 miles in sixteen daysThe division emerged with every mobile gun and wagon and all of its baggage. The episode reports eighty men killed, wounded, or missing during the retreat.
No senior leader casualtyNo important commander was killed or mortally woundedThe operation’s losses fell among the marching columns and rear guards; the episode identifies no senior United States or Confederate leader killed or mortally wounded during the evacuation.

What happened

Bragg leaves Munfordville

Shortages of food, forage, and water forced Bragg to give up his position between Buell and Louisville and turn toward Bardstown and Kirby Smith.

Kirby Smith spreads across north-central Kentucky

Confederate detachments operated within ten miles of Cincinnati and Louisville while Smith held Lexington.

Heth approaches Cincinnati

Henry Heth probed the defenses with about 6,000 men but did not risk an assault; the threat triggered mass defensive preparations.

Louisville is also in turmoil

John S. Scott’s cavalry had approached the city, survivors from Richmond were digging defenses, and Bull Nelson was sidelined by his wound.

Charles C. Gilbert receives two stars

Horatio Wright elevated the Regular Army captain to acting major general and gave him command of Louisville’s growing force.

The focus shifts to Cumberland Gap

Kirby Smith had bypassed the fortress and left Carter Stevenson to contain its isolated United States garrison.

George Morgan’s siege begins

Cut off since August 20, Morgan could probe nearby Confederate positions but could communicate with Cincinnati only by mountain courier.

Seven thousand soldiers hold the Gap

The Seventh Division contained four brigades, including many East Tennessee Unionists with relatives serving on the other side.

Both sides remain active

Stevenson’s pickets skirmished with United States outposts while Morgan launched raids to gather intelligence and prevent a passive defense.

A captured dispatch brings bad news

Morgan learned that John Hunt Morgan’s cavalry was active in eastern Kentucky and that another Confederate force might approach from southwest Virginia.

The bread is gone

Bread ran out on September 6; by September 12 the post also had almost no feed left for the animals needed to move artillery and wagons.

Morgan convenes a council of war

On September 14, his senior officers agreed with engineer William Craighill that Cumberland Gap must be evacuated.

The mountain route is chosen

A westward march risked Kirby Smith’s entire army, so Morgan chose the rugged road north toward the Ohio River.

Evacuation orders go out

The garrison prepared to move on September 17 and to destroy everything that could not accompany the column.

The garrison prepares in secrecy

Soldiers packed wagons, readied buildings for destruction, and spiked four heavy cannon that could not be moved.

The Seventh Division marches out

At 8 p.m. on September 17 the main body started for Manchester, with Spears’s East Tennessee regiments among the last to depart.

Gallup’s rear guard remains behind

Two hundred selected soldiers stayed to burn buildings and protect the withdrawal until the rest of the column was clear.

The Confederates detect the evacuation

Confederate probes moved toward the Gap, but Gallup’s pickets held them back while the fires spread through the post.

Craighill fires the mines

Explosions wrecked forts and magazines and blocked the road from the south before the final detachment marched away near dawn.

The division reaches Manchester

By September 19 the whole force and its 500 wagons had completed the first sixty miles, though Confederate cavalry remained close behind.

Kirby Smith reacts from Lexington

Smith sent Cleburne west, recalled Heth toward Mount Sterling, ordered John Hunt Morgan to Irvine, and told Stevenson to pursue from the Gap.

Smith predicts the wrong destination

Expecting George Morgan to move toward Richmond and Lexington, Smith concentrated forces northwest of Proctor rather than on the road to the Ohio.

The bridge at Proctor is intact

On September 21 the United States column crossed the Kentucky River without opposition, escaping a position that Morgan expected to be dangerous.

A Confederate order has cleared the crossing

Sending John Hunt Morgan to Irvine had moved the cavalry twenty-six miles away and cost the Confederates their first strong chance to stop the column.

The trek resumes toward Hazel Green

John Hunt Morgan hurried back and began harassing the flanks and rear while the soldiers struggled over dry ridges and ruined roads.

Hazel Green offers a day of rest

The division arrived September 23 and halted before the next twelve-mile leg to West Liberty.

Confederate attacks intensify

Cavalry struck the rear near West Liberty, killed six men of the Sixth Tennessee, and scattered part of the cattle herd.

The hardest forty-three miles begin

Between West Liberty and Grayson, Confederate cavalry blocked narrow roads with rocks and felled trees and prepared repeated ambushes.

The two Morgans contest every defile

As George Morgan cleared one end of a gorge, John Hunt Morgan obstructed the other. A thousand soldiers labored with axes, saws, picks, spades, and tackle.

The column reaches Grayson

Five days of obstruction and skirmishing ended on October 1, when the United States division entered Grayson and the Confederate cavalry was recalled.

The Ohio River means safety

On October 3 the column reached Greenupsburg and crossed into Ohio, beyond the Confederate pursuit.

The march is measured

In sixteen days the Seventh Division covered 219 miles through eastern Kentucky’s mountains despite hunger, bad roads, and continual harassment.

Morgan saves the train

The episode reports eighty men killed, wounded, or missing, but every mobile gun and all the army’s wagons and baggage reached the Ohio.

The episode recommends McKnight

Contested Borderland examines both military operations and divided civilian communities in Appalachian Kentucky and Virginia.
