"I HAVE COME TO YOU FROM THE WEST"

The Lincoln administration consolidated three independent Federal commands as the Army of Virginia under Maj. Gen. John Pope. The new army could not immediately cooperate with George B. McClellan’s Army of the Potomac because Robert E. Lee’s army stood between them. Henry W. Halleck became general-in-chief, McClellan’s Peninsula army was ordered north, and Pope’s July addresses and orders marked a more coercive Federal policy toward Confederate property, guerrillas, and disloyal civilians
Episode scopeJohn Pope’s background, the creation of the Army of Virginia, the Federal command problem after the Seven Days, and the policy dispute between Pope and McClellanThe narrative covers Pope’s earlier career before following events from his June appointment through the August 3 order withdrawing the Army of the Potomac from the Peninsula.
John Pope in 1862Forty years old; West Point class of 1842Pope was born in Louisville, Kentucky, grew up in Illinois, served in the Topographical Engineers, and received Mexican War brevets for Monterrey and Buena Vista.
Western recordNew Madrid, Island No. 10, and the advance on CorinthPope’s Army of the Mississippi captured New Madrid in March and Island No. 10 in April 1862, then joined Halleck’s advance on Corinth. These operations established the reputation that brought Pope east.
Army of Virginia createdJune 26, 1862Lincoln’s order combined the commands of John C. Frémont, Nathaniel P. Banks, and Irvin McDowell and assigned the new army to Pope.
Army of Virginia missionProtect Washington and western Virginia, defeat Jackson and Ewell, threaten toward Charlottesville, and aid McClellanThose four requirements appear in Lincoln’s June 26 order. They imposed simultaneous defensive and offensive responsibilities on Pope’s new command.
Initial strength and distributionAbout 45,000 men spread across northern VirginiaThe U.S. Army campaign summary gives approximately 45,000. The episode describes the three commands as occupying an arc of roughly 60 miles from the lower Shenandoah Valley to Fredericksburg.
I CorpsMountain Department troops under Frémont, then Franz SigelFrémont, who was senior to Pope, asked to be relieved rather than serve under him. Stanton selected Sigel as the replacement.
II CorpsShenandoah Department troops under Nathaniel P. BanksBanks’s army had been defeated by Jackson at Front Royal and Winchester during the preceding Valley Campaign.
III CorpsIrvin McDowell’s Department of the Rappahannock troopsPart of McDowell’s command under James Shields had been defeated at Port Republic. Troops retained inside Washington’s fortifications were excluded from the new corps.
Pope’s first headquartersThe War Department in WashingtonPope initially directed concentration from the capital and followed the Seven Days through the War Department telegraph office. He did not join his field command until late July.
McClellan at Harrison’s LandingArmy of the Potomac on the James River from July 2The Seven Days ended with McClellan separated from Pope by Richmond and Lee’s Army of Northern Virginia. McClellan wanted reinforcements and a renewed Peninsula offensive.
Pope’s strategic objectionA direct march toward Richmond would expose his army to Lee before McClellan could support itPope made this argument in his July 4 letter to McClellan. He proposed holding a line that protected Washington and denied easy Confederate access to the Shenandoah until a coordinated plan existed.
New general-in-chiefHenry W. Halleck, appointed July 11, 1862Lincoln restored the post that had been vacant since he removed McClellan from the general-in-chief role in March. Halleck visited Harrison’s Landing July 24–27 to assess McClellan’s position.
Burnside and Army of the Potomac commandThe episode reports that Ambrose E. Burnside declined an offer in late JulyThe hosts place the offer around July 25 and state that Burnside doubted his fitness for the larger command and was loyal to McClellan. The National Park Service confirms that Burnside declined an offer to replace McClellan during the summer crisis.
Peninsula withdrawal orderAugust 3, 1862Halleck ordered the Army of the Potomac transferred from the Peninsula toward Aquia Creek so it could join Pope and cover Washington.
Army condition in late JulyThree corps needing concentration, refit, and resupplyThe episode also emphasizes morale and command friction. Pope used public language and orders to promote a more aggressive identity for the new army.
Pope’s address to the armyJuly 14, 1862The address contrasted Pope’s western service with eastern concern for defensive positions, supply bases, and retreat. The episode’s title comes from its opening claim that Pope had come from the West.
Reaction inside the armiesSome enlisted soldiers approved; many officers resented the criticismThe episode describes the strongest hostility among Army of Virginia officers senior to Pope and among McClellan’s circle in the Army of the Potomac. Fitz John Porter condemned the address in a July 17 private letter.
Administration role in the addressStanton influenced its content and Lincoln approved it in the episode’s accountThe hosts interpret the address as an administration-backed criticism of McClellan’s military approach, not simply an unsanctioned statement of Pope’s personality.
General Orders No. 5Army to subsist from the country in its area of operationsPope permitted requisition of food and forage and distinguished receipts for loyal citizens from vouchers payable only after restoration of peace for those who supported the Confederacy.
General Orders No. 7Collective penalties for guerrilla attacks and damage to communicationsThe order held nearby civilians responsible for repairing railroads, roads, telegraphs, and bridges and authorized severe punishment for attacks made from civilian cover.
General Orders No. 11Loyalty oaths, expulsion, and arrest of disloyal male civiliansMen who took the oath and provided security could remain. Those who refused were to be sent beyond Federal lines and treated as spies if they returned.
Policy purpose and effectSuppress guerrillas and shift the burdens of war onto active Confederate supportersThe National Archives and National Park Service describe the orders as administration-approved measures that allowed executions, financial assessments, confiscation, requisition, and destruction of property. Their broad collective penalties also endangered civilians.
McClellan’s opposing policyA limited war directed against Confederate armed forces rather than the Southern populationMcClellan’s July 7 Harrison’s Landing letter advised against subjugation, confiscation, and forcible abolition. His later instructions warned the Army of the Potomac against plunder.
Second Confiscation ActApproved July 17, 1862The statute expanded Federal authority against rebel property and declared free enslaved people held by persons engaged in rebellion who came under United States control under the act’s terms.
Confederate responseJackson sent toward Gordonsville on July 13Lee moved Jackson north to protect the Virginia Central Railroad junction and confront Pope while Longstreet remained near Richmond. The deployment deepened the danger created by the two separated Federal armies.
Battle losses in this episodeNoneThis is an organizational, strategic, and policy episode. It reviews earlier battles and prepares the Northern Virginia Campaign but does not narrate a new battle.

What happened

The previous episode’s turning point is reviewed

The Seven Days and the failed Peninsula Campaign are linked to Lincoln’s decision to bring Pope and Halleck east.

The episode turns to John Pope

The narrative begins with Pope’s reputation and the circumstances behind his selection for the new Army of Virginia.

A correspondent describes Pope

An August 1862 interview portrays an energetic, confident, self-promoting officer whose strengths were paired with vanity and imprudence.

Pope’s age and origins are introduced

He was forty, born in Kentucky, and raised in Illinois when he took command in Virginia.

West Point and Mexico establish his professional record

Pope graduated in 1842 and earned brevets for Monterrey and Buena Vista during the Mexican War.

Professional ability and abrasive behavior are paired

The episode describes Pope as an expert horseman and capable officer with an impetuous, abrasive personality.

Pope enters Civil War volunteer service

He received a brigadier general’s commission in 1861 and served in Missouri before gaining an army command.

Pope receives the Army of the Mississippi

In February 1862 he took command of the Federal field army operating against Confederate positions on the Mississippi River.

New Madrid and Island No. 10 build Pope’s reputation

The successful campaign forced two important Confederate river positions to surrender in March and April.

Pope joins Halleck’s advance on Corinth

The Army of the Mississippi became part of Halleck’s large combined force after Shiloh, and Confederates evacuated Corinth.

Lincoln selects a western commander

Pope’s victories and lack of involvement in the near-disaster at Shiloh made him a prominent candidate for the Virginia assignment.

Politics also favor Pope

His Republican affiliation, support for abolition, and preference for aggressive war aligned him with the administration’s changing priorities.

Pope reaches Washington

He arrived on June 22 after being summoned from the Western Theater.

The Valley Campaign shapes the new assignment

Banks, Frémont, and part of McDowell’s command had each been defeated during Jackson’s operations in the Shenandoah Valley.

Stanton explains the consolidation

Three independent commands in northern Virginia are to become one army under Pope.

Gordonsville becomes the operational objective

A movement toward the Virginia Central Railroad junction was intended to pressure Richmond and support McClellan.

Pope receives a mixed welcome

Northern newspapers respond more favorably than soldiers and officers in the commands being placed under an unfamiliar general.

All three corps commanders outrank Pope

Frémont, Banks, and McDowell were senior in rank, creating immediate resentment within the new command structure.

Frémont asks to be relieved

He refuses the effective demotion to corps command under Pope and leaves active field service.

Stanton selects Franz Sigel

The secretary of war, rather than Pope, chooses Sigel to replace Frémont in command of I Corps.

McClellan learns of the new army

The Army of the Potomac commander had not been consulted about the creation of the Army of Virginia or Pope’s appointment.

Lee opens the Seven Days

The June 26 attack at Mechanicsville begins the campaign that drives McClellan away from Richmond as Pope’s appointment is announced.

Pope assumes command

He takes formal command on June 27, the day of Gaines’s Mill, and begins concentrating the scattered army.

The three corps span a wide arc

The episode places them from the lower Shenandoah Valley to Fredericksburg along the Rappahannock.

Pope remains at the War Department

He directs the army from Washington rather than immediately establishing headquarters with the troops.

The telegraph office becomes Pope’s observation post

From Washington he monitors McClellan’s battles and advises Lincoln as the president follows the campaign by telegraph.

Pope criticizes the retreat to the James

He argues that abandoning the White House Landing line allows Lee to stand between the two Federal armies.

Pope presents his concern to the cabinet

The episode states that he repeated the strategic objection at a June 28 cabinet meeting.

Harrison’s Landing sharpens the disagreement

After McClellan establishes the James River base on July 2, he asks for reinforcements and wants Pope to open a second front north of Richmond.

Pope writes to McClellan

His July 4 letter begins with a promise of cooperation and then explains why he cannot safely move directly on Richmond.

The Seven Days disrupt Pope’s original plan

Pope states that the recent operations have changed the circumstances under which he expected to aid the Peninsula advance.

Pope defines a defensive interim mission

He proposes guarding Washington and denying Confederate access to the Shenandoah rather than fighting Lee alone near Richmond.

The Federal strategy reaches an impasse

McClellan will not renew operations before receiving major reinforcements, while Pope will not advance without a workable cooperation plan.

Lincoln seeks a superior for both army commanders

The administration responds to the divided command by restoring the position of general-in-chief.

Halleck becomes general-in-chief

The former western department commander is brought to Washington to coordinate Pope and McClellan.

Halleck inspects Harrison’s Landing

He visits July 24–27 with authority to assess McClellan’s position and continued command.

Burnside declines the larger army

The episode reports that Burnside was offered the Army of the Potomac but doubted his ability and would not displace his friend McClellan.

McClellan’s reinforcement demand ends the Peninsula option

After Halleck considers renewed operations, McClellan again asks for a force Washington will not supply, and the army receives withdrawal orders.

Pope finally joins the army in the field

Halleck’s arrival removes Pope’s reason for remaining in Washington, although he is told to wait for McClellan’s troops before major offensive action.

The army waits on the Rapidan and Rappahannock

Pope holds while the administration expects the Peninsula transfer and the junction of the two Federal armies.

The new army needs refit and resupply

Each corps has logistical and organizational problems, and morale also requires attention.

Pope tries to impose an aggressive identity

He spends his first month promoting a forward-looking, offensive spirit within the army.

The July 14 address is introduced

The document defines Pope’s desired command culture and his relationship with eastern officers.

Pope contrasts western attack with eastern caution

He tells the army that he came from the West and dismisses repeated concern with strong positions, supply bases, and retreat routes.

The address becomes a lasting liability

Its confident language is later remembered in light of Pope’s defeat, but the episode notes that many enlisted soldiers initially welcomed it.

Army of Virginia officers resent the criticism

They read the address as an accusation that eastern commanders, rather than their soldiers, lacked determination.

McClellan’s circle reacts more strongly

The deliberate references to positions, retreat, and supply are understood as a direct criticism of the Peninsula Campaign.

Fitz John Porter condemns Pope

McClellan’s close ally responds to the address in a hostile July 17 private letter.

The administration’s role is identified

The episode states that Stanton influenced the text and Lincoln sanctioned its criticism of McClellan.

The failed Peninsula Campaign changes Federal policy

The narrative links McClellan’s retreat to a declining administration belief in conciliation as the central method for restoring the Union.

John Sherman describes support for harder war

The Ohio senator’s letter to his brother reflects growing pressure for confiscation, emancipation, and more forceful prosecution of the rebellion.

Pope issues coercive orders in Virginia

The episode summarizes requisition of property, penalties for guerrilla attacks, loyalty oaths, expulsion, and punishment of civilians who attacked Federal soldiers.

The orders divide the Federal armies

Many enlisted soldiers welcome the harder policy, while McClellan opposes its treatment of property and civilians.

McClellan issues contrary instructions

He warns against plunder and defines the conflict as a war against Confederate armed forces rather than the Southern population.

The policy is traced above Pope

Because Lincoln approved the orders, the episode treats them as evidence of an administration shift rather than an independent outburst by one general.

The Harrison’s Landing letter provides the contrast

McClellan’s July 7 defense of limited war is set against Pope’s orders and the Second Confiscation Act.

McClellan anticipates Pope’s defeat

After learning that Lee sent Jackson against Pope, McClellan privately predicts that Pope will retreat or be beaten and learn the value of logistics and retreat routes.

Peter Cozzens’s Pope biography is recommended

The episode closes with “General John Pope: A Life for the Nation,” a full biography of the controversial commander.
