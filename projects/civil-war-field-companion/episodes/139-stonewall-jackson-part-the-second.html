Episode 139 · April 22–November 5, 1861 · Richmond and Harpers Ferry, Virginia; the northern Shenandoah Valley from Williamsport and Falling Waters through Martinsburg and Winchester; Ashby’s Gap and Piedmont; and Manassas Junction and Henry Hill, Virginia

STONEWALL JACKSON (Part the Second)

Jackson converted Virginia volunteers at Harpers Ferry into the First Virginia Brigade, served under Joseph E. Johnston, conducted a fighting withdrawal at Falling Waters, and led his brigade in Johnston’s rapid movement to Manassas. The brigade’s stand and counterattack on Henry Hill helped produce the Confederate victory at First Manassas and gave Jackson and the brigade the name “Stonewall.” Promoted to major general in October, Jackson left the brigade on November 4 and assumed independent command in the Shenandoah Valley at Winchester on November 5
Episode scopeJackson’s first six months of Civil War commandThe narrative begins with his April 1861 Virginia commission and ends with his assumption of command at Winchester. First Manassas is summarized rather than retold in detail.
Harpers Ferry commandColonel of Virginia forces; arrived April 28, 1861Jackson found an unorganized concentration of volunteers and militia and imposed drill, discipline, and a brigade structure. Sources differ by one day on whether his command technically began April 27 or April 28.
Why Harpers Ferry matteredArmory, railroad, canal, river crossings, and the northern entrance to the ValleyIts industrial and transportation value was considerable, but surrounding heights made the town itself difficult to defend against artillery.
Maryland HeightsJackson occupied the commanding height in May 1861The National Park Service identifies this as one of Jackson’s earliest aggressive command decisions. It also created political risk because Maryland had not seceded.
First Virginia Brigade2nd, 4th, 5th, 27th, and 33rd Virginia Infantry with the Rockbridge ArtilleryThe formation became the Stonewall Brigade after First Manassas. The episode concentrates on Jackson’s training and discipline rather than enumerating every regiment.
B&O railroad seizureThe scale and method are disputedThe traditional Imboden story says Jackson manipulated train schedules and trapped 56 locomotives and more than 300 cars. The U.S. Army Center of Military History repeats the large seizure, but biographer James I. Robertson Jr., as the episode notes, rejected Imboden’s elaborate account for lack of contemporary documentation. Confederate interference with the B&O and seizures of railroad property are not in dispute.
Little SorrelJackson’s small sorrel gelding and best-known wartime mountThe episode links the horse to railroad property seized near Harpers Ferry and says Jackson paid his quartermaster for it. The exact acquisition story depends on later recollections and should not be treated as fully documented.
Johnston takes commandMay 23–24, 1861The Army history dates Johnston’s assumption of command to May 23; Encyclopedia Virginia gives May 24. Jackson initially required written evidence of Johnston’s authority, then accepted command of the First Brigade under him.
Withdrawal from Harpers FerryJune 14, 1861Johnston considered Winchester more defensible and more important to the Valley. Confederate troops destroyed government and railroad property as they left.
Falling Waters / Hoke’s RunJuly 2, 1861; United States tactical successJackson’s mission was to determine Patterson’s strength and retire if the United States force was substantial. His official report says he accomplished that mission and withdrew in order; the Army history describes Patterson as brushing the Confederates aside. Both descriptions fit a limited reconnaissance followed by a Confederate retreat.
Jackson’s force engaged380 men of the 5th Virginia and one artillery pieceJackson’s official report says three of Pendleton’s four guns were left behind. Two additional regiments moved in support but did not enter the action.
Falling Waters lossesContemporary and later totals conflictJackson reported 11 wounded and 9 missing in the 5th Virginia, several of the missing believed dead; Stuart reported 1 wounded and 2 missing. He also claimed 49 United States prisoners. The episode’s rounded statement of about two dozen Confederate casualties and a similar United States total should not be combined with later, higher estimates as if they were one settled count.
Promotion to brigadier generalAppointment dated June 17; news reached Jackson after Falling WatersThe episode places receipt of the commission a few days after the July 2 skirmish. The formal appointment date was earlier.
March to PiedmontAbout 20 miles in 14 hours, July 18–19The episode traces the brigade from Winchester through Millwood, a Shenandoah River ford, Ashby’s Gap, and Paris, followed by another six-mile march to the railroad at Piedmont.
Rail movement to ManassasAbout 34 miles from Piedmont; Jackson arrived July 19Johnston’s infantry used the Manassas Gap Railroad while cavalry and artillery moved overland. This concentration helped the two Confederate armies meet McDowell on July 21.
First ManassasConfederate victory, July 21, 1861Jackson’s roughly 2,500 Virginians formed on Henry Hill. The episode emphasizes his initiative, the brigade’s stand, and its counterattack; the full battle was covered in earlier podcast episodes.
“Stonewall” quotationAttributed to Brig. Gen. Barnard E. Bee Jr.The wording varies among accounts, and some contemporary evidence allows the remark to have been critical rather than purely complimentary. Bee was mortally wounded before he could explain it.
Tactical ideas emphasizedBayonet action, flank attacks, concentration, secrecy, and surpriseThe episode presents Jackson’s preference for the bayonet as increasingly outdated against rifled muskets, while identifying flank attack and concentration against a weak point as more consequential parts of his method.
Independent Valley commandPromoted October 7; assigned district command October 28; reached Winchester November 5Jackson bid farewell to the First Brigade on November 4. The episode’s “6,000-square-mile district” is a descriptive estimate, not a surveyed administrative area.
Book recommendationRebel Yell by S. C. GwynneThe transcript renders the author’s surname incorrectly. The full title is Rebel Yell: The Violence, Passion, and Redemption of Stonewall Jackson.

What happened

The story resumes in April 1861

Jackson and 176 VMI cadets reached Richmond, where the cadets trained Virginia recruits and Jackson received a colonel’s commission.

Jackson is ordered to Harpers Ferry

The episode introduces the armory town at the confluence of the Potomac and Shenandoah, on the B&O Railroad and beside the Chesapeake and Ohio Canal.

The position’s defensive weakness

Surrounding heights dominated the low town. Jackson nevertheless worked to fortify the area and occupied Maryland Heights during his command.

An unorganized garrison awaits him

The episode estimates about 2,500 volunteers and militiamen at Harpers Ferry, with little organization above company level.

Jackson imposes order and training

Militia reorganization removed supernumerary senior officers. Jackson drilled the recruits, enforced regulations, and helped form the First Virginia Brigade.

Secrecy becomes a command habit

The hosts connect Jackson’s reserve and secrecy to his desire to surprise the enemy, while noting that subordinates were often left without enough information.

Discipline is enforced by formal charges

Jackson expected literal compliance and regularly used arrest and court-martial proceedings against soldiers who left their posts or failed to perform duty.

The B&O becomes a military target

The railroad connected the Midwest and the East Coast and carried traffic across the Potomac at Harpers Ferry, making it strategically valuable to both sides.

Lee urges a defensive course

The episode quotes Lee’s May 12 warning about Maryland opinion. Jackson treated the qualifying phrase “if possible” as room for aggressive action.

The locomotive story is introduced and challenged

Imboden’s later story credits Jackson with trapping 56 locomotives and more than 300 cars. The episode immediately notes Robertson’s conclusion that this elaborate version was fictional.

Jackson acquires Little Sorrel

The episode links the gelding to a documented seizure of a train, says Jackson paid for the horse, and explains why its endurance suited his later campaigning.

Joseph E. Johnston supersedes Jackson

Jackson initially refused to yield without written authority. Once Johnston produced documentation, Jackson accepted brigade command in the Army of the Shenandoah.

Johnston withdraws toward Winchester

Facing Patterson’s larger force, Johnston abandoned Harpers Ferry in mid-June and sent Jackson forward toward Martinsburg with the First Brigade.

Patterson crosses the Potomac

On July 2 United States troops crossed near Williamsport. Stuart’s message reached Jackson outside Martinsburg at about 7:30 a.m.

Jackson begins a reconnaissance in force

Kenton Harper’s 5th Virginia moved toward Falling Waters with William Pendleton’s battery; Jackson took only one of its guns into the skirmish.

Skirmishers open the action

The Confederates fired from partly covered positions near the road. United States skirmishers recoiled, re-formed with supports, and pressed forward.

Pendleton’s gun checks the pursuit

A solid shot cleared the road and helped cover the Confederate retirement. The episode’s remembered benediction before firing is an anecdote, not part of Jackson’s official report.

Stuart captures United States soldiers

Separated temporarily from his cavalry, Stuart approached soldiers while still wearing a blue prewar uniform. Jackson’s official report lists 49 prisoners taken by Stuart’s command.

Jackson reaches Big Spring

The reconnaissance ended with Jackson about two and a half miles south of Martinsburg. Patterson occupied Martinsburg the next day.

Jackson receives brigadier general’s rank

The news reached him after Falling Waters, although the formal Confederate appointment was dated June 17.

Johnston orders the movement to Manassas

On July 18 the Army of the Shenandoah began evading Patterson to reinforce Beauregard against McDowell.

The First Brigade crosses the Blue Ridge

After an eleven-mile march to Millwood, the troops crossed the Shenandoah and Ashby’s Gap, reaching Paris at about 2 a.m. after nearly twenty miles.

A sentry story is labeled as legend

The episode says legend has Jackson allowing his exhausted men to sleep while he stood guard. No firm contemporary evidence is supplied for the story.

The brigade boards trains at Piedmont

After another six-mile march, Jackson’s infantry traveled about 34 miles by rail to Manassas Junction.

Henry Hill creates the Stonewall name

The hosts summarize Jackson’s stand and initiative at First Manassas. Barnard Bee’s comparison of Jackson to a stone wall became attached to both commander and brigade.

Jackson’s faith in the bayonet

The episode connects this preference to Mexican War doctrine, then explains that rifled muskets made a close bayonet finish increasingly difficult.

Flank attack and concentration matter more

Jackson’s stated principle was to avoid heavy odds, strike an exposed part of the enemy, and use maneuver, secrecy, and surprise to defeat forces in detail.

Jackson says farewell to the brigade

On November 4 about 1,800 men assembled near Bull Run. Jackson thanked the formation he had trained and led before leaving for independent command.

Jackson travels back across the Blue Ridge

He rode to Manassas Junction, went by train through Front Royal to Strasburg, then rode the Valley Turnpike to Winchester before midnight.

Independent command begins at Winchester

Early on November 5 Jackson notified Richmond that he had assumed command of the Valley district, setting the stage for the 1862 campaign.

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. Barnard E. Bee Jr.

Third Brigade, Army of the Shenandoah. Mortally wounded on Henry Hill at First Manassas on July 21, 1861. His reported comparison of Jackson to a stone wall became the source of Jackson’s enduring nickname.

Confederacy

Col. Francis S. Bartow

Second Brigade, Army of the Shenandoah. Killed at First Manassas on July 21, 1861 while leading his brigade in the fighting associated with the Confederate defense and counterattack.
