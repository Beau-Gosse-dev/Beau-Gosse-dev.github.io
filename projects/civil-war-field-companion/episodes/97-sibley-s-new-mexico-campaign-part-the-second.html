Episode 97 · February 21, 1862 · Valverde Ford on the Rio Grande, about six miles north of Fort Craig, New Mexico Territory

SIBLEY'S NEW MEXICO CAMPAIGN (Part the Second)

Confederate troops under Colonel Thomas Green captured six U.S. artillery pieces and forced Colonel Edward Canby’s field force back to Fort Craig. The Confederates held the battlefield but did not capture the fort, its garrison, or its supplies. Henry Sibley continued north with Fort Craig and more than 3,000 U.S. troops behind him

What happened

Canby took command in New Mexico

Edward R. S. Canby replaced William W. Loring after Loring resigned from the U.S. Army. Canby reorganized the department while officers and regular troops were leaving for service elsewhere.

Canby concentrated the territorial defenses

Canby delayed some troop withdrawals, requested New Mexico and Colorado volunteers, and placed forces at key points along the Rio Grande and Santa Fe Trail.

Fort Fillmore was reinforced

Canby sent supplies and troops to Fort Fillmore and placed its roughly 700-man garrison under Major Isaac Lynde. Lynde later abandoned the post and surrendered his column to John Baylor.

Sibley presented his plan to Jefferson Davis

Sibley argued that a Texas brigade could defeat the small U.S. force in New Mexico and sustain itself with captured federal supplies.

Sibley received authority to raise a brigade

Jefferson Davis commissioned Sibley as a Confederate brigadier general and authorized three cavalry regiments, artillery, and supporting units for the invasion.

The Army of New Mexico was organized in Texas

The 4th, 5th, and 7th Texas Mounted Volunteers formed the main force. With artillery and supply units, the brigade initially numbered about 3,200 men.

Sibley selected the regimental commanders

William R. Scurry led the 4th Texas, Thomas Green led the 5th Texas, and John S. Sutton led the 7th Texas.

The invasion moved north from Fort Thorn

On February 7, 1862, Sibley started 2,515 men, 15 artillery pieces, a supply train, and a cattle herd toward Fort Craig.

A Confederate detachment went to Tucson

Captain Sherod Hunter took 54 men west to protect Confederate supporters, watch for U.S. troops from California, and secure Sibley’s flank.

Canby assembled about 3,800 troops at Fort Craig

The force included about 1,200 regulars, more than 2,000 New Mexico volunteers, roughly 500 militia, and a small Colorado company.

The Confederates reached Fort Craig

By February 15, Sibley’s army was five to seven miles south of the fort. The strengthened post was too strong for a direct assault.

Green took field command

Sibley was unable to direct the movement on February 16. Colonel Thomas Green formed the Confederates south of the fort, but Canby kept the U.S. force behind its defenses.

The Confederates decided to bypass the fort

With about ten days of food left, Green’s council chose to cross to the east bank, march around Fort Craig, and recross at Valverde Ford.

Canby tried to stop the bypass

On February 20, U.S. troops moved against the Confederate dry camp. Artillery fire caused disorder among some New Mexico volunteers, and Canby ended the attack after dark.

U.S. cavalry reached Valverde Ford first

On February 21, Benjamin Roberts brought cavalry, infantry, and artillery north from Fort Craig and drove Charles Pyron’s advance guard away from the river.

The lancer attack failed

A company of 5th Texas lancers charged the Colorado volunteers. A close-range volley broke the attack; only three of about 70 lancers were reported unhurt.

Canby arrived and ordered an attack on the Confederate left

Canby recalled Kit Carson’s regiment and tried to reinforce the assault. Most of the 2nd New Mexico refused the order, and the attack did not develop as planned.

Green attacked both U.S. flanks

Henry Raguet’s attack on the U.S. right was repulsed. Samuel Lockridge then led about 750 dismounted Texans against Alexander McRae’s battery on the other flank.

The Texans captured McRae’s battery

The Confederates crossed open ground under canister fire, reached the guns, and fought at close range. Lockridge and McRae were both killed during the fighting.

Canby ordered a retreat

The loss of the battery broke the U.S. line. Canby withdrew the field force across the Rio Grande to Fort Craig, and a truce allowed both sides to collect casualties.

Fort Craig did not surrender

Sibley demanded the fort and its supplies the next morning. Canby refused because his force remained intact behind the fortifications.

The victory did not solve the Confederate supply problem

The Confederates held the field but had lost more than 200 men killed or wounded and had only about five days of provisions. The fort and its stores remained under U.S. control.

Sibley continued north

Unable to capture or besiege Fort Craig, Sibley chose to leave it behind and move toward the federal depots at Albuquerque and Santa Fe.
