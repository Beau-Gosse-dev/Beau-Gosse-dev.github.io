Episode 150 · May 24–25, 1862 · Washington, D.C., and northern Virginia; the battlefield south of Winchester from Bowers Hill across the Valley Turnpike to Camp Hill and the Front Royal Road, followed by the United States retreat through Winchester toward Martinsburg and the Potomac River

STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: FRONT ROYAL & WINCHESTER (Part the Fourth)

Decisive Confederate victory at the First Battle of Winchester. Jackson and Ewell attacked Banks’s exhausted command at daylight on May 25. The initial Confederate attack against Camp Hill failed, but Richard Taylor’s Louisiana Brigade turned the United States right on Bowers Hill and forced both Federal brigades to withdraw through Winchester. Banks preserved much of his mobile force by retreating north, while disorganized and misplaced Confederate cavalry failed to convert victory into destruction. Jackson cleared the northern Valley and disrupted the planned reinforcement of McClellan near Richmond; Lincoln responded by redirecting forces under McDowell and Frémont in an attempt to trap Jackson

Step 1 of 33

Robert Barton recalls the artillery advance

The opening reading follows Rockbridge Artillery private Robert T. Barton as the battery moved under shellfire south of Winchester. He describes clinging to a moving caisson and then finding Robert T. McKim dead beside the road.

Department of the RappahannockMaj. Gen. Irvin McDowellOrdered to put 20,000 men in motion toward the Shenandoah instead of continuing the planned advance from Fredericksburg toward Richmond
Mountain DepartmentMaj. Gen. John C. FrémontOrdered to advance east toward the Valley as the western arm of the proposed convergence behind Jackson
Department of the ShenandoahMaj. Gen. Nathaniel P. BanksOverall commander at Winchester; chose to make a delaying stand, then directed the retreat north toward Martinsburg and the Potomac
Infantry division under BanksBrig. Gen. Alpheus S. WilliamsControlled Gordon’s and Donnelly’s brigades; ordered the eastern brigade to withdraw after the western position collapsed
Western brigade, Bowers Hill sectorCol. George H. Gordon2nd Massachusetts, 3rd Wisconsin, 27th Indiana, and 29th Pennsylvania; faced Winder and Taylor west of the Valley Turnpike
Eastern brigade, Camp Hill sectorCol. Dudley Donnelly5th Connecticut, 28th New York, and 46th Pennsylvania, supported by the 10th Maine skirmish line and United States artillery; faced Ewell on the Front Royal Road
Cavalry under BanksBrig. Gen. John P. HatchApproximately 1,500 mounted troops in the episode’s accounting; attended the midnight council and accompanied the withdrawal but had limited value in the fixed infantry defense

Confederacy

Overall commander, Valley forceMaj. Gen. Thomas J. “Stonewall” JacksonDirected the converging approach on Winchester, ordered Winder onto the western ridge, committed Taylor against Bowers Hill, and sought a cavalry pursuit
Division commander, eastern approachMaj. Gen. Richard S. EwellAdvanced along the Front Royal Road, opened the fight against Camp Hill, and shifted forces toward Donnelly’s flank and withdrawal route
Brigade, Ewell’s divisionBrig. Gen. Isaac R. Trimble21st North Carolina, 21st Georgia, 15th Alabama, and 16th Mississippi; the two 21st regiments led the unsuccessful initial attack in the fog
1st Maryland Infantry, Confederate StatesCol. Bradley T. JohnsonMoved toward the far United States left during the lull, helping threaten Donnelly’s flank and line of retreat
Stonewall Brigade, Jackson’s divisionBrig. Gen. Charles S. WinderOccupied the first ridge west of the Valley Turnpike and held under fire from Gordon’s stronger Bowers Hill position until Taylor attacked
Louisiana Brigade, Ewell’s divisionBrig. Gen. Richard TaylorMoved from reserve against the Federal right and delivered the decisive attack on Bowers Hill about 7:30 a.m.
Chief of artilleryCol. Stapleton CrutchfieldDirected Jackson’s artillery on the western sector, including batteries supporting Winder and Taylor near the Valley Turnpike
Valley cavalryCol. Turner AshbyExpected to support the pursuit north of Winchester; disorganization and discipline failures left much of the cavalry ineffective at the decisive moment
