Episode 150 · May 24–25, 1862 · Washington, D.C., and northern Virginia; the battlefield south of Winchester from Bowers Hill across the Valley Turnpike to Camp Hill and the Front Royal Road, followed by the United States retreat through Winchester toward Martinsburg and the Potomac River

STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: FRONT ROYAL & WINCHESTER (Part the Fourth)

Decisive Confederate victory at the First Battle of Winchester. Jackson and Ewell attacked Banks’s exhausted command at daylight on May 25. The initial Confederate attack against Camp Hill failed, but Richard Taylor’s Louisiana Brigade turned the United States right on Bowers Hill and forced both Federal brigades to withdraw through Winchester. Banks preserved much of his mobile force by retreating north, while disorganized and misplaced Confederate cavalry failed to convert victory into destruction. Jackson cleared the northern Valley and disrupted the planned reinforcement of McClellan near Richmond; Lincoln responded by redirecting forces under McDowell and Frémont in an attempt to trap Jackson
Episode scopeWashington’s reaction on May 24 and the First Battle of Winchester on May 25, 1862The opening eyewitness readings describe the fighting and retreat before the episode begins its chronological account. The pursuit toward the Potomac closes this installment; the attempt to trap Jackson belongs to the next episode.
Battle resultDecisive Confederate victoryBanks abandoned Winchester and continued north toward the Potomac. The Confederate victory was operationally important but incomplete because the United States command escaped.
Strategic consequenceMcDowell’s movement toward Richmond was suspendedLincoln redirected part of McDowell’s force west and ordered Frémont east. These movements were intended to close behind Jackson, but they also prevented McDowell from joining McClellan before Richmond as planned.
Lincoln’s responseAn attempted envelopment, not a simple flight from dangerThe episode rejects the familiar description of Lincoln as panicked. His May 24 orders show an aggressive effort to use Frémont and McDowell as converging forces in Jackson’s rear, although concern for Harpers Ferry, the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, and Washington was real.
McDowell orderMove 20,000 men toward the Shenandoah ValleyThis halted the planned southward cooperation with McClellan. The episode presents the decision as Jackson’s greatest strategic effect during this phase of the Valley Campaign.
Frémont orderAdvance east from the Mountain Department toward the ValleyLincoln expected Frémont and McDowell to become the two sides of a trap behind Jackson. Terrain, distance, roads, timing, and divided command made coordination difficult.
“Commissary Banks”A nickname built around captured United States suppliesThe episode argues that Confederate enthusiasm reflected chronic supply shortages as much as the scale of Banks’s losses. It cites a United States quartermaster report of no more than 106 vehicles lost during the retreat from Strasburg to Williamsport and says at least 500 wagons escaped.
Confederate numbersUncertain; Jackson believed about 11,000 were within striking distanceHeavy straggling makes the number actually present on May 25 difficult to establish. The NPS narrative uses close to 16,000 for Jackson’s available force, while the episode’s lower figure concerns men believed to be within immediate reach. These are different measures and should not be merged.
United States numbersProbably about 4,400 infantry plus roughly 1,500 cavalry in the episode’s estimateBanks’s same-day report said fewer than 4,000 were engaged, while NPS accounts use approximately 5,000 or 6,500 depending on scope. The episode excludes Hatch’s cavalry from its estimate of troops useful in a stand-up infantry fight.
Federal purposeDelay the Confederates while trains and troops moved northBanks, Alpheus Williams, and John Hatch agreed at a midnight council to offer battle despite expecting defeat. Winchester lay directly behind the defensive line, making withdrawal through town hazardous.
TerrainA roughly 2.5-mile defensive front north of Abram’s CreekGordon’s brigade held the western sector near Bowers Hill and the Valley Turnpike. Donnelly’s brigade held the eastern sector near Camp Hill and the Front Royal Road.
Opening conditionsDense fog at daylight after little restJackson’s men began filing into position about 4:00 a.m. after roughly two hours of rest. The fog restricted visibility and delayed or disrupted observation on both sectors.
Ewell’s instructionAttack at daylightThe episode describes Jackson’s dispatch as a single sheet containing a sketch map of the positions and the written order. Ewell approached from the southeast along the Front Royal Road while Jackson advanced on the Valley Turnpike.
Camp Hill attackRepulsedThe 21st North Carolina and 21st Georgia advanced first through the fog. Donnelly’s infantry stopped the attack, including a second effort described in the episode as a bayonet charge. Ewell then shifted troops toward the exposed Federal flank and line of retreat.
Bowers HillThe decisive sector west of the Valley TurnpikeWinder occupied an initial ridge but encountered a stronger United States position beyond it. Gordon’s line and artillery held until Taylor’s Louisiana Brigade moved against the western flank.
Decisive attackTaylor’s Louisiana Brigade advanced about 7:30 a.m.Taylor moved around the United States right, then attacked Bowers Hill. The 29th Pennsylvania and 27th Indiana broke; the 2nd Massachusetts and 3rd Wisconsin then withdrew as Gordon’s position became untenable.
Federal withdrawalBoth brigades retired through WinchesterOnce the right collapsed, Williams ordered Donnelly to withdraw from the eastern sector. The episode says the battle was effectively over by about 9:30 a.m.
Civilians in WinchesterRejoicing is well documented; firing on retreating soldiers is reported but its extent is uncertainContemporary accounts describe civilians greeting Confederate troops. Numerous Federal reports also accused townspeople of firing from streets or buildings, but the guide does not treat every allegation or the number of participants as established.
Confederate cavalry failureBanks’s retreating force was not destroyedThe episode attributes the failure to command confusion among Ewell’s mounted troops and poor discipline and organization in Turner Ashby’s command. Jackson later regretted that his cavalry was not in position for the pursuit.
Retreat distanceAbout 22 miles from Winchester to Martinsburg, then onward to the PotomacBanks reported reaching Martinsburg at 2:40 p.m. on May 25 with his trains ahead. The episode says the force then covered roughly another dozen miles toward Williamsport.
Retreat timing discrepancyThe transcript’s “Tuesday at 2 a.m.” crossing is not supported by the cited official accountThe episode audio appears to place the Potomac crossing early Tuesday, May 27. The NPS narrative places it that evening, and Banks’s May 25 dispatch said the trains were already in advance toward the river. The guide flags the audio date rather than silently adopting it.
United States casualties71 killed, 243 wounded, and 1,714 missing for May 23–25Banks’s reports combined losses at Front Royal, the May 24 retreat, Winchester, and the subsequent withdrawal. The NPS battle database gives 2,019 United States casualties for Winchester I, while the detailed NPS narrative repeats the 2,028 three-day total. These figures use different scopes.
Confederate casualties68 killed, 329 wounded, and 3 missing for May 23–25Jackson’s reported total covers the three days rather than Winchester alone. The NPS battle database gives 400 Confederate casualties for Winchester I, nearly the same number but not explicitly the same accounting.
Tactical assessmentSuccessful flank attack after limited coordinationThe episode criticizes Jackson and Ewell for failing to combine multiple brigades simultaneously and for underestimating fatigue. The victory rested on maneuver, numerical advantage, and Taylor’s decisive attack more than on coordinated tactical control across the field.
Important leader lossesNo senior commander was killed or mortally woundedMaj. Wilder Dwight of the 2nd Massachusetts was captured during the retreat through Winchester but survived this battle. The opening account identifies Rockbridge Artillery private Robert T. McKim as killed; he was not a command-level casualty.

What happened

Robert Barton recalls the artillery advance

The opening reading follows Rockbridge Artillery private Robert T. Barton as the battery moved under shellfire south of Winchester. He describes clinging to a moving caisson and then finding Robert T. McKim dead beside the road.

Wilder Dwight sees the Federal right collapse

Maj. Wilder Dwight of the 2nd Massachusetts saw the 27th Indiana and 29th Pennsylvania break as Confederates came around the right. He withdrew on foot, was cut off in Winchester, and became a prisoner.

The episode turns to May 25

After the eyewitness readings, the hosts resume the Valley Campaign on the morning of the First Battle of Winchester.

The “Commissary Banks” story is qualified

The episode contrasts Confederate boasts about captured trains with the quartermaster’s report of no more than 106 vehicles lost during the two-day retreat, while at least 500 wagons escaped.

Washington assesses Jackson’s offensive

Lincoln and Stanton received Banks’s report from Winchester and began assembling reinforcements while evaluating the danger to Harpers Ferry, the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad, and the capital.

Lincoln returns from McDowell’s camp

Lincoln had just reviewed the proposed advance from Fredericksburg toward Richmond with Irvin McDowell and James Shields when the news from Front Royal changed the strategic problem.

Lincoln sees an opportunity to trap Jackson

The president reasoned that a deeper Confederate advance down the Valley could expose Jackson’s rear to converging United States columns.

McDowell is redirected

Lincoln suspended McDowell’s movement toward McClellan and ordered 20,000 men west toward the Shenandoah Valley.

Frémont is ordered east

John C. Frémont was to move from the Mountain Department toward the Valley, forming the western side of the attempted envelopment.

Lincoln informs McClellan

In the May 24 telegram quoted by the episode, Lincoln explained that Banks’s critical position had compelled him to suspend McDowell’s movement and send Frémont and part of McDowell’s force into Jackson’s rear.

The focus returns to Winchester

Jackson prepared to fight on Sunday, May 25, before Banks could complete the movement of his army and trains toward the Potomac.

Confederates deploy in fog

At about 4:00 a.m., after roughly two hours of rest, Jackson’s tired troops moved into position southwest of Winchester under heavy fog.

Ewell receives a sketch map and order

About a mile southeast of town, Ewell read Jackson’s dispatch showing the relative position near the Valley Turnpike and directing him to attack at daylight.

Confederate strength remains uncertain

Jackson believed close to 11,000 troops were within striking distance, but heavy overnight straggling prevents a reliable count of the men actually ready to fight.

Banks chooses to make a stand

Banks, Williams, and Hatch had agreed at midnight that the army should resist long enough to protect the northward movement, even though they expected the position to be defeated.

Two Federal brigades cover Winchester

Gordon’s four regiments held the western sector facing Jackson. Nearly two miles east, Donnelly’s three-regiment line covered the Front Royal Road against Ewell.

Ewell attacks Camp Hill

The 21st North Carolina and 21st Georgia advanced through the fog against Donnelly. Federal fire repulsed the first approach and a renewed bayonet attack.

Ewell shifts toward the flank

During a lull of nearly thirty minutes, Ewell moved the Confederate 1st Maryland and Trimble’s brigade toward the extreme Federal left, threatening Donnelly’s withdrawal route.

Winder occupies the first ridge

Jackson ordered Charles Winder to take the height in front. The Stonewall Brigade reached it with little opposition, then came under heavy fire from Gordon’s stronger position beyond.

Gordon’s line holds Bowers Hill

The episode places the 2nd Massachusetts in the center, the 29th Pennsylvania and 27th Indiana on the right, and the 3rd Wisconsin on the left of the western Federal sector.

Taylor is sent to carry the hill

After Winder requested support, Jackson directed Richard Taylor’s Louisiana Brigade against the position anchoring the Federal right.

Taylor restores order under artillery fire

Taylor’s sharp rebuke stopped his men from ducking and bunching under shells. Jackson then quietly reproved Taylor’s profanity before the brigade formed for its assault.

The Louisiana Brigade advances

At about 7:30 a.m., Taylor led the brigade in line toward the exposed western flank of the Bowers Hill position.

The United States right breaks

Taylor’s attack overwhelmed the 29th Pennsylvania and 27th Indiana. Their collapse forced the 2nd Massachusetts and 3rd Wisconsin to withdraw.

Williams orders a general withdrawal

With Gordon’s sector collapsing, Williams instructed Donnelly to retire. The two brigades moved through Winchester, and the episode places the effective end of the battle around 9:30 a.m.

The retreat passes through Winchester

Reports accused some residents of firing at Federal soldiers, while other civilians entered the streets to welcome Confederate troops. The exact scale of armed civilian participation remains uncertain.

Confederate cavalry fails to close the victory

Jackson wanted a mounted pursuit, but command confusion and poor organization left the cavalry unable to overtake and destroy Banks’s retreating column.

Banks retreats toward the Potomac

The defeated command marched about 22 miles to Martinsburg and continued toward the river crossing near Williamsport. Banks’s same-day dispatch reported arrival at Martinsburg at 2:40 p.m.

Casualty totals cover three days

The episode gives 2,028 United States casualties and 400 Confederate casualties for May 23–25, warning that the official returns combine Front Royal, the retreat, Winchester, and the continued withdrawal.

The episode separates tactics from maneuver

Jackson is criticized for piecemeal brigade employment and insufficient allowance for fatigue, while his ability to place troops for battle receives credit for making the victory possible.

The operational success is decisive

Jackson captured Winchester, drove Banks across the Potomac, and compelled Lincoln to divert troops that otherwise were intended to reinforce McClellan near Richmond.

The episode recommends Gallagher’s collection

The recommended book is “The Shenandoah Valley Campaign of 1862,” edited by Gary W. Gallagher, a collection of interpretive essays and a bibliographic essay.

The podcast marks episode 150

The hosts note that the first 149 regular episodes totaled 80 hours and 50 minutes and reflect on the program’s growth since its November 2012 launch.
