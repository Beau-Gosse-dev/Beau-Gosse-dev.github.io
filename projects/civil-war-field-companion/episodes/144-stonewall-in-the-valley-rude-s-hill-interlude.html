Episode 144 · March 27–May 1, 1862 · The Shenandoah Valley from Winchester and Stony Creek through Mount Jackson, Rude’s Hill, New Market, Harrisonburg, and Swift Run Gap, Virginia

STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: RUDE'S HILL INTERLUDE

No set-piece battle was fought at Rude’s Hill. Jackson used Banks’s cautious pursuit after First Kernstown to rest, reorganize, recruit, and improve the Valley Army’s map intelligence. Banks crossed Stony Creek on April 16–17 and advanced through Mount Jackson, prompting Jackson to abandon Rude’s Hill and New Market rather than risk being turned. Jackson reached Harrisonburg on April 18 and moved east to Conrad’s Store near Swift Run Gap on April 19. Banks occupied New Market, then Harrisonburg, but incorrectly concluded that Jackson had permanently left the Valley. On May 1, Washington ordered Shields’s division toward McDowell while Banks remained in the Valley with Williams’s division
Episode scopeThe political consequences of First Kernstown and the operational pause between Kernstown and Jackson’s move toward the McDowell campaignThe narrative covers federal command changes, Jackson’s Rude’s Hill encampment, the appointment of Jedediah Hotchkiss, personnel changes, Confederate conscription, Banks’s advance, and Jackson’s withdrawal toward Swift Run Gap.
Rude’s Hill positionHigh ground several miles south of Mount Jackson inside a bend of the North Fork of the Shenandoah RiverThe position guarded the Valley Turnpike approach from the north. Distances and unit locations in the companion diagrams are schematic.
Operational resultUnited States advance; Confederate withdrawal without battleBanks occupied Mount Jackson and New Market on April 17. Jackson yielded Rude’s Hill before the threatened frontal and flanking movements could close on his smaller army.
Kernstown consequenceBanks stayed in the Valley and McDowell’s corps remained in northern VirginiaLincoln’s April 9 letter explained that Banks’s diversion from the planned Manassas position left Washington inadequately covered and drove the decision to retain McDowell.
Federal reorganizationBanks received the independent Department of the Shenandoah; McDowell received the Department of the Rappahannock; Frémont commanded the Mountain DepartmentThe changes removed these forces from McClellan’s direct control while his Army of the Potomac operated on the Peninsula.
McDowell retainedAbout 37,000 men remained in northern VirginiaThe episode uses 37,000 for McDowell’s force. Contemporary totals vary with dates, definitions, and troops present or en route.
Jackson at Rude’s HillAbout two weeks of rest, drill, outpost duty, and reorganizationAshby’s cavalry screened the army from Thomas Brook and then Stony Creek while poor weather and federal caution slowed pursuit.
Hotchkiss joins JacksonSummoned to headquarters on March 27, four days after KernstownJackson directed the self-taught topographer to prepare a Valley map from Harpers Ferry to Lexington showing points of offense and defense. The familiar wording comes from later recollection; the assignment and resulting map are well documented by the Library of Congress.
Hotchkiss’s valueDetailed operational knowledge of roads, rivers, gaps, and terrainHotchkiss had taught and traveled extensively in the Valley before the war. His 1862 manuscript map became a major planning tool, but maps alone did not determine the campaign’s results.
Banks pausesOne week at Thomas Brook and roughly two weeks north of Stony CreekBanks overestimated Jackson’s strength after accepting Shields’s inflated post-Kernstown estimate. Burned bridges, flooded streams, cold rain and snow, supply trouble, and poor shoes also delayed movement.
Garnett removedRelieved from the Stonewall Brigade and arrested on April 1 for alleged neglect of duty at KernstownJackson blamed Garnett’s unauthorized retreat for losing a chance to hold the field. The episode emphasizes that the brigade’s officers and men largely believed Garnett had acted correctly. His court-martial did not reach a verdict before the war intervened.
Stonewall Brigade reactionWidespread anger and resentment toward JacksonThe episode cites Maj. Frank Jones and Henry Kyd Douglas for the brigade’s hostile reaction. Their testimony describes morale and opinion, not a formal vote of every member.
New brigade commanderBrig. Gen. Charles S. Winder replaced GarnettWinder was a Maryland-born West Pointer and strict disciplinarian. The brigade initially resented both his appointment and Garnett’s removal.
Other command changesWilliam B. Taliaferro took the Third Brigade; Col. John A. Campbell temporarily led the Second BrigadeThe automated transcript renders Taliaferro as “Tolliver” and Fulkerson as “Folkerson.” Taliaferro replaced Samuel V. Fulkerson; Campbell acted while Jesse S. Burks was on extended sick leave.
Confederate congressional thanksCongress formally thanked Jackson and his command for KernstownThe resolution passed despite the tactical defeat, describing the action as gallant and meritorious service against a greatly superior force.
First Confederate Conscription ActApproved April 16, 1862It subjected white male citizens ages 18–35 to three years of service, extended many existing one-year enlistments, and initially permitted substitution. The law was the first national conscription statute in United States history.
Rockingham resistanceAbout 200 militia members deserted near Swift Run GapThe episode says Jackson sent infantry, cavalry, and artillery against the holdouts and that nearly all were rounded up. Motives varied; some resistance was pro-Union, while pacifist Mennonites, Dunkers, and Quakers also resisted military service on religious grounds.
Valley Army strengthJust over 6,000 by late AprilThe episode attributes the increase to conscription, enlistment before the law took effect, and the return of veterans from furlough.
Blenker’s ordealLouis Blenker’s division was delayed near Paris, Virginia, and then sent directly to FrémontThe episode reports that forty men drowned during an attempted river crossing and describes severe shortages of food, shoes, tents, ammunition, and wagons. That drowning figure is retained as the episode’s report and has not been independently reconciled here.
April 16 cavalry actionUnited States troops captured more than sixty of Ashby’s cavalrymenBanks reported the capture near Columbia Furnace. His April 16 dispatch called it an entire company of more than sixty men and horses.
April 17 advanceKimball advanced on the Valley Turnpike while Carroll used the Back RoadBanks reported Mount Jackson occupied at 7 a.m. Federal cavalry saved the North Fork bridge, locomotives, and rail cars that Confederate troops had prepared to burn.
Tom TelegraphTurner Ashby’s white war horse was mortally wounded on April 17Henry Kyd Douglas later described the animal collapsing after carrying Ashby from the North Fork bridge to the protection of Confederate artillery. This is an animal loss and is not included in the people-losses field.
Jackson’s withdrawalRude’s Hill and New Market abandoned April 17; Harrisonburg reached April 18; Conrad’s Store reached April 19Jackson moved south along the main Valley corridor, then about twenty miles east toward Swift Run Gap, preserving options to unite with Richard Ewell or move toward Staunton.
Banks loses contactIncorrectly reported that Jackson had permanently left the ValleyBanks occupied New Market and moved cautiously to Harrisonburg. He asked to transfer both divisions east, but the May 1 order detached Shields while Banks and Williams remained in the Valley.
