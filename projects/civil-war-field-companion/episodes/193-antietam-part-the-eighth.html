Episode 193 · September 17, 1862 · The northern Antietam battlefield near Sharpsburg, Maryland, from the Joseph Poffenberger farm south through the East Woods and Miller’s Cornfield to the Mumma farm and Dunker Church plateau

ANTIETAM (Part the Eighth)

Between about 7:30 and 9 a.m., the United States XII Corps relieved Hooker’s exhausted I Corps and drove D. H. Hill’s reinforcements from the East Woods, Cornfield, Mumma farm, and ground east of the Hagerstown Turnpike. Maj. Gen. Joseph K. F. Mansfield was mortally wounded as his inexperienced corps deployed. Brig. Gen. Alpheus S. Williams then directed the corps while Brig. Gen. George S. Greene exploited the collapse of Garland’s brigade, turned Colquitt’s flank, and reached the Dunker Church plateau. The advance put the Confederate left in grave danger, but the wider breakthrough continues in the next episode
Episode scopeThe XII Corps attack, about 7:30–9 a.m.The episode begins as Hooker’s and Jackson’s formations are spent, follows Mansfield’s corps through the East Woods and Cornfield, and ends with Greene near the Dunker Church.
Opening balanceHooker about 2,600 casualties; Jackson about 3,000These are the episode’s estimates for the first ninety minutes of fighting. Both commands were badly damaged, with neither side holding a decisive advantage at about 7:30 a.m.
McClellan’s next reinforcementTwo divisions of Sumner’s II CorpsMcClellan ordered Sumner forward at about 7:20 a.m. but kept one division east of Antietam Creek until Morell’s V Corps division could replace it in reserve.
Uncommitted Federal wingBurnside’s IX Corps still had no order to attackThe quiet on the Confederate right allowed Lee to send troops north toward the crisis on his left.
XII Corps strengthAbout 7,200 men in the episode’s estimateRoughly half were entering battle for the first time, including five recently mustered regiments.
Mansfield’s tenureTwo days in command of XII CorpsThe 58-year-old career engineer had taken command on September 15 and was leading his first important Civil War field command.
Approach to battleColumns of companies with repeated haltsThe corps marched from its bivouac near the Poffenberger farms. Officers struggled to move and deploy the many new regiments while the men repeatedly tried to make breakfast.
Mansfield’s mortal woundShot in the chest in the East WoodsMansfield initially believed the 10th Maine was firing into retreating Federal troops. After recognizing the Confederates in front, he was hit and carried to the rear; he died on September 18.
128th Pennsylvania118 casualties in the episode’s accountThe new regiment lost Col. Samuel L. Croasdale and had Lt. Col. William W. Hammersly wounded while attempting to deploy. It then advanced into the Cornfield before breaking under Confederate fire.
Change of commandAlpheus S. Williams took over XII CorpsWilliams received Hooker’s instructions under fire and placed Crawford on the right, Gordon in the center, and Greene on the left.
D. H. Hill’s reinforcementAbout 3,400 men in three brigadesRipley was already south of the Cornfield while Colquitt and McRae advanced north from the Confederate center.
Gordon’s stand2nd Massachusetts, 3rd Wisconsin, and 27th IndianaThese regiments held the higher Miller pasture north of the Cornfield and fought Colquitt’s brigade advancing through the corn.
Lost Order casualtiesMitchell and Bloss wounded; Capt. Peter Kop mortally woundedThe three men of the 27th Indiana had handled the lost copy of Lee’s Special Orders No. 191 after its discovery near Frederick.
Replacement systemsConfederate replacements joined veteran regimentsThe episode contrasts this practice with the Federal tendency to organize new regiments. Experienced comrades could train Confederate conscripts, but McRae’s brigade showed that the system did not eliminate battlefield panic.
Garland’s brigade breaksA perceived flank threat triggered a stampedeStill shaken by its rout and the death of Brig. Gen. Samuel Garland at South Mountain, McRae’s brigade broke in the East Woods when the large 28th Pennsylvania approached its flank.
Greene’s attacking forceAbout 1,700 men in two brigadesGreene’s official report gives 1,727 men remaining under his immediate command after his third brigade was detached to the right.
Tyndale’s pivotEast Woods to a west-facing lineLt. Col. Hector Tyndale wheeled the 28th Pennsylvania and three Ohio regiments against Colquitt’s exposed flank at the north edge of the Cornfield.
Close-range volleyAbout 30 yards in the episode’s accountTyndale’s line struck Colquitt while the Confederates were focused on Gordon’s troops in the Miller pasture.
Colquitt’s lossAbout half the brigadeColquitt’s official report states that he lost one-half of the men in the ranks during the unequal fight.
Stainrook’s turning movementA wider sweep to Smoketown Road and the Mumma farmGreene sent Col. Henry J. Stainrook’s brigade south and west, forcing the remaining Confederates from their foothold in the East Woods.
Captured Confederate gunsA two-gun section sent forward by Stephen D. LeeThe episode says men of the 28th Pennsylvania captured the section after the Confederate withdrawal began.
Dunker Church plateauStephen D. Lee abandoned the morning artillery positionGreene’s advance through the East Woods and Mumma farm threatened the plateau, forcing Lee’s batteries to pull back beyond the West Woods.
Federal high-water mark in scope125th Pennsylvania entered the West Woods near the churchBy close to 9 a.m., XII Corps held the Cornfield, East Woods, Mumma farm, and the ground east of the turnpike.
Northern-field tollAbout 8,000 casualties by 9 a.m. in the episode’s estimateThe episode treats the losses as roughly evenly divided between United States and Confederate forces after three hours of combat.
Immediate opportunityThe Confederate left appeared close to collapseHooker believed he might capture Lee’s army or drive it into the Potomac, but the XII Corps advance was unsupported and Sumner’s attack is reserved for the next episode.
Recommended book“Landscape Turned Red” by Stephen W. SearsThe hosts recommend Sears’s narrative history of the Maryland Campaign and Battle of Antietam.

Important leaders killed or wounded

United States

Maj. Gen. Joseph K. F. Mansfield

Commander of XII Corps. Mortally wounded by a rifle ball in the chest while directing troops in the East Woods; carried to a field hospital and died on September 18 after only two days in corps command.

United States

Col. Samuel L. Croasdale

Commander of the 128th Pennsylvania, Crawford’s brigade. Killed as his inexperienced regiment attempted to deploy from column into line near the East Woods and Miller pasture.

United States

Lt. Col. William W. Hammersly

Second-in-command of the 128th Pennsylvania. Wounded moments after Croasdale was killed, deepening the command confusion before the regiment entered the Cornfield.

United States

Capt. Peter Kop

Company F, 27th Indiana, Gordon’s brigade. Mortally wounded by a stray bullet later on September 17 after surviving the morning’s close-range fighting; he died at a field hospital on September 24. Earlier in the campaign, Barton Mitchell and John Bloss had brought Lee’s lost order to him.
