Episode 193 · September 17, 1862 · The northern Antietam battlefield near Sharpsburg, Maryland, from the Joseph Poffenberger farm south through the East Woods and Miller’s Cornfield to the Mumma farm and Dunker Church plateau

ANTIETAM (Part the Eighth)

Between about 7:30 and 9 a.m., the United States XII Corps relieved Hooker’s exhausted I Corps and drove D. H. Hill’s reinforcements from the East Woods, Cornfield, Mumma farm, and ground east of the Hagerstown Turnpike. Maj. Gen. Joseph K. F. Mansfield was mortally wounded as his inexperienced corps deployed. Brig. Gen. Alpheus S. Williams then directed the corps while Brig. Gen. George S. Greene exploited the collapse of Garland’s brigade, turned Colquitt’s flank, and reached the Dunker Church plateau. The advance put the Confederate left in grave danger, but the wider breakthrough continues in the next episode

What happened

The shattered northern battlefield is recapped

At about 7:30 a.m., Hooker and Jackson have lost roughly 5,600 men between them, yet the lines remain close to their dawn positions.

McClellan finally orders Sumner forward

Hood’s counterattack prompts an order for II Corps to reinforce the northern battle, though one division must remain behind until relieved.

Burnside remains idle

No order has yet sent IX Corps against the Confederate right, allowing Lee to shift troops toward his endangered left.

D. H. Hill feeds in Ripley’s brigade

Ripley reaches the pasture south of the Cornfield too late to join Hood’s charge but in time to meet the Federal pursuit.

XII Corps approaches from the north

Mansfield’s mission is now to support Hooker’s wrecked formations and prevent a Confederate breakthrough.

The march repeatedly stalls

Stephen Sears’s account describes columns halting, men lighting breakfast fires, and orders arriving before the coffee can boil.

Wilder Dwight writes from the saddle

The lieutenant colonel of the 2nd Massachusetts tells his mother that Hooker is heavily engaged and that he is well so far.

Hooker asks for help everywhere

Mansfield learns that I Corps needs support in the West Woods, East Woods, and Cornfield, then rides back to hurry his corps.

The corps’ inexperience is measured

About half of XII Corps’ 7,200 men have never fought, and five new regiments have been in service for less than a month.

Mansfield’s first field command is introduced

After forty years in the Regular Army, the 58-year-old engineer has led XII Corps for only two days.

Green regiments need personal guidance

Williams uses a fence to align one regiment and explains how simple maneuvers could throw raw officers and men into confusion.

Mansfield challenges the 10th Maine’s fire

Believing friendly troops remain in the East Woods, Mansfield orders the veterans to stop until they point out the Confederates.

Mansfield is mortally wounded

A bullet strikes him in the chest; he is carried through the fire to an ambulance and dies the following day.

The 128th Pennsylvania tries to deploy

The new regiment loses its colonel and second-in-command during an unfamiliar movement from column into line.

The 128th attacks through the corn

Its remaining commander chooses movement over standing under fire, but the regiment breaks after emerging from the Cornfield with 118 casualties.

Williams takes command

After Mansfield falls, Williams confers with Hooker, Gibbon, and Meade under fire and assumes direction of XII Corps.

Hill rebuilds the Confederate line

About 3,400 men in the brigades of Ripley, Colquitt, and McRae form from the turnpike toward the East Woods.

Colquitt meets Gordon in the Cornfield

Three Federal regiments stand on the elevated Miller pasture while Colquitt’s Georgians and Alabamians advance through the corn.

The Lost Order trio’s Antietam fates are recalled

The episode notes that Barton Mitchell and John Bloss were wounded and that their company commander Peter Kop was also a battle casualty; later records identify Kop’s wound as mortal.

The replacement systems are compared

The episode explains how Confederate conscripts joined veteran regiments while Federal states more often raised entirely new units.

McRae’s brigade panics

Men fearing another flank attack break in the East Woods and carry most of Garland’s brigade toward Sharpsburg.

Robert Gould Shaw crosses the wrecked field

Shaw describes Gordon’s brigade moving among Confederate dead and wounded and Federal soldiers offering water and aid.

Greene seizes the opening

The veteran division commander sends Tyndale through the East Woods and pivots the brigade west toward Colquitt’s flank.

Tyndale delivers a close volley

The 28th Pennsylvania and three Ohio regiments fire into Colquitt’s line at roughly 30 yards, then charge into the Cornfield.

Stainrook makes the wider turn

Greene’s second brigade reaches Smoketown Road and the Mumma farm, forcing Hood’s remnants from the East Woods.

The 28th Pennsylvania captures guns

A two-gun Confederate section sent forward by Stephen D. Lee cannot withdraw before Federal soldiers reach it.

Stephen D. Lee abandons the plateau

Greene’s advance forces the Confederate artillery battalion away from its commanding position east of the Dunker Church.

The 125th Pennsylvania reaches the church

The new regiment crosses the Hagerstown Turnpike and enters the West Woods near and north of the Dunker Church.

XII Corps turns the battle

By close to 9 a.m., Mansfield’s corps has rescued I Corps and set the conditions for a Federal breakthrough on the northern field.

The three-hour cost reaches 8,000

The episode estimates that casualties are about equally divided across the devastated Cornfield, woods, pasture, and turnpike.

Hooker believes victory is within reach

With Greene near the church and the 125th Pennsylvania in the West Woods, Hooker thinks the Confederate army might be captured or driven into the Potomac.

The book recommendation closes the episode

The hosts recommend Stephen W. Sears’s “Landscape Turned Red: The Battle of Antietam.”

Important leaders killed or wounded

United States

Maj. Gen. Joseph K. F. Mansfield

Commander of XII Corps. Mortally wounded by a rifle ball in the chest while directing troops in the East Woods; carried to a field hospital and died on September 18 after only two days in corps command.

United States

Col. Samuel L. Croasdale

Commander of the 128th Pennsylvania, Crawford’s brigade. Killed as his inexperienced regiment attempted to deploy from column into line near the East Woods and Miller pasture.

United States

Lt. Col. William W. Hammersly

Second-in-command of the 128th Pennsylvania. Wounded moments after Croasdale was killed, deepening the command confusion before the regiment entered the Cornfield.

United States

Capt. Peter Kop

Company F, 27th Indiana, Gordon’s brigade. Mortally wounded by a stray bullet later on September 17 after surviving the morning’s close-range fighting; he died at a field hospital on September 24. Earlier in the campaign, Barton Mitchell and John Bloss had brought Lee’s lost order to him.
