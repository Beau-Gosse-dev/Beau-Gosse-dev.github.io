What happened

The New Mexico campaign extended the war far west

The 1861–62 Confederate invasion of New Mexico was the westernmost major campaign of the Civil War and included two major battles and several smaller engagements.

Davis authorized Sibley to organize a brigade

Henry Hopkins Sibley resigned his U.S. Army commission, presented a plan to Jefferson Davis, and received authority on July 5, 1861, to raise mounted volunteers in Texas and drive U.S. forces from New Mexico.

Sibley planned a wider western advance

According to his artillery chief, Sibley intended to take New Mexico, move into Colorado’s mining districts, and eventually reach ports in Southern California.

Kit Carson entered U.S. service

Carson became lieutenant colonel of the 1st New Mexico Volunteer Infantry. The episode uses him to introduce local opposition to the Confederate invasion.

New Mexico Territory covered a much larger area

In 1860 the territory included present-day New Mexico and Arizona and the southern tip of Nevada, with about 100,000 residents.

Roads and the Rio Grande controlled movement

The Santa Fe Trail entered from the northeast, the Overland Mail route crossed southern New Mexico, and the Camino Real followed the Rio Grande. The territory had no railroad or telegraph.

Texas secessionists bypassed Sam Houston

Delegates elected without effective Unionist opposition met at Austin and adopted a secession ordinance on February 1, 1861, by 166 votes to 8.

A committee began seizing U.S. property

Before the public referendum, the convention created a Committee of Public Safety and authorized it to take federal posts, supplies, and other property in Texas.

Texas demanded the surrender of the Department of Texas

The committee sent commissioners to David Twiggs while Ben McCulloch brought about 1,000 armed men into San Antonio to support the demand.

Twiggs surrendered U.S. posts and supplies

On February 18, Twiggs agreed to turn over federal buildings, military property, and 19 forts and posts. Roughly 2,500 U.S. soldiers were ordered to leave Texas by the Gulf Coast.

Texas voters approved secession

The February 23 referendum passed 46,100 to 14,600. Sam Houston refused to swear allegiance to the Confederacy and was removed as governor in March.

Texas formed mounted regiments for its frontier

John R. Baylor became lieutenant colonel of the 2nd Texas Mounted Rifles and was sent west to secure abandoned U.S. posts, including Fort Bliss.

Van Dorn ordered Baylor toward Fort Fillmore

Earl Van Dorn sent Baylor from San Antonio to West Texas and authorized an attack on Fort Fillmore, about 40 miles north of Fort Bliss.

The march reduced Baylor’s force

About 1,000 men left San Antonio, but desertion, illness, heat, fatigue, and detachments at posts left roughly 400 at El Paso in early July.

Baylor invaded New Mexico with fewer than 300 men

He moved north on July 23. After a deserter warned Fort Fillmore, Baylor crossed the Rio Grande and entered Mesilla.

Lynde failed to remove Baylor from Mesilla

Major Isaac Lynde brought most of his larger garrison across the river, made an unsuccessful attack on July 25, and returned to Fort Fillmore.

The U.S. garrison evacuated Fort Fillmore

Lynde left at 1 a.m. on July 27 and took the desert road toward Fort Stanton. Heat, lack of water, and whiskey taken from the post weakened and scattered the column.

Lynde surrendered near San Augustine Springs

Only about 100 soldiers formed when Baylor caught the column. Lynde surrendered more than 500 men to fewer than 300 Texans without a final exchange of fire.

Baylor proclaimed Confederate Arizona

Unable to continue north with his small force, Baylor remained at Mesilla. On August 1 he declared the area south of the 34th parallel the Confederate Territory of Arizona and named himself governor.
