Episode 182 · September 4–13, 1862 · The Potomac crossings and the Maryland campaign corridor from Washington and Frederick west across South Mountain to Hagerstown, Martinsburg, and Harpers Ferry

LEE'S SPECIAL ORDERS No. 191

Operational turning point. Special Orders No. 191 divided the Army of Northern Virginia to clear the United States garrisons at Martinsburg and Harpers Ferry, but the detachments fell behind Lee’s timetable while the Army of the Potomac advanced faster than he expected. By noon on September 13, a lost copy of the order had reached George B. McClellan at Frederick
Episode scopeLee’s September 4–13 Maryland movements, McClellan’s pursuit, and the plan contained in Special Orders No. 191The episode ends when McClellan receives the lost copy. The consequences of that discovery are reserved for the next episode.
Confederate crossingThe Army of Northern Virginia began crossing the Potomac on September 4The movement into Maryland continued through the weekend; the army was assembled near Frederick by September 7.
Confederate strengthUncertain; historians’ estimates cited in the episode range from 50,000 to 75,000The hosts use about 65,000 as a plausible starting figure after applying Lee’s later statement that straggling reduced his Antietam strength by roughly one-third. This is an estimate, not a contemporary return.
Condition of Lee’s armyVeteran and confident, but exhausted and poorly suppliedThe episode emphasizes inadequate food, clothing, and shoes. Straggling and desertion sharply reduced the force that reached Antietam.
Lee’s command structureJackson and Longstreet led the two principal infantry wingsD. H. Hill, Lafayette McLaws, and Richard H. Anderson had just arrived from Richmond and initially reported directly to Lee; J. E. B. Stuart’s cavalry also reported to army headquarters.
Command frictionA. P. Hill and John Bell Hood entered Maryland under arrestJackson had relieved Hill during a dispute over march discipline. Hood’s arrest grew from a dispute with Nathan “Shanks” Evans over captured ambulances. Both remained near their commands.
Lee’s injuryA broken bone in his right hand and a severe sprain in his leftLee was injured while trying to control his horse, Traveller, and entered Maryland in an ambulance because he could not hold the reins.
McClellan restoredPlaced over the forces around Washington on September 2Units from John Pope’s Army of Virginia were merged into the Army of the Potomac. The episode stresses the rapid improvement in morale after McClellan’s return.
Lee’s central assumptionMcClellan would need three or four weeks before undertaking an offensiveLee judged both the commander and the reorganized Federal army too cautious and disordered to react quickly. The Army of the Potomac began moving from Washington on September 5.
Federal warningA Sugarloaf Mountain signal station confirmed the crossing on September 4The station, about ten miles south of Frederick, helped confirm the direction of Lee’s movement.
Army of the Potomac advanceThree wings spread northwest from Washington like a tridentBurnside’s right guarded against a move on Baltimore, Sumner’s center approached Frederick, and Franklin’s left kept nearer the Potomac. Pleasonton’s cavalry screened the advance.
Lee’s campaign objectiveMove west from Frederick and draw the United States army away from WashingtonCrossing the Catoctin and South Mountain ranges would place Lee in the Cumberland Valley, threaten Pennsylvania, and reopen communications through the Shenandoah Valley.
Obstacle in the lower valleyUnited States garrisons remained at Martinsburg and Harpers FerryThe episode gives about 3,000 men under Julius White at Martinsburg and 10,000 under Dixon S. Miles at Harpers Ferry. Lee had expected both posts to be evacuated.
Order issuedHeadquarters, Army of Northern Virginia, September 9, 1862After consulting Jackson and Longstreet, Lee dictated the plan to assistant adjutant general Robert H. Chilton, who prepared and distributed copies as Special Orders No. 191.
Jackson’s assignmentCapture Martinsburg, then move toward Harpers FerryJackson’s three divisions were to march through Middletown and Sharpsburg, cross the Potomac, and clear the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad approaches.
Longstreet’s assignmentHalt at Boonsboro with the reserve supply and baggage trainsLee later sent Longstreet north to Hagerstown after reports of flour there and of Pennsylvania militia approaching from Chambersburg.
McLaws’s assignmentSeize Maryland Heights and help capture Harpers FerryLafayette McLaws commanded his own division and Richard H. Anderson’s, roughly 15,000 men in the episode’s account.
Walker’s assignmentRecross the Potomac, occupy Loudoun Heights, and cooperate against Harpers FerryJohn G. Walker’s division approached the garrison from south of the Potomac while McLaws moved against it from the north and east.
D. H. Hill and StuartHill guarded the rear; Stuart screened the army and watched the Federal advanceStuart was also to provide cavalry detachments to the principal marching columns and bring up stragglers.
Planned reunionBoonsboro or Hagerstown after the valley garrisons surrenderedLee expected the operation to take only a few days. Jackson, McLaws, and Walker were then to rejoin the main body.
Plan already changingJackson went by Williamsport and Longstreet continued to HagerstownJackson marched from Frederick on September 10, halted after crossing the mountains, and turned northwest the next day. These movements spread the army more widely than the written plan indicated.
Martinsburg clearedJulius White’s command escaped to Harpers Ferry on September 12The evacuation removed the Martinsburg post but strengthened Dixon Miles’s Harpers Ferry garrison by about 3,000 men.
Harpers Ferry surroundedConfederate columns occupied the surrounding heights by late September 13Jackson reached Bolivar Heights from the west, McLaws secured Maryland Heights after fighting, and Walker occupied Loudoun Heights. The garrison had not surrendered.
Federal paceThe Army of the Potomac reached Frederick on September 12–13Lee had written on September 8 that the enemy appeared to be concentrating around Washington, but Federal cavalry was already skirmishing and the army was moving rapidly northwest.
The lost copyDelivered to McClellan at Frederick around noon on September 13The National Park Service identifies the finders as members of Company F, 27th Indiana. The copy was intended for D. H. Hill; how it was lost remains uncertain.
Episode recommendationWinter 2016 issue of The Civil War MonitorThe recommended feature is Stephen W. Sears’s examination of the “Lost Order.”

What happened

Lee’s army begins crossing the Potomac

The episode opens its narrative on September 4 with the Army of Northern Virginia entering western Maryland.

The uncertainty over Confederate strength is explained

The hosts distinguish estimates ranging from 50,000 to 75,000 from the incomplete Confederate returns.

Exhaustion becomes the army’s limiting factor

Veteran soldiers enter the campaign with high morale but without the food, clothing, shoes, and endurance required for sustained marching.

Lee’s field organization is outlined

Jackson and Longstreet lead the principal wings while newly arrived divisions and Stuart’s cavalry remain directly under Lee.

A. P. Hill and Hood are under arrest

Two important Confederate division commanders march near their commands while personal and disciplinary disputes remain unresolved.

Lee’s injured hands force him into an ambulance

A riding accident leaves Lee unable to hold Traveller’s reins as the army enters Maryland.

McClellan returns to command

Lee learns that Lincoln has placed McClellan over the Federal forces regrouping around Washington.

Lee counts on a three-to-four-week window

He expects McClellan and the reorganized United States army to remain near Washington rather than begin an immediate pursuit.

Sugarloaf Mountain confirms the Confederate crossing

A United States signal station reports on September 4 that Confederate troops are crossing the Potomac.

The Army of the Potomac moves in three wings

Burnside takes the right, Sumner the center, and Franklin the left while Pleasonton’s cavalry screens the advance.

Lee’s army concentrates near Frederick

By September 7 the Confederate army is across the Potomac and positioned to threaten Washington and Baltimore before turning west.

The lower-valley garrisons block Lee’s communications

United States troops remain at Martinsburg and Harpers Ferry instead of evacuating as Lee had expected.

Lee explains the valley operation

On September 9 he meets with Jackson and Longstreet and describes how the army will remove the two Federal garrisons.

Jackson receives the Martinsburg assignment

His three divisions are to cross the mountains, recross the Potomac, capture Martinsburg, and then move toward Harpers Ferry.

McLaws is directed toward Maryland Heights

McLaws, with Richard Anderson’s division added, is to turn south at Middletown and dominate Harpers Ferry from the north.

Walker is sent to Loudoun Heights

Walker’s division must recross the Potomac and cooperate with the other columns against the garrison.

Hill and Stuart receive covering assignments

D. H. Hill forms the rear guard while Stuart screens the routes, gathers stragglers, and watches McClellan.

Jackson and Longstreet question the separation

Both lieutenants object to aspects of the plan; Longstreet initially persuades Lee to stop the main body at Boonsboro rather than Hagerstown.

The plan becomes Special Orders No. 191

Lee dictates the operation to his staff, and copies are prepared for the commanders named in it.

The timetable rests on faulty intelligence

Lee believes time is on his side, but Stuart’s reports have not revealed how rapidly the Federal army is moving.

Jackson starts west from Frederick

The September 10 march crosses the Catoctin and South Mountain ranges, but Jackson halts outside Boonsboro after thirteen miles.

Jackson turns toward Williamsport

Instead of continuing toward Sharpsburg, his column turns northwest and fords the Potomac to approach Martinsburg from the north and west.

Longstreet continues to Hagerstown

Lee changes the Boonsboro halt after receiving reports about flour and a possible Pennsylvania militia threat.

The Martinsburg garrison escapes

White’s men join the Harpers Ferry garrison, forcing Jackson to continue south rather than reunite with Lee.

The Harpers Ferry columns fall behind schedule

McLaws and Walker do not secure Maryland and Loudoun Heights until September 13, while Jackson approaches Bolivar Heights from the west.

The United States army reaches Frederick

Federal advance elements enter on September 12; the main army arrives the next morning and pushes west toward South Mountain.

McClellan receives the lost order

At about noon on September 13, the Confederate operational order is unexpectedly handed to the Army of the Potomac commander.

The episode recommends Stephen Sears on the Lost Order

The hosts recommend the Winter 2016 issue of The Civil War Monitor for a closer investigation of Special Orders No. 191.
