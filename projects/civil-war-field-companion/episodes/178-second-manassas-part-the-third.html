Episode 178 · August 30, 1862 · The Second Manassas battlefield around Groveton, the unfinished railroad, Brawner Farm, Dogan Ridge, Chinn Ridge, and the Warrenton Turnpike in Prince William County, Virginia

SECOND MANASSAS (Part the Third)

Confederate victory. Porter’s and Hatch’s afternoon assault briefly pressed Jackson’s line at the unfinished railroad but was repulsed. Longstreet then struck the exposed Federal left, destroyed Warren’s small brigade, and began the counterattack that drove Pope’s army from the field; this episode ends before the fighting on Chinn Ridge and Henry Hill is completed
Episode scopeMcClellan’s response to Pope’s crisis and the first major actions of August 30The episode follows events through the repulse of Porter and Hatch and the opening of Longstreet’s counterattack. The battle’s final phase is reserved for the next episode.
McClellan and FranklinFranklin’s VI Corps did not reach Pope before the battleThe episode describes Halleck’s repeated efforts to move Franklin from Alexandria. Franklin marched about 10 miles on August 29 and about 12 miles on August 30, reaching the vicinity of Centreville only after the battle was already in progress.
McClellan’s August 29 telegramOne option was to “leave Pope to get out of his scrape”The Library of Congress preserves McClellan’s telegram to Lincoln. McClellan offered either to concentrate available forces to open communication with Pope or to leave Pope and secure Washington, and asked for a prompt decision.
Sumner’s II CorpsHeld near Washington until the afternoon of August 30Sumner’s corps was the last Army of the Potomac formation to reach Alexandria from the Peninsula. It started toward Centreville on August 30 and arrived on August 31.
Last Confederate arrivalsRichard H. Anderson’s division reached the field at about 3 a.m.The episode gives Anderson 6,100 infantry and Stephen D. Lee 18 guns after a 17-hour march. Anderson’s confused movement in the darkness helped reinforce Pope’s mistaken belief that Lee was withdrawing.
Pope’s central errorHe interpreted Confederate activity as a retreatFederal probes met resistance on the morning of August 30, but Pope continued to treat the opposition as a rear guard and did not adequately account for Longstreet’s large force south of the turnpike.
Noon pursuitPorter and Ricketts were ordered forward and stopped by artilleryAfter the short-lived movement showed that the Confederates had not left, Pope ordered a larger assault against Jackson rather than withdrawing behind Bull Run.
Federal assault forceAbout 10,000 men under Porter and HatchPorter’s troops formed on the left, Hatch’s division on the right, and Sykes’s regulars in reserve. The episode places the main advance at 3 p.m.
Assault groundOpen fields, Schoolhouse Branch, and a steep rise to the railroadThe National Park Service describes 300 to 600 yards of exposed ground in front of Starke’s line, with the final 150 yards rising sharply. Butterfield’s men on the Federal left had the longest approach.
Confederate defendersWilliam E. Starke’s division behind the unfinished railroadBradley T. Johnson held the Deep Cut, Leroy A. Stafford held the sector known as the Dump, and the Stonewall Brigade supported Johnson’s right.
Confederate artillery advantageStephen D. Lee’s 18 guns enfiladed the Federal assaultLee’s battalion occupied high ground near Brawner Farm. Jackson’s guns joined the fire, and Longstreet added batteries rather than sending infantry directly to Jackson.
Federal footholdHatch’s men reached the embankment near the DumpTwo New York regiments from Timothy Sullivan’s brigade fought Stafford’s Louisianians at close range while parts of Butterfield’s command pressed Johnson at the Deep Cut.
Stonewall Brigade counterattackFewer than 500 men helped close a gap in Jackson’s lineThe brigade initially recoiled after its commander, Col. William S. H. Baylor, was killed. It rallied and helped restore the Confederate position.
Rock throwingSoldiers on both sides threw stones across the railroad gradeSome Confederate units exhausted their ammunition during the close fight. Federal soldiers returned stones thrown from behind the embankment.
Why the assault failedArtillery crossfire, Confederate reinforcements, and no timely Federal reserveLawton and A. P. Hill shifted troops toward Starke. Porter did not commit Sykes’s division to renew the attack, and the exposed Federal formations withdrew under fire.
Reynolds moved northMcDowell weakened the Federal left to support Porter’s withdrawalReynolds’s Pennsylvania Reserves left Chinn Ridge and crossed the turnpike. The move was unnecessary north of the road and left only McLean’s and Warren’s small brigades in Longstreet’s path.
Exposed Federal flankAbout 2,200 Federals faced Longstreet’s five divisionsThe episode earlier estimates 8,000 Federals south of the turnpike against 25,000 Confederates. After Reynolds moved, the immediate Federal covering force was much smaller.
Longstreet’s objectiveHenry Hill and the Federal retreat routes across Bull RunLongstreet’s front extended about a mile and a half from Brawner Farm to beyond the Manassas Gap Railroad. His divisions had between roughly one and a half and two miles to cover.
Counterattack formationHood led; Kemper advanced on his right; Anderson supportedDavid R. Jones’s division had the longest and most obstructed route on the Confederate right. Wilcox maintained the connection between Longstreet and Jackson.
Opening of Longstreet’s attackHood advanced shortly after 4 p.m.The Texas Brigade spearheaded the attack astride the Warrenton Turnpike and struck Warren’s two New York regiments before the Federal command understood the scale of the Confederate movement.
Warren’s brigadeThe 5th and 10th New York were broken in minutesWarren had moved south of the turnpike on his own initiative and joined Charles E. Hazlett’s battery. Hazlett withdrew his guns, but Warren’s exposed infantry suffered catastrophic losses.
5th New York losses117 killed or mortally wounded from 490 engagedThe New York State Military Museum gives this regimental total. The National Park Service calls it the greatest number killed in ten minutes by any regiment during the Civil War.
Episode endpointThe Texas Brigade surged toward Chinn RidgeHood’s troops overran Mark Kern’s Pennsylvania battery and shattered stragglers from Reynolds’s movement. The episode closes before the improvised Federal defense on Chinn Ridge.
Recommended book“Summer Lightning: A Guide to the Second Battle of Manassas” by Matt Spruill III and Matt Spruill IVThe episode recommends the guide for its official-report excerpts, battle narrative, tactical maps, and 20-stop battlefield tour.

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Col. William S. H. Baylor

Commander, Stonewall Brigade. Killed while leading the depleted Stonewall Brigade into the threatened Deep Cut sector. The National Park Service account states that he died with the 33rd Virginia’s flag wrapped around his body.

United States

Col. Henry S. Roberts

Commander, 1st Michigan Infantry. Killed during Porter’s assault. The 1st Michigan belonged to Charles W. Roberts’s brigade in the center of the Federal formation advancing toward the unfinished railroad.
