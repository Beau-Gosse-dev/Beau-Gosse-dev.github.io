What happened

Lee held an inverted-V position at North Anna

The Confederate line met at Ox Ford and extended southwest and southeast. Grant’s two wings could not easily support one another across the river, while Lee could move troops inside the angle.

A later account claimed Lee intended to attack

Charles Venable said Lee wanted Hill to strike Warren’s isolated V Corps but illness prevented close direction. The episode notes that no contemporary order or troop movement clearly supports the claim.

The Confederate posture remained defensive

Hill and Anderson made no preparation for a general assault. Ewell’s limited attack against Gibbon on May 24 avoided a larger engagement.

U.S. scouts mapped Lee’s position

By noon on May 25, probes had established the shape of the Confederate line. Grant told Halleck that Lee was making a determined stand between the North and South Anna rivers.

Grant revised operations elsewhere in Virginia

He planned to withdraw most of Smith’s troops from Bermuda Hundred and send them to the Pamunkey, while David Hunter renewed operations in the Shenandoah Valley.

Grant considered moving around either flank

A western movement could cut the Virginia Central Railroad but would complicate supply. An eastern movement repeated the earlier turns but offered a safer route.

The council first selected a western movement

Grant, Warren, and Henry Hunt initially supported moving around Lee’s left. Grant reconsidered because the army could not maintain a dependable supply line on that route.

Grant ordered the army toward Hanovertown

He gave Meade detailed marching orders for a southeastern movement to the Pamunkey River, about fifteen miles east of Hanover Junction.

Wilson feinted against the Confederate left

Dismounted cavalry and twelve guns created a noisy demonstration on May 26. Lee briefly believed the U.S. Army might move west.

U.S. troops destroyed the local railroads

Work parties burned ties and heated and twisted rails. At least eight miles of the Virginia Central Railroad and part of the Richmond, Fredericksburg and Potomac line were disabled.

Grant believed Lee’s army was close to defeat

Reports from prisoners and Lee’s use of earthworks led Grant to tell Halleck on May 26 that U.S. morale was higher and eventual success appeared assured.

The turning movement began

Supply wagons and artillery moved first. Torbert and Gregg’s cavalry divisions and David Russell’s VI Corps division led the march toward the Pamunkey.

U.S. forces secured Hanovertown

Cavalry drove away a small Confederate force, engineers built two pontoon bridges, and Sheridan was south of the Pamunkey by 9 a.m. on May 27.

The Army of the Potomac completed the crossing

Most infantry crossed by noon on May 28. The last U.S. troops reached the south bank shortly after midnight on May 29.

Lee shifted toward Atlee Station

Once he recognized the southeastern move, Lee withdrew roughly twelve miles. His new position left only about nine miles between the army and Richmond.

Lee sent Hampton to find the U.S. infantry

Hampton took four cavalry brigades, horse artillery, and three mounted-infantry regiments toward the Hanovertown crossings on May 28.

The reconnaissance forces met near Haw’s Shop

Hampton’s column encountered Henry Davies’s brigade, the lead of Gregg’s U.S. cavalry reconnaissance, about three-quarters of a mile from the road junction.

Hampton formed a strong dismounted line

A stream and mill pond protected the flanks. Confederate cavalry and mounted infantry built a barricade across the road, forcing Gregg to attack directly.

Gregg requested reinforcements

Two U.S. brigades could not break the position. Repeating rifles produced heavy fire, but the dismounted battle remained unresolved for more than five hours.

Custer’s brigade entered the battle

Meade refused Sheridan’s request for infantry, so Sheridan committed Custer’s available Michigan cavalry brigade to support Gregg.

Hampton began withdrawing after completing his mission

Captured soldiers confirmed that V and VI Corps were across the Pamunkey. Hampton ordered his brigades to disengage.

Custer struck Butler’s mounted infantry

Inexperienced Confederates misread U.S. movement as a retreat and advanced. Custer’s fresh brigade counterattacked, captured at least eighty men, and pressed the main Confederate line.

Both sides suffered several hundred casualties

The episode counts 297 casualties in Gregg and Custer’s commands and says Confederate losses were probably higher. The National Park Service estimates 744 total casualties.

The battle supplied the missing intelligence

Hampton confirmed the location of V and VI Corps. Sheridan learned the positions of Anderson and Ewell from Confederate prisoners.
