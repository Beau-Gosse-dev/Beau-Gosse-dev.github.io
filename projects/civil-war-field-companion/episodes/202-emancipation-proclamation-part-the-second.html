EMANCIPATION PROCLAMATION (Part the Second)

Self-emancipating people and Union field commanders forced the United States government to decide how its armies would treat people escaping slavery. Benjamin Butler’s refusal to return three men used on Confederate fortifications led to the “contraband” policy, but neither that policy nor the First Confiscation Act freed every enslaved person who reached United States lines. Congress prohibited military personnel from returning freedom seekers in March 1862. Lincoln revoked broader emancipation orders issued by John C. Frémont and David Hunter while unsuccessfully urging the loyal slave states to adopt gradual, state-directed emancipation with federal compensation. By July 1862, field practice, legislation, and military necessity had moved federal policy substantially closer to presidential emancipation
Episode scopeThe federal policy path from “contraband” to compensated emancipationThe episode follows events from May 1861 through Lincoln’s final July 1862 appeal to border-state representatives. The Second Confiscation Act and Lincoln’s presidential proclamation are reserved for the next episode.
Enslaved population in 18603,953,760 people in the United StatesThe episode rounds the total to almost four million. The 1860 census map counted 3,350,343 enslaved people in the fifteen slaveholding states shown on the map; the national total also included enslaved people elsewhere.
Deep South totalMore than 2.3 million enslaved people in seven seceding statesThe state totals for South Carolina, Mississippi, Louisiana, Alabama, Florida, Georgia, and Texas sum to 2,312,046.
Largest state proportions57.2% in South Carolina and 55.1% in MississippiIn 1860 enslaved people were also 47.0% of Louisiana’s population, 45.1% of Alabama’s, 43.9% of Florida’s, and 43.7% of Georgia’s.
Fort Monroe’s statusA United States-held fort inside seceded VirginiaIts position at Hampton Roads made it both a military base and a reachable place of refuge for people escaping nearby enslavement.
The first three at Fort MonroeFrank Baker, Shepard Mallory, and James TownsendThese are the names by which the three men are known today; the National Park Service notes that definitive contemporary identification records have not survived. They escaped after being forced to work on Confederate gun positions.
Arrival and decisionMay 23–24, 1861The men arrived on May 23. Butler interviewed them the next day and refused a demand that they be returned to enslaver and Confederate colonel Charles K. Mallory.
Butler’s legal reasoningEnemy property used for war could be retained as “contraband”Butler accepted slavery’s property-law premise for the limited purpose of denying labor to the rebellion. He also argued that Virginia could not claim the benefits of the Fugitive Slave Act while claiming to have left the United States.
What the contraband decision did not doIt did not issue a formal declaration of freedomThe three men passed from an enslaver’s control into United States military custody and were hired to work at the fort. Their initiative protected them in practice, but their legal status remained unsettled.
Administration approvalSecretary of War Simon Cameron approved Butler’s policy on May 30General-in-Chief Winfield Scott and the Lincoln administration supported the refusal to surrender people reaching United States lines and the use of their labor.
Refugee movementMore than 1,000 freedom seekers near Fort Monroe by August 1861The policy followed, rather than initiated, self-emancipation. Men, women, and children continued crossing into United States-held territory, expanding the issue beyond Butler’s immediate labor needs.
House resolutionJuly 9, 1861: soldiers had no duty to capture and return fugitivesThe House resolution, urged by Representative Owen Lovejoy, expressed policy but was not yet the binding military prohibition Congress enacted in March 1862.
First Confiscation ActSigned August 6, 1861The law authorized forfeiture of an enslaver’s claim when the enslaved person was required or permitted to work in support of the rebellion. It did not abolish slavery generally or automatically cover every person who reached United States lines.
Additional Article of WarSigned March 13, 1862Congress prohibited officers and other military or naval personnel from using forces under their command to return people escaping claimed service or labor. A court-martial conviction required dismissal from service.
Frémont’s proclamationAugust 30, 1861, in MissouriMaj. Gen. John C. Frémont imposed martial law and declared enslaved people belonging to active Confederate supporters free. He acted without prior approval from Washington.
Lincoln’s response to FrémontOrdered the emancipation clause conformed to the First Confiscation ActLincoln first asked Frémont to modify the order on September 2. After Frémont declined to do so on his own authority, Lincoln publicly directed the change on September 11.
Hunter’s proclamationMay 9, 1862, in South Carolina, Georgia, and FloridaMaj. Gen. David Hunter declared enslaved people in the Department of the South free. United States control in the department extended over only limited coastal areas.
Lincoln’s response to HunterDeclared the emancipation provision void on May 19Lincoln stated that no commander had authority from the government to issue such a declaration and reserved to himself the decision whether emancipation had become a military necessity.
Border-state constraintDelaware, Maryland, Kentucky, and Missouri remained in the United StatesLincoln feared that unauthorized general emancipation could damage Unionist support in loyal slave states. Missouri was also the setting of Frémont’s order, making the issue immediate rather than hypothetical.
Contradictory field practiceSome officers sheltered freedom seekers while others excluded or returned themOrders by Henry W. Halleck and John A. Dix restricting entry to military lines illustrate why federal practice remained inconsistent before Congress imposed the March 1862 prohibition.
Compensated-emancipation proposalLincoln asked Congress for federal aid on March 6, 1862His proposed resolution offered federal financial assistance to any state that voluntarily adopted gradual abolition. Congress approved the joint resolution in April, but no loyal slave state adopted the program.
Colonization in the proposalVoluntary emigration was paired with gradual emancipationLincoln presented colonization as a way to reduce white resistance. Most Black Americans opposed leaving the country, and the wartime colonization projects attempted by the administration failed.
Delaware test case1,798 enslaved people in the 1860 censusLincoln hoped the smallest slaveholding state would lead, but opposition was strong enough that supporters did not bring a compensated-emancipation proposal to a vote in its legislature.
Final border-state appealJuly 12, 1862Lincoln warned representatives that slavery would be destroyed by the continuing war without compensation. A majority response dated July 14 disputed his reasoning and declined immediate action, although its signers said their states could consider the proposal.
Policy transitionCongress enacted the Second Confiscation Act on July 17, 1862The episode stops just before discussing the act. Its emancipation and Black-enlistment provisions supplied part of the immediate legal setting for Lincoln’s July 22 draft proclamation.
Agency and policyFreedom seekers made federal inaction impossibleBaker, Mallory, Townsend, and thousands of others acted before national emancipation policy existed. Their arrival inside United States lines forced commanders, Congress, and the president to respond.
Recommended book“The Fiery Trial” by Eric FonerThe full title is “The Fiery Trial: Abraham Lincoln and American Slavery.” The hosts had also recommended it in episode 50.
Language in this guide“Contraband” appears only as a period legal and administrative termThe term treated human beings as seized property. This guide otherwise uses “enslaved people,” “freedom seekers,” and “self-emancipating people” to describe the people whose actions drove the policy change.

What happened

The episode restates the military case against slavery

Enslaved labor sustained Confederate agriculture, logistics, and fortification work, making slavery both a war resource and the subject of a growing attack by the United States.

The 1860 enslaved population is quantified

The census counted nearly four million enslaved people nationwide, with more than 2.3 million in the seven states of the Deep South.

Military necessity and justice meet political resistance

The hosts distinguish the case for weakening the Confederacy and ending oppression from the racism and political divisions that constrained policy in the loyal states.

The war changes northern calculations

Mounting casualties, military reverses, and a longer conflict weakened arguments that the United States could defeat the rebellion while leaving slavery untouched.

Self-emancipation turns debate into an operational question

As armies entered Confederate territory, enslaved people left enslavers and reached United States lines, requiring commanders to decide whether to shelter, employ, exclude, or return them.

The narrative returns to Fort Monroe

The fort at the tip of the Virginia Peninsula remained in United States hands and became the setting of the first widely influential “contraband” decision.

Three men arrive after forced fortification work

Frank Baker, Shepard Mallory, and James Townsend escaped to Fort Monroe on May 23, 1861, after being forced to labor on Confederate gun positions.

Butler’s prewar politics are established

Benjamin Butler had been a pro-Southern Massachusetts Democrat and had supported Jefferson Davis for the Democratic presidential nomination in 1860.

Butler refuses the return demand

A Confederate representative demanded the three men under the Fugitive Slave Act. Butler refused to restore laborers who had been used to build enemy fortifications.

The contraband rationale is explained

Butler used the law-of-war concept of enemy property liable to seizure. The argument denied labor to the rebellion without yet declaring the people legally free.

Custody is distinguished from emancipation

The men were retained by the United States and hired for work at the fort, but Butler’s initial decision did not itself create a comprehensive legal status of freedom.

Secession is turned against the return demand

Butler argued that Virginia could not claim to be a foreign country through secession and simultaneously demand enforcement of a United States fugitive-slave law.

Washington endorses Butler’s action

General-in-Chief Winfield Scott, Secretary of War Simon Cameron, and President Lincoln accepted the policy of not surrendering the freedom seekers.

Cameron leaves final status unresolved

The War Department authorized protection and labor while reserving the people’s ultimate legal disposition for later federal action.

The contraband decision becomes a policy precedent

Other commanders copied Butler’s practice because it weakened Confederate labor resources and added workers inside United States lines.

The House supports refuge

With Owen Lovejoy’s urging, the House resolved on July 9, 1861, that returning fugitives was not part of a United States soldier’s duty.

Congress later makes non-return a military rule

The March 13, 1862, Additional Article of War prohibited military and naval personnel from using forces to return people escaping claimed labor.

The First Confiscation Act is introduced

The August 6, 1861, law forfeited an enslaver’s claim when an enslaved person was required or permitted to support the rebellion.

The act’s limits are stated

The first act did not abolish slavery generally and covered only labor tied to support for the rebellion.

Fort Monroe’s refugee population expands

The arrival of women and children, as well as more men than the army needed for labor, exposed the inadequacy of a policy framed only around military employment.

Butler treats the refugees as free in practice

He concluded that the United States should not become their new owner and said they should never be reclaimed by former enslavers.

Field commanders claim broader authority

The discussion shifts from case-by-case protection to departmental emancipation proclamations issued without prior presidential authorization.

Frémont takes command in Missouri

John C. Frémont commanded the Department of the West during violent irregular warfare and political conflict in the loyal slave state.

Frémont declares martial law and emancipation

His August 30 proclamation threatened execution for captured guerrillas and freedom for enslaved people belonging to Confederate supporters.

Hunter’s Department of the South is defined

David Hunter’s jurisdiction covered South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida, although United States forces controlled only limited coastal areas.

Hunter declares departmental emancipation

General Orders No. 11 of May 9, 1862, declared enslaved people throughout the three-state department free.

Lincoln rejects unauthorized general emancipation

He distinguished Butler’s limited military seizure from a field commander independently changing national policy across an entire department.

Lincoln reserves the decision to the president

His May 19 proclamation declared Hunter’s emancipation provision void and stated that the question of military necessity belonged to presidential authority.

Border-state loyalty shapes presidential policy

Lincoln feared that Frémont’s order in Missouri could alienate Unionists in Kentucky and other loyal slave states.

Lincoln countermanded both generals

Frémont’s emancipation clause was narrowed to the First Confiscation Act; Hunter’s three-state emancipation provision was declared void.

Halleck excludes freedom seekers from lines

After replacing Frémont, Henry Halleck issued restrictive orders in the western theater that conflicted with more protective field practice.

John A. Dix follows a restrictive course in Virginia

The hosts use Dix and Halleck to show that federal policy remained inconsistent across commands.

Contradictory orders create policy ambiguity

Before the March 1862 law, freedom seekers could encounter shelter in one command and exclusion or return in another.

Lincoln’s early policy is characterized as conservative

The episode contrasts his caution about national emancipation with Congress’s willingness to legislate against slaveholders supporting rebellion.

War is already eroding slavery

Lincoln later described slavery as being worn down by the ordinary incidents of war even before a comprehensive presidential policy existed.

Compensated emancipation becomes Lincoln’s border-state strategy

From late 1861 into July 1862, he urged loyal slave states to choose gradual abolition financed by the federal government.

Lincoln presents compensation as a military measure

He argued that voluntary abolition in loyal slave states would remove Confederate hopes that those states might yet join the rebellion.

The hosts question Lincoln’s strategic premise

The episode notes that no evidence shows disappointment over Maryland and Kentucky would have caused the seceded states to abandon the war.

Lincoln sends his March proposal to Congress

The March 6 message asked Congress to promise financial cooperation with any state voluntarily adopting gradual abolition.

Colonization accompanies gradualism and compensation

Lincoln paired the state-directed plan with voluntary emigration in an effort to reduce resistance among white slaveholders and northern voters.

Congress approves, but the states do not act

Both houses adopted the joint resolution, yet none of the loyal slave-state legislatures accepted the program.

Delaware declines to lead

Despite having only 1,798 enslaved people in the 1860 census, Delaware’s opposition prevented supporters from bringing a plan to a legislative vote.

Lincoln makes a final appeal to border-state representatives

At the July 12 meeting he warned that continued war would destroy slavery without compensation and again requested an immediate decision for gradual abolition.

A majority rejects immediate adoption

Most border-state representatives disputed Lincoln’s military argument and declined to endorse his proposal, though they left the matter to their states and constituents.

The episode reaches the July 1862 policy break

Congress adjourned after enacting stronger confiscation legislation, and Lincoln moved toward a commander-in-chief emancipation measure.

The book recommendation begins

The hosts recommend Eric Foner’s “The Fiery Trial: Abraham Lincoln and American Slavery.”
