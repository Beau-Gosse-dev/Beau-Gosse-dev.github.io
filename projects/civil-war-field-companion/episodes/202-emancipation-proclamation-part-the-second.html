EMANCIPATION PROCLAMATION (Part the Second)

Self-emancipating people and Union field commanders forced the United States government to decide how its armies would treat people escaping slavery. Benjamin Butler’s refusal to return three men used on Confederate fortifications led to the “contraband” policy, but neither that policy nor the First Confiscation Act freed every enslaved person who reached United States lines. Congress prohibited military personnel from returning freedom seekers in March 1862. Lincoln revoked broader emancipation orders issued by John C. Frémont and David Hunter while unsuccessfully urging the loyal slave states to adopt gradual, state-directed emancipation with federal compensation. By July 1862, field practice, legislation, and military necessity had moved federal policy substantially closer to presidential emancipation

The episode restates the military case against slavery

Enslaved labor sustained Confederate agriculture, logistics, and fortification work, making slavery both a war resource and the subject of a growing attack by the United States.

United States civil and military authority

Commander in Chief and chief executivePresident Abraham LincolnApproved Butler’s limited field policy, ordered Frémont’s proclamation narrowed, voided Hunter’s proclamation, and proposed state-directed compensated emancipation
War Department through January 14, 1862Secretary of War Simon CameronApproved Butler’s refusal to return freedom seekers and authorized their employment while leaving final legal status unresolved
War Department from January 15, 1862Secretary of War Edwin M. StantonLed the department when Congress prohibited military returns and Lincoln revoked Hunter’s departmental emancipation order
General in Chief through November 1, 1861Lt. Gen. Winfield ScottReceived Butler’s report from Fort Monroe and supported the decision to retain people used in Confederate military labor
General in Chief, November 1, 1861–March 11, 1862Maj. Gen. George B. McClellanHeld senior Army authority during part of the period; field policies nevertheless remained inconsistent across departments

United States field commands

Department of Virginia at Fort MonroeMaj. Gen. Benjamin F. ButlerRefused to return Baker, Mallory, and Townsend; retained and employed freedom seekers reaching United States lines
Department of the WestMaj. Gen. John C. FrémontIssued the August 30, 1861, Missouri proclamation without prior presidential approval
Department of the Missouri, later Department of the MississippiMaj. Gen. Henry W. HalleckReplaced Frémont and issued orders excluding freedom seekers from military lines before the March 1862 statutory prohibition on returns
Departmental command in VirginiaMaj. Gen. John A. DixUsed restrictive policy toward freedom seekers, contributing to inconsistent treatment across United States commands
Department of the SouthMaj. Gen. David HunterDeclared enslaved people in South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida free; Lincoln voided that provision ten days later
