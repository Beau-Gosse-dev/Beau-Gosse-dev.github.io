Episode 151 · May 26–30, 1862 · Lower Shenandoah Valley and its approaches: Harpers Ferry, Halltown, Charles Town, Winchester, Front Royal, Strasburg, Moorefield, and the roads west from Fredericksburg

STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: THE HUNTER BECOMES THE HUNTED

Jackson completed his demonstration against Harpers Ferry and began withdrawing toward Winchester, but the Federal attempt to cut his route through Strasburg was still developing when the episode ended. Shields retook Front Royal on May 30 and Frémont approached from the west, placing Jackson’s separated columns in serious danger. No decisive battle or final result occurs within this episode; the escape through Strasburg continues in episode 152
Episode scopeMay 26 through the night of May 30, 1862The opening reviews the campaign through First Winchester. The ending looks ahead to the threatened closure of Jackson’s escape route on May 31.
Strategic purposeKeep United States troops away from RichmondJackson’s Valley operations forced Washington to retain or redirect formations that otherwise could reinforce George B. McClellan on the Peninsula.
May 26–27Two rest days at Winchester, then a move toward Harpers FerryJackson ordered thanksgiving services on May 26. On May 27 he decided to carry out Robert E. Lee’s direction to create the appearance of a threat to the Potomac line.
Stonewall BrigadeOrdered toward Harpers Ferry by way of Charles TownCharles S. Winder’s brigade led the demonstration and later had to hurry back to rejoin the army.
Confederate flank guards12th Georgia at Front Royal; Taylor at Berryville; Ashby to the west; 1st Maryland at MartinsburgThe detachments watched routes into Jackson’s exposed flanks and rear while the main body moved north.
Harpers Ferry defenseAbout 7,000 United States troops under Brig. Gen. Rufus SaxtonThis is the episode’s figure. Saxton held commanding ground that included Maryland Heights.
Confederate high-water markLoudoun Heights and the approaches to Bolivar HeightsBy May 29 the Stonewall Brigade occupied Loudoun Heights, while Jackson brought the main body to Halltown within sight of Bolivar Heights.
Warning and confirmationA civilian warned of Shields; Ashby and scouts later confirmed both Federal pincersJackson initially dismissed the unidentified civilian’s report as rumor. On the night of May 29 he accepted military reports that Frémont was moving toward Strasburg and Shields toward Front Royal.
Federal trapFrémont from the west and Shields from the east, converging toward StrasburgStrasburg controlled Jackson’s direct southward route on the Valley Turnpike.
Federal forces in the episodeSaxton 7,000; Banks 7,000; Frémont 14,800; Shields 11,000; Ord 10,000Those figures sum to 49,800. The episode says “just over 52,000”; the Army Center of Military History also places about 2,000 under Geary east of the Blue Ridge, bringing the wider nearby total to about 51,800. These were approximate strengths spread across the theater.
Jackson’s strengthAbout 16,000 in the episode’s accountingThe National Park Service gives approximately 17,000 by late May. Differences reflect rounding and the inclusion or availability of detached troops.
Frémont’s original orderMove from Franklin to HarrisonburgThe episode gives 41 miles on the map but emphasizes that poor roads, weak horses, bad weather, and an overextended supply line made the direct route impracticable.
Frémont’s chosen routeBack to Petersburg, then east through Moorefield toward StrasburgHe left extra baggage, tents, and knapsacks at Petersburg, replenished ammunition, and issued five days of cooked rations.
Lincoln’s rebuke“You were expressly ordered to march to Harrisonburg. What does this mean?”The episode dates the telegram at 9:58 p.m. after Frémont reached Moorefield. The wording is preserved in the Official Records.
Revised convergence orderShields at Front Royal and Frémont at or near StrasburgAt noon on May 29 Lincoln directed Frémont to be at Strasburg, or as near it as Jackson could be, by noon May 30. Frémont replied that he could not arrive before 5 p.m. May 31.
Lincoln to McDowell“It is for you a question of legs”Lincoln urged maximum speed on May 28. McDowell answered that he was using everything “which legs and steam are capable of.”
Shields’s movementDeparted Fredericksburg on May 26 and pushed rapidly toward Front RoyalEdward O. C. Ord’s division followed on a more circuitous route, and George D. Bayard’s cavalry was ordered to use the shortest road.
May 30 withdrawalMost of Jackson’s army left the Harpers Ferry front for WinchesterWinder remained behind for one final feint, then became the rear guard. Heavy rain and muddy roads slowed the march.
Jackson’s rail movementCharles Town to Winchester by trainAlexander R. Boteler recalled that Jackson slept during the trip except when Jedediah Hotchkiss intercepted the train at Stephenson’s Depot.
Front Royal retakenNathan Kimball’s brigade arrived about 11:30 a.m. on May 30The 12th Georgia withdrew. Conner’s men burned the road bridge over the South Fork but left the railroad bridge intact.
Conner arrestedJackson held him responsible for yielding Front Royal without lossesThe episode’s dialogue comes from postwar recollection. Conner reported that none of his men had been killed; that statement is not a complete casualty return.
Night of May 30Jackson sent Hotchkiss to recall Winder after 10 p.m.Hotchkiss later remembered unusual anxiety as Jackson waited in Winchester for the distant Stonewall Brigade.
Race to StrasburgShields about 12 miles away; Frémont about 20 miles awayThe episode places Jackson’s main body about 25 miles from Strasburg at Stephenson’s Depot and the Stonewall Brigade nearly twice that distance away. These are approximate nighttime positions.
Casualties and leader lossesNo complete loss return; no important leader killed or mortally woundedConner’s assertion of no men killed does not establish zero wounded, captured, or missing. The episode is dominated by marches, demonstrations, reports, and command decisions.
Transcript normalizationsFrémont, Ewell, Staunton, Schenck, Luray, Massanutten, Loudoun Heights, Moorefield, Ord, Conner, Hotchkiss, Boteler, and StrasburgThe automated transcript renders several of these names as “Yule,” “Stanton,” “Cenk,” “LeRae,” “Massanet,” “Loudon,” “Moorfield,” “Orr,” “Connor,” “Jedidiah,” “Bodler,” and “Strasbourg.”
