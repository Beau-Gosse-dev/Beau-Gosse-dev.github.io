PERRYVILLE (Part the Second)

Operational United States success leading into battle. Don Carlos Buell’s rapid advance forced the Confederates to abandon Bardstown and Frankfort, while Braxton Bragg’s faulty information and divided command delayed a coherent response. Bragg ordered Leonidas Polk to fight at Perryville as a temporary check before concentrating at Harrodsburg. Before dawn on October 8, Daniel McCook’s United States brigade drove the 7th Arkansas from Peters Hill and secured Doctors Creek. The main Battle of Perryville occurs after this episode’s narrative ends
Episode scopeThe march from Louisville and the opening clash at PerryvilleThe narrative begins with Buell’s October 1 advance, follows the Confederate withdrawals from Bardstown and Frankfort, and ends after the pre-dawn fight for Peters Hill on October 8.
United States planFour columns left Louisville on October 1Joshua W. Sill demonstrated toward Frankfort while the columns under Alexander McCook, Thomas Crittenden, and Charles Gilbert moved southeast toward Bardstown on parallel roads.
Campaign stakesConfederates controlled most of Kentucky east of the Louisville and Nashville RailroadBragg and Edmund Kirby Smith also intended to install a Confederate state government at Frankfort, making a successful United States counteroffensive politically as well as militarily urgent.
Green regimentsInexperienced soldiers discarded equipment on the first marchPrivate Timothy Pendergrass of the 2nd Minnesota recalled roadside piles of boots, blankets, clothing, books, and other possessions abandoned by overloaded recruits.
Failed Confederate reconnaissancePolk learned of Buell’s advance only on October 2Confederate cavalry mounts were worn down after weeks of campaigning, while newly equipped United States cavalry screened the four advancing columns.
Bragg’s first misreadingHe treated Sill’s Frankfort movement as Buell’s main effortWithout cavalry close at hand to verify the reports, Bragg planned to use Kirby Smith as a blocking force west of Frankfort while Polk struck the United States flank and rear from Bardstown.
Polk’s dilemmaBragg’s order pointed directly across McCook’s pathBy October 3, Polk recognized that Buell’s main columns were converging on Bardstown. A move northeast through Bloomfield risked battle against McCook before the other United States columns could be held off.
Council of warPolk’s officers agreed to withdraw toward HarrodsburgAt Bardstown on October 3, Patton Anderson first argued for literal obedience but conceded that, absent Bragg’s order, retreat was the only realistic course. Polk chose the retreat because Bragg lacked current information.
A fugitive reaches the 21st WisconsinThe escaped man got away during a confrontation at BloomfieldColonel Benjamin Sweet refused to help three pursuers. Division commander Lovell Rousseau ordered the man returned, but the dispute escalated after soldiers drove Rousseau’s aide from camp with thrown corncobs.
Rousseau’s responseThe 21st Wisconsin was placed unarmed inside a hollow squareRousseau threatened the men who had attacked his aide. Several soldiers stepped forward; after Sweet and his officers intervened, Rousseau released them, and the fugitive had meanwhile escaped.
Legal settingKentucky remained a slave state in the UnionThe preliminary Emancipation Proclamation applied to states or parts of states in rebellion, not loyal Kentucky. The Bloomfield incident exposed a sharp conflict between military authority, slaveholders’ claims, and antislavery soldiers.
Bardstown evacuatedPolk and Hardee marched east on October 4Their wings used separate roads no more than about ten miles apart, protecting one another and the Confederate supply depot at Camp Breckinridge, formerly Camp Dick Robinson.
Frankfort ceremonyRichard Hawes was installed as Confederate governor on October 4Bragg proceeded despite appeals to postpone. Artillery from Sill’s approaching column interrupted the luncheon, and the Confederates abandoned Frankfort the same day.
One hundred hoursBuell overturned the Confederate position in roughly four daysBy October 4 the United States advance had recovered Bardstown and Frankfort and forced Bragg to search for a place to concentrate and fight.
Bragg’s intended concentrationHarrodsburg, not PerryvilleHarrodsburg stood near the supply depot at Camp Breckinridge and at a road junction where Bragg expected his Army of Mississippi and Kirby Smith’s Army of Kentucky to unite.
Perryville assignmentPolk was to strike, disengage, and return to HarrodsburgAfter Hardee’s left wing halted around Perryville, Bragg sent Polk with Benjamin Cheatham’s division and ordered him to give battle on October 8, rout the pursuers, and then rejoin the planned concentration.
Forces near PerryvilleAbout 16,000 Confederates by the night of October 7Polk assumed local command. Hardee’s divisions under Simon B. Buckner and Patton Anderson held positions west and northwest of town while Cheatham arrived by the Harrodsburg Pike.
Drought battlefieldWater shaped the opening movementsMost streams were dry or nearly dry after prolonged heat. Doctors Creek, although low, was one of the best nearby sources and lay between Sheridan’s soldiers and the Confederate position on Peters Hill.
Perryville crossroadsFour major roads met at a town of about 400 peopleRoads linked Perryville to Harrodsburg, Danville, Lebanon, and Springfield. Smaller roads reached Mitchellsburg and Mackville, giving the hamlet operational importance beyond its size.
Civilian flightMost residents left on the night of October 7–8Some families sheltered in cellars, but many fled as the approaching armies transformed farms around the Chaplin River into a battlefield.
Peters HillHigh ground controlling the Springfield Pike and Doctors CreekSt. John R. Liddell sent the 7th Arkansas forward from Bottom Hill because Peters Hill commanded the approach and the pike crossed the creek immediately below it.
United States night advanceDaniel McCook’s 36th Brigade moved at about 3 a.m.Sheridan wanted full canteens before the expected battle and received permission to secure Doctors Creek and the hill. The 52nd Ohio led, with Illinois regiments supporting and in reserve.
Opening infantry clashThe 7th Arkansas withdrew from Peters HillCaught by the night movement, the Arkansans exchanged a short, sharp fire and fell back to Bottom Hill. McCook’s soldiers held the creek and hill within roughly an hour.
Leaders soon lostJackson, Terrill, and Webster fell later on October 8The episode foreshadows the deaths of three of the four United States officers who met on October 7: Brig. Gen. James S. Jackson was killed; Brig. Gen. William R. Terrill and Col. George P. Webster were mortally wounded. Philip Sheridan survived.

What happened

Buell’s four-column plan resumes

One column would demonstrate toward Frankfort while the other three advanced through Bardstown to force battle or threaten the Confederate line of communications.

The Army of the Ohio leaves Louisville

Buell’s counteroffensive began October 1, less than a week after the army reached and reorganized at Louisville.

Four roads carry the advance

Sill moved toward Frankfort; McCook, Crittenden, and Gilbert formed the left, center, and right of the main southeastward movement.

Buell surprises the Confederate cavalry

Worn mounts and effective United States screening kept Polk from learning of the advance until October 2.

Bragg misreads Sill’s demonstration

Bragg assumed Frankfort was Buell’s principal target and planned a concentration west of the capital without personally verifying the movement.

Polk faces an impossible order

Bragg directed him through Bloomfield against Buell’s flank, but Polk saw that the main United States columns were already closing on Bardstown.

Polk convenes a council of war

His officers concluded that Bragg lacked current information and agreed the Army of Mississippi should withdraw east toward Harrodsburg.

The retreat order is issued

Polk chose to ignore Bragg’s operational direction and evacuate Bardstown before Buell’s converging columns could trap him.

A fugitive enters the 21st Wisconsin camp

Three pursuers demanded the escaped man’s return, but Colonel Benjamin Sweet refused to assist them.

Rousseau orders the man returned

The division commander’s order led to a confrontation in which Wisconsin soldiers drove his aide from camp with thrown corncobs.

Rousseau forms a hollow square

He placed the unarmed 21st Wisconsin inside the rest of Starkweather’s armed brigade and demanded that the men responsible step forward.

The fugitive escapes

Rousseau released the soldiers after their officers intervened; during the commotion, the man at the center of the dispute got away.

The Confederates abandon Bardstown

Polk’s right wing and Hardee’s left wing started east on separate but mutually supporting roads early on October 4.

Bragg proceeds with the inauguration

Despite the deteriorating military position, Bragg oversaw Richard Hawes’s installation as Confederate governor at Frankfort.

United States artillery interrupts the luncheon

Sill’s arrival forced the ceremony’s participants to flee, and his troops reoccupied Frankfort that night.

Buell regroups at Bardstown

The three main corps resumed their parallel eastward pursuit on October 5 while Sill slowly moved south from Frankfort.

Bragg selects Harrodsburg

Road connections and proximity to Camp Breckinridge made Harrodsburg the intended concentration point for Bragg and Kirby Smith.

Cavalry screens Hardee’s retreat

Joseph Wheeler’s horsemen delayed Gilbert’s pursuing corps and fought a successful action at Pottsville on October 7.

Hardee halts at Perryville

His two divisions stopped around the town while the leading United States troops camped about three miles west.

Bragg orders Polk to fight

Polk was to bring Cheatham’s division, attack on October 8, and then move back toward the planned concentration at Harrodsburg.

Buell prepares with an injured foot

A horse had fallen on Buell that afternoon, but he recognized that part of the withdrawing Confederate army had been brought to ground at Perryville.

Four officers meet before battle

Sheridan, James Jackson, William Terrill, and George Webster discussed the odds of all four being killed. Sheridan alone would survive the next day.

Drought and heat punish both armies

Most streams were dry, and the Confederates often emptied scarce water sources before the pursuing United States troops arrived.

The armies enter Kentucky’s historic triangle

Harrodsburg, Danville, and Perryville lay roughly ten miles apart and formed the terrain on which the campaign would be decided.

Perryville’s roads converge

The small town connected directly with Harrodsburg, Danville, Lebanon, and Springfield, making it an important crossroads.

Residents flee the coming battle

Most civilians left during the night of October 7–8, although some remained and sheltered in cellars.

Hardee deploys west of town

Buckner’s division held ground west of Perryville and Anderson’s division stood to the northwest as Polk assumed local command.

Peters Hill commands the creek

Liddell pushed the 7th Arkansas onto the height because it overlooked the Springfield Pike crossing and one of the area’s few usable water sources.

Polk takes command at Perryville

About 16,000 Confederate soldiers were then in and around the town, with Cheatham’s division nearby on the Harrodsburg Pike.

Sheridan focuses on Doctors Creek

His thirsty 11th Division held the closest United States position and expected the next day to bring a battle.

Daniel McCook receives the assignment

Sheridan entrusted the creek and hill to the untested 36th Brigade, whose discipline and leadership had impressed him.

The 52nd Ohio moves before dawn

McCook’s troops advanced under a nearly full moon, crossed Doctors Creek, and climbed Peters Hill.

The infantry battle opens

The 7th Arkansas was surprised by the night advance and withdrew toward Bottom Hill after a short exchange of fire.

McCook secures water and high ground

Within about an hour, the United States brigade controlled Doctors Creek and Peters Hill, setting the stage for the main battle.

Important leaders killed or wounded

United States

Brig. Gen. James S. Jackson

Commander, 10th Division, I Corps. Killed in the main battle on October 8. The episode foreshadows his death after describing his meeting with Sheridan, Terrill, and Webster the night before.

United States

Brig. Gen. William R. Terrill

33rd Brigade commander, 10th Division, I Corps. Mortally wounded by shell fragments during the main battle and died that night. He was one of the three officers from the October 7 meeting who did not survive.

United States

Col. George P. Webster

34th Brigade commander, 10th Division, I Corps. Mortally wounded during the main battle and died soon afterward. Sheridan was the sole survivor of the four officers who met on the eve of battle.
