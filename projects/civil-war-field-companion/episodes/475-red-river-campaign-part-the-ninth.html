Episode 475 · April 7–25, 1864 · Red River and Cane River corridor from Loggy Bayou and Grand Ecore to Alexandria, Louisiana

RED RIVER CAMPAIGN (Part the Ninth)

After Banks’s defeats at Mansfield and Pleasant Hill, Porter abandoned the advance toward Shreveport and brought his fleet back to Grand Ecore under repeated attack. The gunboat Eastport struck a mine and was later destroyed when it could not be freed. Banks left Grand Ecore on April 21. At Monett’s Ferry, Emory used an unguarded ford to send Birge’s brigade behind Hamilton Bee’s blocking force, which withdrew and opened the road to Alexandria. The last Union troops crossed on April 24 and the head of the column reached Alexandria on April 25, but low water trapped much of Porter’s fleet above the city

What happened

The narrative returned to Louisiana

Banks and Porter were both withdrawing after the April 8 defeat at Mansfield and the April 9 fighting at Pleasant Hill.

Porter had left Grand Ecore on April 7

He selected shallow-draft, heavily armed vessels for the final approach toward Shreveport because the Red River was falling, narrow, and sharply curved.

The fleet escorted army transports

About twenty transports carried supplies and roughly 1,600 XVII Corps soldiers under T. Kilby Smith. The transports were armed and protected with cotton bales and sacks of oats.

Low water delayed the movement

Iberville ran aground on April 8. Confederates had reduced the river’s flow by destroying the Tone’s Bayou dam on March 18.

Poor maps left Porter uncertain of his position

Federal charts misplaced and misnamed waterways near Loggy Bayou and Springfield Landing.

A scuttled steamer blocked the river

Confederates placed New Falls City across the channel, preventing the fleet from continuing toward Shreveport.

Kilby Smith secured the riverbank

The growing Confederate presence convinced Porter that Banks had probably been defeated.

The fleet learned of Banks’s retreat

William Andres and fifty cavalrymen reported the fighting at Mansfield and Pleasant Hill and ordered Kilby Smith back to Grand Ecore.

The vessels immediately turned back

Porter expected Confederate cavalry and artillery to attack the fleet along the confined river.

Large ships had to back downstream

The channel was too narrow for them to turn. Snags, grounding, damaged steering gear, and continuous musket fire slowed the withdrawal.

The fleet reached Blair’s Landing

On April 12, about 2,500 Texas cavalrymen under Tom Green attacked from concealed positions supported by four artillery pieces.

Osage grounded under fire

The transport Black Hawk, tied alongside the monitor, was struck repeatedly and most people aboard took shelter inside Osage.

Lexington silenced the Confederate battery

The timberclad used its eight-inch guns for about an hour while Osage worked free.

Tom Green was killed

After Osage broke free, one of its eleven-inch guns fired grapeshot into Green’s cavalry and killed the Confederate commander.

John Warner grounded

The supply ship could not be freed on April 13. Fort Hindman stayed behind until it pulled the vessel loose on April 15.

The fleet reunited with the army

The last vessels reached Grand Ecore on April 15.

Banks still proposed another advance

He told Grant that the Confederates were on the defensive and said he intended to try another route to Shreveport.

Banks retained A. J. Smith’s troops

Sherman demanded their return for the Atlanta campaign, but Banks used his seniority to keep them in Louisiana.

Banks removed subordinates

He replaced chief of staff Charles Stone with William Dwight and relieved cavalry commanders Albert Lee and Napoleon Dudley.

The army and fleet committed to Alexandria

Porter said falling water made a naval withdrawal unavoidable. Banks then said the army could not advance without naval support.

Eastport struck a mine

The ironclad hit a Confederate mine on April 16, was refloated five days later, and grounded again near Montgomery.

Porter destroyed Eastport

With water falling at Alexandria, he abandoned and blew up the immobilized ironclad on April 26 rather than delay the fleet further.

Confederate artillery attacked the remaining ships

At the Cane River mouth a battery sank one support vessel, disabled another, and hit Cricket thirty-eight times, causing twenty-five casualties.

Low water trapped the fleet at Alexandria

The river had fallen six feet, leaving most vessels unable to pass the rapids below town.

Banks left Grand Ecore on April 21

His column marched south along Cane River while Porter’s vessels used the roughly parallel Red River channel.

Taylor tried to close Monett’s Ferry

If Confederates held the southern end of the island between the rivers, the Union column could be trapped.

The column marched twenty miles overnight

The lead stopped at 2:30 a.m. on April 22 so the rear guard could close up.

Bee held the ferry crossing

About 1,600 Confederate cavalrymen and four artillery batteries occupied the wooded bluff across the river.

An unguarded ford was found

A local enslaved man showed Union cavalry a crossing about two miles from the ferry.

Birge approached the Confederate rear

His brigade crossed at the ford, made a wide flanking march, and reached the ridge without detection.

Emory fixed Bee at the ferry

Union artillery and an infantry feint forced Bee to divide attention between his front and Birge’s force behind him.

Bee withdrew

An incorrect report of another threat to his flank contributed to the decision. The withdrawal opened the crossing before Taylor could complete the trap.

Banks’s army escaped the island

The final troops crossed on April 24, and the lead elements reached Alexandria the next afternoon.
