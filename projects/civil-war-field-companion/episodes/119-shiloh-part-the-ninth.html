Episode 119 · April 6, 1862, about noon through nightfall · Jones Field and the western Shiloh battlefield; Crump’s Landing, Stoney Lonesome, the Shunpike, River Road, and Pittsburg Landing, Tennessee

SHILOH (Part the Ninth)

Sherman and McClernand counterattacked from Jones Field around noon or 12:30 p.m. Fourteen regiments advanced, surprised the Confederate left, and drove part of it back nearly half a mile before ammunition shortages forced a return to Jones Field. The attack delayed and further disorganized Hardee’s forces. North of the battlefield, Lew Wallace received Grant’s written order at about 11:30 a.m., allowed a meal break, and marched on the Shunpike toward Sherman’s former position. After learning after 2 p.m. that the United States line had moved, he countermarched, crossed to the River Road, and reached Grant’s army after dark following a march of at least sixteen miles

What happened

The episode places the Hornet’s Nest in context

The center’s defense was important, but it did not face the same concentration of Confederate forces as Sherman and McClernand on the western battlefield.

Sherman and McClernand reorganized at Jones Field

Seven brigades had suffered different levels of damage; only McDowell’s brigade remained fresh and nearly intact.

Sherman decided to counterattack

He judged that Confederate disorganization created an opportunity to delay the next assault and restore part of the United States position.

Fourteen regiments moved south

Around noon or 12:30, a ragged line advanced from Jones Field. About one-third of the formations in the sector did not participate.

The counterattack achieved surprise

McDowell’s and Marsh’s brigades formed the most stable parts of the advance and collected soldiers separated from other units.

The fighting divided into two results

Marsh fought a bloody standoff against Preston Smith and Patton Anderson, while McDowell drove Confederates back nearly half a mile.

Hardee threatened McDowell’s exposed flank

The flank threat halted further progress, and ammunition shortages then forced United States regiments back to Jones Field.

The counterattack delayed the Confederate left

It increased the confusion among Hardee’s intermingled formations and postponed another organized offensive.

Grant expected two reinforcing divisions

William Nelson approached on the east bank from Savannah, while Lew Wallace’s 3rd Division was near Crump’s Landing on the west bank.

Nelson’s march was delayed by mud

The head of his column did not reach the river opposite Pittsburg Landing until nearly sunset, too late for Sunday’s main fighting.

Wallace assembled at Stoney Lonesome

Around 6 a.m., he concentrated his division where roads led both to Pittsburg Landing and to Sherman’s original right.

Grant met Wallace at Crump’s Landing

At about 8:30 a.m., Grant told Wallace to remain ready to march when further orders arrived.

Grant sent the written order

After reaching Pittsburg Landing around 9 or 9:30, Grant dispatched A. S. Baxter by steamer to order Wallace forward.

Baxter could not find the division

Crump’s Landing was deserted. Wallace had left a horse but no staff officer to direct the messenger to Stoney Lonesome, costing about an hour.

Wallace received the order at about 11:30

The written order was later lost, and witnesses disagreed over whether it specified Sherman’s right or the River Road toward Pittsburg Landing.

Wallace selected the Shunpike

He chose the road leading to Sherman’s original right rather than the River Road leading behind the army’s changing line.

The division paused for a meal

Wallace allowed a half-hour dinner break after receiving the order, despite knowing that a battle was underway.

Rowley reached Wallace after 2 p.m.

He explained that Sherman no longer held the Shunpike destination and that Grant wanted the division at Pittsburg Landing.

Wallace countermarched

He preserved the order of his column, adding about half an hour before taking a rough crossroad toward the River Road.

Rawlins and McPherson overtook the column

They found Wallace’s division halted on the crossroad and urged faster movement; McPherson suggested moving artillery to speed the march.

The column reached the River Road

It moved south through deep mud and standing water in the Snake Creek bottomland.

The final defensive guns were audible

Grant’s siege guns signaled that the army was making its last stand near Pittsburg Landing.

Wallace arrived after dark

The 3rd Division completed at least sixteen miles in seven or eight hours despite beginning the day roughly five miles from the battlefield.
