Episode 156 · July 27, 1861–June 1, 1862 · Washington, D.C.; northern Virginia; Hampton Roads; and the Virginia Peninsula from Fort Monroe through Yorktown and Williamsburg to the Chickahominy River and the eastern approaches to Richmond

BACK TO THE PENINSULA...

Campaign recap through the indecisive Battle of Seven Pines, also called Fair Oaks. The Army of the Potomac had advanced to Richmond’s outskirts but had not captured the Confederate capital. Joseph E. Johnston’s May 31 attack failed to destroy the two Federal corps south of the flooded Chickahominy, and Johnston was seriously wounded. Jefferson Davis placed Robert E. Lee in command of the Confederate field army on June 1. The Seven Days Battles are outside this episode’s scope
Episode scopeMcClellan’s rise, campaign planning, and Peninsula advance through Seven Pines and Lee’s appointmentThis is a chronological review before the podcast begins the Seven Days Battles. It covers several operations rather than one battlefield action.
Starting pointMcClellan took command of the forces around Washington on July 27, 1861He rebuilt the defeated force into the Army of the Potomac and became general-in-chief after Winfield Scott retired in November 1861.
Lincoln’s pressure for movementGeneral War Order No. 1, issued January 27, 1862, called for a general movement on February 22Lincoln’s January 31 Special War Order No. 1 separately directed the Army of the Potomac toward the Manassas area. McClellan answered with an alternative amphibious plan.
Original campaign designMove by water to Urbanna, turn Johnston’s right, and approach Richmond before the Confederate army could respondThe plan depended on water transport, rapid movement, and leaving enough troops to secure Washington.
Why Urbanna was abandonedJohnston evacuated the Manassas–Centreville position on March 9 and withdrew toward the RappahannockThe retreat removed the fixed Confederate position that the Urbanna landing was meant to turn. The episode places the movement one day early; the guide uses the date in official campaign chronologies. McClellan shifted the landing point to Fort Monroe.
Change in Federal commandLincoln removed McClellan as general-in-chief on March 11 while leaving him in command of the Army of the PotomacThe president and Secretary of War Edwin M. Stanton temporarily handled the broader coordination of the armies.
Hampton Roads constraintCSS Virginia remained a threat after its March 9 duel with USS Monitor and complicated use of the James RiverThe episode treats the ironclad’s presence as a reason McClellan committed his advance to the York River line. Virginia was scuttled on May 11, after the campaign’s supply arrangements had already developed on the York and Pamunkey rivers.
Hampton Roads date correctionCSS Virginia attacked the Federal wooden warships on March 8; USS Monitor fought Virginia on March 9The audio says March 7 and “the very next day.” National Park Service and Naval History and Heritage Command records establish March 8–9.
Peninsula movement beginsThe first Army of the Potomac troops sailed from Alexandria on March 17An armada carried more than 100,000 soldiers to the Fort Monroe area over the following weeks. The number available at any one stage varied as formations arrived and other forces were retained outside the peninsula.
Washington-security disputeLincoln withheld Irvin McDowell’s I Corps after concluding that McClellan had not left the promised covering forceThe episode emphasizes the mistrust created by McClellan’s April 1 strength report. Exact counts depend on which garrisons and field formations are included.
Yorktown obstacleMagruder’s line used Yorktown, earthworks, the Warwick River, dams, and flooded ground to block the peninsulaThe National Park Service places Magruder’s initial force at roughly 11,000. Demonstrations and the defensive terrain helped convince McClellan that a deliberate siege was necessary.
Federal numerical advantageMcClellan had a large superiority when he reached the Warwick line but believed the defense was much stronger than it wasStrength estimates changed as Johnston reinforced Magruder. The episode’s “five to one” statement applies to the initial confrontation, not the entire month-long siege.
Siege of YorktownMcClellan halted on April 5 and prepared heavy batteries instead of forcing an immediate passageThis delay allowed Johnston to transfer most of his field army to the peninsula. McClellan planned to open a major bombardment on May 5.
Confederate strategy disputeJohnston wanted to fall back toward Richmond; Davis ordered the Yorktown–Warwick line heldAt a long April 14 conference, Davis, Lee, Secretary of War George W. Randolph, Johnston, Gustavus W. Smith, and James Longstreet debated the position. Johnston complied but continued preparing to retreat.
Yorktown evacuationJohnston withdrew during the night of May 3–4 before McClellan’s bombardment beganConfederate artillery fire helped cover the retreat. Federal troops occupied the abandoned works on May 4.
Battle of WilliamsburgA Confederate rear guard delayed the Federal pursuit on May 5, after which Johnston continued toward RichmondNational Park Service figures give approximately 2,300 United States and 1,600 Confederate casualties. The engagement did not stop the Confederate withdrawal.
James River remains closedFederal gunboats were repulsed at Drewry’s Bluff on May 15Norfolk had fallen and CSS Virginia had been scuttled, but the Confederate batteries and obstructions at Drewry’s Bluff prevented a naval advance to Richmond.
Federal supply lineWhite House Landing on the Pamunkey and the Richmond & York River Railroad supported the advanceProtecting this supply system influenced McClellan’s decision to keep a large part of his army north of the Chickahominy.
McDowell does not joinLincoln suspended McDowell’s march from Fredericksburg after Jackson’s victories in the Shenandoah ValleyBy May 24 elements of McClellan’s army were about five miles from Richmond, but the expected reinforcement from the north was redirected toward the Valley crisis.
Army divided by the ChickahominyKeyes’s IV Corps and Heintzelman’s III Corps stood south of the river while three corps were north of it before the battleHeavy rain flooded the low ground and threatened the bridges. Johnston saw an opportunity to strike the two exposed corps before support crossed.
Seven Pines attackJohnston ordered an attack for May 31, with D. H. Hill’s division bearing the main early fightingThe episode reconstructs a coordinated attack involving Longstreet on the left and Benjamin Huger on the right. Johnston issued incomplete oral and written instructions, and surviving accounts disagree over intended routes and responsibility for the resulting confusion.
Federal reinforcementEdwin V. Sumner sent II Corps troops across the swollen Chickahominy to stabilize the Federal positionThe crossing kept the Confederate attack from isolating and destroying the corps south of the river.
Battle resultIndecisive after fighting on May 31 and June 1Confederate attacks drove back parts of IV Corps but did not break the Federal army. National Park Service battle data estimates about 5,000 United States and 6,100 Confederate casualties; the episode gives roughly 5,000 Confederate casualties. Totals vary by compilation.
Command consequenceJohnston’s serious wounds removed him from command; Davis appointed Lee on June 1Gustavus W. Smith briefly held command but was not retained. Lee inherited the army defending Richmond and immediately began preparing for the operations later known as the Seven Days Battles.
Important leader lossBrig. Gen. Robert H. Hatton was killed at Fair Oaks on May 31Hatton commanded a Tennessee brigade in Gustavus W. Smith’s wing. Johnston was seriously wounded but survived; no army commander was killed or mortally wounded in the episode’s covered operations.
Transcript normalizationsValley Campaign, McDowell, Johnston, Magruder, Huger, Drewry’s Bluff, Chickahominy, Heintzelman, and Allan PinkertonThe automated transcript renders several of these as “ballet campaign,” “MacDowell,” “Johnson,” “UJ,” “Drury,” “Chickahomony,” and other variants. The guide uses standard historical spellings.

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. Robert H. Hatton

Commander of a Tennessee brigade in Gustavus W. Smith’s wing. Killed at Fair Oaks on May 31, 1862. The National Park Service identifies Hatton as the Confederate general officer killed in the battle.

Confederacy

Gen. Joseph E. Johnston

Commander of the Confederate army defending Richmond. Seriously wounded late on May 31 by a rifle bullet and shell fragment. He survived, but the wounds removed him from command for several months and led directly to Lee’s appointment.
