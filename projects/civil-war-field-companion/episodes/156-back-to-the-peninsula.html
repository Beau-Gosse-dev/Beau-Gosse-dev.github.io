BACK TO THE PENINSULA...

Campaign recap through the indecisive Battle of Seven Pines, also called Fair Oaks. The Army of the Potomac had advanced to Richmond’s outskirts but had not captured the Confederate capital. Joseph E. Johnston’s May 31 attack failed to destroy the two Federal corps south of the flooded Chickahominy, and Johnston was seriously wounded. Jefferson Davis placed Robert E. Lee in command of the Confederate field army on June 1. The Seven Days Battles are outside this episode’s scope

People and command

United States

Commander in chiefPresident Abraham LincolnApproved the amphibious strategy with a Washington-security condition; removed McClellan as general-in-chief, withheld McDowell’s I Corps, and later redirected McDowell toward the Valley crisis
Army of the PotomacMaj. Gen. George B. McClellanDesigned and conducted the Peninsula Campaign; exercised direct field command after March 11
II CorpsMaj. Gen. Edwin V. SumnerInitially north of the Chickahominy; sent Sedgwick’s division across during the May 31 crisis, followed by Richardson’s division
III Corps south of the ChickahominyMaj. Gen. Samuel P. HeintzelmanJoseph Hooker and Philip Kearny divisions; reinforced the threatened Federal position at Seven Pines
IV Corps south of the ChickahominyBrig. Gen. Erasmus D. KeyesSilas Casey and Darius Couch divisions held the most exposed Federal positions on the Williamsburg Road and around Seven Pines
V CorpsBrig. Gen. Fitz John PorterRemained north of the Chickahominy protecting the line toward White House Landing before and during the opening of the battle
VI CorpsBrig. Gen. William B. FranklinRemained north of the Chickahominy as Johnston concentrated against the two corps south of the river
I Corps and Department of the RappahannockMaj. Gen. Irvin McDowellHis corps was retained near Washington; a later movement from Fredericksburg toward McClellan was suspended during Jackson’s Valley Campaign

Commander in chiefPresident Jefferson DavisDirected that the Yorktown line be held in April and appointed Robert E. Lee to field command on June 1 after Johnston was wounded
Confederate field army defending RichmondGen. Joseph E. JohnstonReinforced Magruder, withdrew from Yorktown toward Richmond, and ordered the May 31 attack at Seven Pines; seriously wounded late that day
Left wing under Johnston; brief acting army commanderMaj. Gen. Gustavus W. SmithHis command fought near Fair Oaks; held army command between Johnston’s wounding and Davis’s appointment of Lee
Right wing and delegated executor of the May 31 attackMaj. Gen. James LongstreetD. H. Hill and Benjamin Huger divisions operated within his wing; route and command confusion prevented the planned concentration
Division in Longstreet’s wingMaj. Gen. D. H. HillOpened the main Confederate attack along the Williamsburg Road and drove back parts of Keyes’s IV Corps
Division in Longstreet’s wingMaj. Gen. Benjamin HugerAssigned to support the right of the attack; delayed and unable to provide the expected early support
Army of the Peninsula, later a wing or division under JohnstonMaj. Gen. John B. MagruderBuilt and held the Yorktown–Warwick defensive line long enough for Johnston’s army to arrive

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. Robert H. Hatton

Commander of a Tennessee brigade in Gustavus W. Smith’s wing. Killed at Fair Oaks on May 31, 1862. The National Park Service identifies Hatton as the Confederate general officer killed in the battle.

Confederacy

Gen. Joseph E. Johnston

Commander of the Confederate army defending Richmond. Seriously wounded late on May 31 by a rifle bullet and shell fragment. He survived, but the wounds removed him from command for several months and led directly to Lee’s appointment.
