THE BURNSIDE EXPEDITION (Part the First)

United States Army and Navy forces captured Roanoke Island on February 8, 1862. Burnside landed about 7,500 soldiers south of the Confederate forts, attacked the central road redoubt, and sent brigades through the swamps around both flanks. Colonel Henry Shaw surrendered the island and about 2,675 men. The victory opened Albemarle Sound and eastern North Carolina to further United States operations

What happened

Hatteras Inlet provided the base

United States forces captured the inlet’s forts in August 1861 and retained the position as a blockading base and supply depot.

The sounds exposed coastal North Carolina

Control of Hatteras Inlet opened Pamlico Sound. Roanoke Island still blocked access to Albemarle Sound and the rivers and towns beyond it.

McClellan and Burnside planned a coastal force

Both later claimed the Roanoke Island idea. On September 12, McClellan ordered Burnside to recruit New England troops familiar with ships, surf boats, and coastal work.

The division needed its own shallow-draft transport

Burnside was told to obtain vessels able to carry troops, mount heavy guns, and operate in the bays, rivers, and shallow sounds of Virginia and North Carolina.

McClellan became general in chief

Winfield Scott retired on November 1, 1861. McClellan then commanded the Army of the Potomac and directed all United States armies.

Rush Hawkins argued for a larger operation

Hawkins, commanding at Hatteras, urged the War Department to reinforce the position and attack the North Carolina coast.

The cabinet approved the coastal campaign

Hawkins presented the case in Washington. McClellan and Navy Secretary Gideon Welles supported further operations.

The campaign had several military objectives

A coastal force could threaten Confederate troops in Virginia, disrupt the Wilmington and Weldon Railroad, seize blockade-running ports, and free naval vessels for other duties.

Lincoln expected support from North Carolina Unionists

The administration hoped occupation would help organize a loyal state government and return North Carolina to the United States.

Burnside and Goldsborough received the joint mission

Burnside commanded the army component. Louis M. Goldsborough, head of the North Atlantic Blockading Squadron, commanded the naval force.

McClellan set the objectives

Orders dated January 7 called for Roanoke Island, New Bern, and Fort Macon. A later advance might threaten Raleigh and the Wilmington and Weldon Railroad.

Roanoke Island controlled Albemarle Sound

The island lay across the water route between Pamlico and Albemarle sounds and protected the approaches to northeast North Carolina and Norfolk.

Confederate defenses developed slowly

Benjamin Huger assigned troops after the loss of Hatteras, but command disputes and limited resources delayed construction.

Henry Wise took command in December

Confederate Secretary of War Judah P. Benjamin assigned Wise to the island late in 1861.

Wise found the garrison unprepared

About 1,500 soldiers were poorly trained, armed, clothed, and housed. The island’s artillery had inadequate ammunition and weak positions.

The garrison increased but Wise became ill

The force grew to about 2,500. Colonel Henry M. Shaw of the 8th North Carolina took field command when Wise was too sick to serve.

Burnside assembled more than 15,000 men

At Annapolis, he organized regiments from five northeastern states into brigades under John G. Foster, Jesse L. Reno, and John G. Parke.

Goldsborough organized the naval force

Twenty-one warships were to support the operation after it reached Hatteras. The transports were a varied group selected mainly because they drew less than eight feet of water.

The expedition left Annapolis

The transports departed January 9, met the naval force at Fort Monroe, and left Hampton Roads on January 11.

A storm scattered and damaged the fleet

Two gunboats and a transport sank, another transport went ashore and broke apart, and Burnside’s headquarters tug Picket nearly foundered.

The Hatteras bar was shallower than reported

Burnside had planned for eight feet of water but found about six. Several vessels deliberately grounded while current and tugs cut a deeper channel beneath them.

The force assembled inside Pamlico Sound

By February 5, the surviving fleet had crossed the bar. Fog delayed the final movement until February 7.

The ships entered Croatan Sound

The expedition approached Ashby’s Harbor on the island’s west side. Thomas Robinson, a self-emancipated local teenager, had identified it as the best landing site.

The Confederates had built several defenses

Forts Huger, Blanchard, and Bartow mounted 25 guns. A three-gun redoubt blocked the central road, and the floating Fort Forrest held seven guns near the mainland.

Four regiments defended the island

The garrison included the 8th and 31st North Carolina and the 49th and 59th Virginia. Sunken vessels and pilings obstructed Croatan Sound.

The naval engagement occupied February 7

Confederate gunboats fought from behind the obstructions and then withdrew. United States ships covered the army transports moving toward Ashby’s Harbor.

Burnside began landing at 4 p.m.

Almost 4,000 men were ashore within an hour. By midnight, about 7,500 soldiers and a howitzer battery had landed south of the Confederate forts.

Shaw blocked the island road

He placed about 1,000 infantry at the central earthwork. Rain soaked both armies during the night.

The advance began at 8 a.m. on February 8

Fog, mud, felled trees, and a narrow road slowed the United States column.

The redoubt had room for only part of the garrison

Shaw could place about 400 men at the breastworks. The remainder waited roughly 250 yards behind them and could not reinforce quickly.

Foster led the direct attack

The 25th Massachusetts advanced along the road, Reno followed, and Parke remained in reserve.

The frontal advance stopped

Confederate musketry and canister forced the 25th Massachusetts to the ground. The 10th Connecticut relieved it after about two hours.

Both leading brigades entered the swamps

Foster sent the 23rd and 27th Massachusetts around the Confederate left. Reno sent three regiments around the right.

Reno’s regiments reached the right flank

United States troops emerged from waist-deep swamp, drove off the nearest defenders, and fired along the earthwork.

Foster’s men reached the other flank

The 23rd Massachusetts appeared on the Confederate left. Fire from both directions made the redoubt untenable.

The 9th New York charged up the road

Rush Hawkins sent the regiment past the 10th Connecticut after the flanking attacks had already begun to break the Confederate line.

Shaw surrendered the island

About thirty minutes after the earthwork fell, Shaw concluded that further resistance would only cause unnecessary losses.

The victory cost fewer than 300 United States casualties

The episode gives army losses as 37 killed and 214 wounded and naval losses as 3 killed and 11 wounded.

Most of the Confederate force became prisoners

The episode gives Confederate losses as 23 killed, 58 wounded, and 2,675 captured. Burnside paroled the prisoners because he could not hold them.

Burnside prepared to attack New Bern

The capture of Roanoke Island opened Albemarle Sound. On March 11, Burnside embarked about 11,000 troops for the next operation.
