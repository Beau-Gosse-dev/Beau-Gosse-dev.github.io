What happened

Grant coordinated multiple Virginia offensives

He intended to apply pressure to Confederate armies and resources at the same time rather than allow troops to move freely between theaters.

About 165,000 U.S. troops entered Virginia

Grant and Meade had 122,000 men with the Army of the Potomac; Butler, Sigel, Crook, and Averell led supporting forces elsewhere in the state.

Caution weakened the supporting operations

Several U.S. commanders accepted alarming reports without verification and did not use their numerical advantages, allowing reinforcements to move toward Lee.

Grant originally planned one large western raid

Edward O. C. Ord was to destroy the Virginia and Tennessee Railroad, combine with Crook near Saltville, and, if practical, continue to Lynchburg and the James River.

Conflict with Franz Sigel broke up the original plan

Sigel reduced Ord’s force and Ord asked for reassignment. The operation became three separate columns under Crook, Averell, and Sigel.

Crook received the main railroad assignment

His 6,000 infantry were ordered toward Dublin Depot. Averell’s 2,000 cavalry targeted lead mines and salt works, while Sigel advanced in the Shenandoah Valley.

Crook left Gauley Bridge on May 2

The column contained three infantry brigades and two artillery batteries. Rutherford B. Hayes commanded one of its regiments and strongly supported Crook.

Breckinridge’s forces covered 120 miles

About 7,500 Confederates were scattered across the region. Success depended on learning the U.S. routes early and concentrating faster than the attackers.

Breckinridge moved his strongest brigades toward the Shenandoah Valley

Lee considered Sigel the larger threat. Albert Jenkins remained responsible for the defense against Crook and Averell.

Crook turned the New River Narrows position

The U.S. column crossed Wolf Creek Mountain at Rocky Gap on May 7, placing itself on Jenkins’s flank and forcing the small Confederate force to retreat toward Dublin.

McCausland’s brigade reinforced Jenkins

The brigade had been waiting for transportation to the Shenandoah Valley at Dublin. Its retention raised Jenkins’s available force to about 3,000.

The armies prepared on opposite sides of Cloyd’s Mountain

Crook had about 6,500 effective troops at Poplar Hill. Jenkins occupied a high knoll beyond the mountain gap with fence-rail breastworks and nine guns.

Crook observed the full Confederate position

From near the summit, Crook and Carr White saw the defensive line about two miles away and watched additional Confederate infantry arrive.

Crook planned a frontal attack and a flank movement

Two brigades advanced directly across the valley while White led the third through dense woods against the Confederate right on Shuffle Ridge.

Jenkins shifted troops toward White

He first sent a small battalion and then one of his largest regiments to the threatened flank, reducing the strength available against Crook’s main assault.

Sickel and Hayes crossed the meadow

Horatio Sickel commanded the brigade on the U.S. right and Rutherford B. Hayes the one on the left. Crook accompanied the frontal attack under musket and artillery fire.

Back Creek disrupted the U.S. line

The four-foot-deep gully was not visible from the mountain. Troops lost formation in the water but used the banks for cover while officers reorganized them.

The U.S. attack captured the breastworks

Crook’s troops resumed the charge, reached the fence-rail defenses, and overwhelmed the defenders after brief hand-to-hand fighting.

Jenkins was wounded and captured

He was shot in the arm while trying to rally the line. John McCausland assumed command but could not establish another position.

The Confederate withdrawal became a rout

Crook’s four hundred cavalrymen increased the pressure. The retreat remained disorganized until the 5th Kentucky arrived near Dublin.

The 5th Kentucky covered the retreat

Only about four hundred men reached the field after a train derailment. Howard Smith’s regiment held for more than an hour against cavalry and then Hayes’s brigade.

Crook occupied Dublin at dusk

The episode counts more than one hundred U.S. dead, more than five hundred wounded, and seventy-two missing. Confederate losses included about two hundred missing.

U.S. troops burned railroad facilities on May 10

They destroyed the Dublin telegraph office, station, and supply depot before moving east to the New River railroad bridge.

Artillery fought over the New River bridge

After a two-hour exchange, Confederate ammunition ran low and soldiers from Sickel’s brigade set the four-hundred-foot wooden bridge on fire.

Crook withdrew instead of continuing the raid

McCausland retreated twelve miles toward Christiansburg, but Crook did not pursue. The U.S. column returned through heavy rain and reached Union, West Virginia, on May 14.
