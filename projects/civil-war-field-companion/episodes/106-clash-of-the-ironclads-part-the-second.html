CLASH OF THE IRONCLADS (Part the Second)

The United States Navy approved John Ericsson’s ironclad design, built USS Monitor in about 100 days, and commissioned the ship under John L. Worden. Monitor survived severe flooding and toxic fumes during its trip from New York and reached Hampton Roads on the night of March 8. It took position beside the grounded USS Minnesota before CSS Virginia returned the next morning

What happened

The episode turns to the United States ironclad

Reports of the Confederate Merrimack conversion convinced Secretary of the Navy Gideon Welles that the Hampton Roads squadron needed an armored warship.

The episode introduces Gideon Welles

Welles came from Connecticut, worked in journalism and politics, and headed the Navy’s Bureau of Provisions and Clothing during the Mexican War.

Welles joined the Republican Party

He broke with the Democratic Party over the Kansas–Nebraska Act, became a Republican candidate for governor, and helped Lincoln win the party’s 1860 presidential nomination.

Lincoln appointed Welles navy secretary

Welles entered the cabinet in March 1861 and remained secretary of the navy throughout the war.

Congress authorized three ironclads

In August 1861, Congress provided $1.5 million. Welles appointed Hiram Paulding, Joseph Smith, and Charles H. Davis to evaluate designs.

The board reviewed sixteen proposals

The board initially selected two designs, including Samuel Pook’s proposal backed by Cornelius Bushnell.

The episode introduces John Ericsson

The Swedish-born engineer had served in the Swedish army, moved to England to work on steam propulsion, and developed a screw propeller.

The Princeton accident damaged Ericsson’s relationship with the Navy

Ericsson designed the ship’s machinery, but a cannon designed by Robert Stockton exploded during an 1844 demonstration. Ericsson was blamed despite having no responsibility for the gun.

Bushnell brought Ericsson’s design to Washington

Bushnell visited Ericsson for advice on Pook’s ship, saw Ericsson’s ironclad model, and persuaded him to let Welles and Lincoln examine it.

Lincoln supported the design

Lincoln attended the Ironclad Board’s meeting and endorsed Ericsson’s proposal when naval officers questioned whether it would work.

The board approved three different ships

Ericsson’s Monitor joined Pook’s USS Galena and the larger armored frigate USS New Ironsides in the construction program.

Monitor used two connected hulls

A low armored raft formed the visible upper hull while a deeper metal hull contained the machinery and living spaces.

A rotating turret carried both guns

The 120-ton turret was 20 feet wide and 9 feet high. Eight layers of one-inch plate protected two 11-inch Dahlgren guns, and steam machinery rotated it through 360 degrees.

Special carriages controlled the recoil

Each nearly eight-ton gun fired a 166-pound solid shot. Friction carriages stopped the recoil inside the confined turret.

Monitor had a very low profile

The ship’s deck stood about 18 inches above the waterline. Its shallow draft suited coastal and river operations but left it vulnerable in ocean waves.

The contract required rapid construction

Ericsson agreed to deliver a complete ship in 90 days and refund the $275,000 price if it was not a success, although the contract did not define success.

Construction began in October

Ericsson signed the contract on October 4 and the keel was laid at Continental Iron Works on October 25. Engines and the turret were built by separate New York firms.

Ericsson named the ship Monitor

He intended the name to describe a warning or instruction to the Confederacy and to foreign governments considering support for it.

Monitor launched after 98 days

The ship entered the East River on January 30, 1862, and floated at the draft Ericsson had predicted.

John Worden received command

Welles and Ironclad Board member Joseph Smith selected the 43-year-old lieutenant, a 27-year Navy veteran who had recently returned from Confederate imprisonment.

Worden received orders for Hampton Roads

After provisioning and trials at Brooklyn Navy Yard, Worden was ordered south on March 4. Weather delayed the departure for two days.

Monitor left New York on March 6

The tug Seth Low towed the ironclad, and the gunboats Sachem and Currituck escorted it down the coast.

A gale exposed serious leaks

On March 7, water entered around the turret base and through ventilation ducts. Wet drive belts stopped the ventilation fans.

Toxic gases filled the engine room

With the fans stopped, carbon monoxide and other fumes accumulated. Engineers abandoned the machinery space, and the ship lost pumping capacity.

The tow saved the ship

Worden signaled for help. Seth Low pulled Monitor toward calmer water, where engineers ventilated the ship, restarted the machinery, and pumped it dry.

A steering failure created a second emergency

Loose tiller ropes left Monitor broadside to the waves. The crew repaired the steering in about half an hour.

Monitor entered Chesapeake Bay on March 8

The crew sighted Cape Charles around noon, passed Cape Henry, and then saw flashes and heard the fighting in Hampton Roads.

A pilot reported Virginia’s attack

A harbor pilot told the crew that CSS Virginia had destroyed or disabled ships in the blockading squadron.

Monitor anchored near Roanoke

Monitor reached Hampton Roads around 9 p.m. while Congress was still burning. Congress’s magazine exploded after midnight.

Monitor took position beside Minnesota

Worden was ordered to protect the grounded frigate. The exhausted crew prepared for Virginia to return on the morning of March 9.
