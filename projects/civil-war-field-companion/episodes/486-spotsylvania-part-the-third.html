Episode 486 · May 11–18, 1864 · Mule Shoe salient and Bloody Angle, Spotsylvania Court House, Virginia

SPOTSYLVANIA (Part the Third)

Hancock’s assault captured the northern half of the Mule Shoe, 20 guns, and about 3,000 prisoners. Confederate counterattacks stopped the advance while Lee’s army built a new line across the base of the salient and withdrew to it early on May 13. Later U.S. attacks did not break that new line

What happened

Grant evaluated the attacks of May 10

He believed Emory Upton’s temporary breakthrough could have produced a larger victory if it had received immediate support.

Lee reported that his line had been restored

The Confederate commander described the day’s attacks as repulsed except for Upton’s temporary success at the Mule Shoe.

Lee concentrated on the Mule Shoe

He brought Heth’s division back toward Spotsylvania Court House and told Richard Ewell to prepare for another attack on the salient.

Grant selected the Mule Shoe for a larger assault

Reconnaissance showed the salient’s full outline. Grant and Meade concluded that a heavy attack against its apex could repeat and enlarge Upton’s breakthrough.

Hancock received the main assignment

II Corps was ordered to move at night and attack at 4 a.m. with Burnside’s IX Corps supporting from the east. Warren and Wright were told to be ready to exploit success.

Barlow’s division became the lead formation

Francis Barlow would spearhead the attack, David Birney and Gershom Mott would advance on his right, and John Gibbon would follow in reserve.

Barlow reached the Brown house after midnight

His only usable information about the ground came from a rough sketch drawn on a wall by Lieutenant Colonel Waldo Merryman, who had participated in the May 10 attack.

Fog delayed the start by 45 minutes

Rain had soaked the troops and fields during the night. Hancock waited for enough light, then ordered the advance at 4:45 a.m.

About 19,000 U.S. soldiers advanced

Barlow used a dense, stacked formation based on Upton’s method. Mott and Birney moved in conventional lines on the right, with Gibbon behind them.

Lee had removed most artillery from the salient

Incorrect reports suggested Grant was moving toward Fredericksburg. Orders to return the guns arrived too late, and batteries were only beginning to reenter the works when the attack struck.

The attack overran the Confederate pickets

Fog and poor readiness allowed Hancock’s troops to reach the works after only brief resistance.

Hancock captured the northern Mule Shoe

Within minutes, U.S. troops took 20 guns and thousands of prisoners. Edward Johnson and brigade commander George H. Steuart were captured, and Johnson’s division ceased to function as an organized force.

The U.S. advance lost organization

Units became mixed while taking prisoners and crossing the works. Officers could not immediately form the mass into a coordinated force for an advance through the woods.

John B. Gordon organized a counterattack

Gordon first sent Robert D. Johnston’s small brigade forward to gain time, then assembled more troops from Ewell’s reserve division.

Lee tried to lead the counterattack

Gordon and his soldiers stopped Lee from advancing with the line. Gordon then sent three brigades against the disorganized U.S. force.

The counterattack recovered most of the salient

Gordon’s troops pushed Hancock back toward the northern tip. Additional brigades arrived while Lee moved reinforcements into the fight.

Wright’s VI Corps entered the battle

Hancock reported that all his troops were committed and asked for immediate help. VI Corps attacked but neither side could dislodge the other from the Bloody Angle.

The battle reached a stalemate by 7:30 a.m.

Hancock had captured about 3,000 prisoners and 20 guns, but his advance was stopped. Wright was also stalled and Burnside had made little progress.

Meade ordered Warren to attack Laurel Hill

Grant believed Lee must have weakened other parts of his line. Warren argued that the position remained fully defended and delayed while seeking artillery preparation.

V Corps attacked and was repulsed

Warren’s troops crossed open ground under artillery and musket fire. A single entrenched Confederate division retained Laurel Hill.

Nearly the entire U.S. army was engaged

Reinforcements crowded into the Mule Shoe, sometimes 20 ranks deep, but their numbers could not be used effectively in the confined ground.

Lee ordered a new line across the salient’s base

Confederate troops held the Bloody Angle to give engineers and infantry time to complete a shorter defensive line behind them.

Close-range fighting continued at the Bloody Angle

Soldiers fired and stabbed across the log parapet in rain and mud. The intensity rose and fell, but combat continued through the afternoon and night.

Confederates withdrew early on May 13

After the new line was complete, the defenders pulled back around 3 a.m. U.S. losses exceeded 6,000; the episode counts at least 4,000 Confederates killed or wounded and another 4,000 captured.

Rain stopped Grant’s attempted flank movement

Warren and Wright shifted toward the Confederate right on the night of May 13, but mud slowed the march and Lee adjusted his line before they could attack effectively.

Grant ordered another Mule Shoe attack

Six U.S. divisions returned to the area for a May 18 assault. Delays gave the Confederates warning, and artillery stopped the attack before it reached the new works.

Defeats elsewhere changed Grant’s calculation

News that Sigel was retreating and Butler had withdrawn meant Confederate reinforcements could reach Lee. Grant began preparing another movement around Lee’s flank.
