Episode 479 · Spring 1864 · Virginia theater: the Rapidan River, the Wilderness, Richmond, Bermuda Hundred, and the Shenandoah Valley

THE WILDERNESS (Part the First)

Grant made Lee’s army the main U.S. objective in Virginia and paired the Army of the Potomac’s movement around Lee’s right with supporting offensives by Benjamin Butler and Franz Sigel. Meade’s staff planned to cross east of Lee and move through the Wilderness in one day before the Confederates could interfere. The Army of the Potomac and IX Corps fielded more than 116,000 men, compared with fewer than 64,000 in Lee’s army, but the U.S. force contained many inexperienced soldiers and short-term veterans. What happened

Grant planned simultaneous offensives

The 1864 strategy called for pressure in several theaters so Confederate armies could not reinforce one another.

Lee’s army was the main eastern objective

Political and military considerations required the principal U.S. effort in Virginia to target the Army of Northern Virginia.

Virginia’s geography restricted movement

Chesapeake Bay, the mountains, and west-to-east rivers limited north–south routes and repeatedly favored defenders.

U.S. sea power created another approach

Federal control of the coast made it possible to land and supply troops southeast of Richmond instead of crossing every Virginia river overland.

Grant assigned forces in southeastern Virginia

About 21,500 troops already defended Hampton Roads, and Grant added roughly 10,000 men from X Corps for offensive use.

Grant met Benjamin Butler

At Fort Monroe, Butler proposed moving by water up the James River and landing at Bermuda Hundred.

Bermuda Hundred threatened two cities

The peninsula lay near the Richmond–Petersburg railroad, about 16 miles from Richmond and eight miles from Petersburg.

Grant considered the main Rapidan movement

Lee’s army camped between Gordonsville and Orange Court House, covered the Rapidan fords, and protected important rail and Shenandoah Valley connections.

Grant rejected a frontal river attack

The fortified Rapidan crossings were too strong. A movement around Lee’s western flank would also create serious supply problems.

Grant chose Lee’s eastern flank

Crossing below Lee would be easier to supply and would keep open the possibility of cooperation with Butler.

Meade retained operational command

Grant accompanied the Army of the Potomac but left its day-to-day management to George Meade.

Andrew Humphreys designed the movement

Meade’s chief of staff planned the detailed turn around Lee’s right.

The Wilderness was the main obstacle

Roughly 70 square miles of dense second-growth vegetation and few roads made organized movement difficult and favored defense.

The plan required a one-day passage

The Army of the Potomac was to cross the Rapidan and clear the Wilderness before Lee could intervene.

Humphreys planned to turn Mine Run

A wide movement toward the stream’s headwaters would make Lee’s fortified Mine Run position unusable and force him to retreat or fight in open country.

Grant added the Shenandoah offensive

Franz Sigel’s forces would move up the valley while other columns attacked the Virginia and Tennessee Railroad, lead mines, and saltworks.

The presidential election affected strategy

A major U.S. defeat could increase support for a negotiated peace, while battlefield success could strengthen Lincoln’s reelection.

Longstreet proposed a political offensive

He suggested a Confederate movement into Kentucky to damage Northern morale and Lincoln’s electoral prospects, but practical objections ended the plan.

Lee faced desertion and shortages

Absences reduced Confederate strength, while inadequate food and forage threatened the army’s ability to respond quickly.

Lee operated his own intelligence system

Reports came through Stuart, Richmond officials, scouts, civilians, and newspapers; Lee had no formal chief of intelligence.

Lee anticipated the supporting offensives

By April 30, he expected operations in the Shenandoah Valley, against southwestern railroads, and from southeastern Virginia.

Lee underestimated Meade’s strength

He estimated 75,000 U.S. troops. The Army of the Potomac already exceeded 100,000 and Burnside would add more than 16,000.

Lee fielded three infantry corps

Longstreet had about 10,000 men, Ewell about 17,200, and A. P. Hill just over 22,300, plus Stuart’s cavalry.

Meade also fielded three infantry corps

Sedgwick’s VI Corps had about 24,000, Hancock’s II Corps about 26,600, and Warren’s V Corps about 24,100.

Sheridan commanded about 15,800 cavalry

Grant brought him east from infantry command in the Western Theater to lead the Army of the Potomac’s three cavalry divisions.

Burnside remained administratively separate

IX Corps had about 19,200 men and reported directly to Grant because Burnside outranked Meade.

The U.S. numerical advantage had limits

Up to one-third of Meade’s soldiers lacked combat experience, and some veteran enlistments were close to expiring.

About 27,000 veterans reenlisted

Federal bonuses and furloughs retained many experienced soldiers, but thousands of others were due to leave during the campaign.

The U.S. army still had about 70,000 veterans

The Army of the Potomac had roughly 65,000 experienced troops; IX Corps veterans raised the combined total slightly above 70,000.

The Army of the Potomac was well supplied

Morale and material condition were strong, with adequate clothing, equipment, food, and an effective logistical system.
