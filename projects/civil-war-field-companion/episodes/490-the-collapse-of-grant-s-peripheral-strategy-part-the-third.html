What happened

The episode returns to Grant’s peripheral strategy

Earlier operations had ended in defeat at New Market and the mortal wounding of J. E. B. Stuart at Yellow Tavern. Butler’s Army of the James still threatened Richmond from the south.

Butler had a large force close to Richmond

Union control of the lower James allowed roughly 36,000 troops to land at Bermuda Hundred, within twenty miles of a lightly defended Confederate capital.

Richmond depended on rapid reinforcement

Jefferson Davis kept most available troops in field armies. The Department of Richmond had only a little more than 7,100 soldiers on April 20 and expected militia and outside troops to meet an emergency.

The James River gave the expedition direct access

The river was navigable from Hampton Roads to Richmond. Confederate defenses began where the channel narrowed above Bermuda Hundred.

Mines, Fort Darling, and warships protected the upper river

Confederates placed electrically detonated mines in the bends, mounted heavy guns on the ninety-foot bluff at Fort Darling, and held naval forces farther upstream.

Grant expected professional corps commanders to advise Butler

William F. Smith commanded XVIII Corps and Quincy Gillmore commanded X Corps. Grant expected their combat experience to compensate for Butler’s limited field experience.

Bermuda Hundred placed three targets within reach

Richmond was about fifteen miles north, Petersburg eight miles south, and the Richmond and Petersburg Railroad less than five miles west.

Butler fortified his base first

After landing on May 5, he spent May 6 strengthening the five-mile neck between the James and Appomattox rivers and then probed toward the railroad.

Grant’s orders emphasized Richmond but left the method open

Butler was told to secure ground along the south side of the James and treat Richmond as the objective. He worried that moving north would expose his base to troops arriving from the Carolinas.

The May 9 movement reached Swift Creek

Nine U.S. brigades advanced to within about two miles of Petersburg, skirmished across the creek, and damaged the Richmond and Petersburg Railroad without forcing a general battle.

Smith and Gillmore proposed a different operation

They recommended returning to Bermuda Hundred, bridging the Appomattox, and attacking Petersburg’s southern rail links. Sending the proposal in writing deepened Butler’s distrust of both commanders.

False reports redirected Butler toward Richmond

War Department messages incorrectly said Lee was retreating and Grant was coming to join Butler. Butler canceled the renewed Petersburg operation and prepared to advance north.

The advance toward Richmond began May 12

Butler intended to demonstrate against the southern defenses and wait for Grant rather than storm Richmond. By then Confederate reinforcements had made a direct attack impractical.

Pickett initially had only 750 troops near Petersburg

George Pickett reported the Union landing and tried to organize a defense while Confederate authorities resolved unclear departmental responsibilities.

Confederate strength increased rapidly

The available force grew from about 1,350 on May 7 to 7,500 on May 10. Robert Ransom also led a 5,000-man field force from the Richmond defenses.

Beauregard took command and planned a counterattack

He reached Petersburg on May 10 and judged that Butler could be pushed back into the Bermuda Hundred peninsula.

Kautz disrupted Confederate reinforcement routes

A 3,000-man U.S. cavalry raid burned two bridges on the Weldon and Petersburg Railroad, forcing troops from the Carolinas to march across a ten-mile break in the line.

Butler captured the outer works on May 13

U.S. troops gained part of the exterior Drewry’s Bluff defenses, then stopped and consolidated instead of continuing against the main position.

Beauregard had about 20,000 troops at Fort Darling

He believed this force could drive Butler back but asked for 15,000 troops from Lee to make the destruction of the Army of the James more likely.

Davis rejected Beauregard’s request for Lee’s troops

Braxton Bragg argued that Lee could not safely retreat sixty miles while Grant pursued him and that Beauregard already had enough troops to attack Butler.

The Confederate plan used three converging forces

Ransom was to break the U.S. right and cut it off from the James; Hoke was to pin the rest of the line; W. H. C. Whiting was to advance north from Petersburg into the U.S. rear.

Ransom attacked before sunrise on May 16

Fog concealed the approach and helped the initial breakthrough, but it also disorganized the attackers. Confederate troops captured prisoners and artillery without completing the flank movement.

Hoke’s attack stalled and Whiting did not arrive

Hoke gained some ground before meeting stronger resistance. Whiting allowed a small blocking force to stop his column south of the battlefield, so it never joined the main attack.

Butler ordered Gillmore to attack the weakened Confederate right

The heaviest Confederate pressure fell on Smith’s XVIII Corps. Butler correctly concluded that fewer troops opposed X Corps, but Gillmore did not make the requested counterattack.

Both U.S. corps withdrew about a mile

Smith ordered his formations back, Gillmore followed, and the Army of the James formed a new line by early afternoon. Disorganized Confederate units did not immediately pursue.

Butler returned to Bermuda Hundred

With both corps commanders unwilling to renew the battle and reports of Whiting approaching from Petersburg, Butler ordered a general withdrawal at about 3 p.m.

The Confederate line contained Butler’s army

Beauregard entrenched across the peninsula after the withdrawal. He then sent about 7,000 troops to reinforce Lee and promised additional men.

Grant’s five supporting operations had ended

By May 18 the offensives led by Averell, Sigel, Butler, Crook, and Sheridan were no longer holding Confederate troops away from Lee.

Grant learned of the defeats during the Spotsylvania fighting

A dispatch from Henry Halleck reported Sigel’s retreat and Butler’s withdrawal. Grant expected Lee to receive troops from Beauregard and the Shenandoah Valley.

Grant ordered another movement around Lee’s right

He decided the Spotsylvania position offered no further advantage and directed the Army of the Potomac to begin moving toward the North Anna the next day.
