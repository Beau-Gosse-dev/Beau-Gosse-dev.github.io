What happened

The Army of Tennessee withdrew from the Shoup Line

Johnston moved the army behind Peachtree Creek after U.S. forces crossed the Chattahoochee. The new line covered Atlanta from the north.

Sherman planned a general right wheel

Sherman chose to move around the north and east of Atlanta rather than attack the city defenses directly.

Each U.S. army received a route

McPherson was to move toward Stone Mountain, Schofield toward Decatur, and Thomas through Buckhead toward Peachtree Creek.

U.S. forces prepared three bridgeheads

For six days the armies built bridges, moved supplies to Vinings Station, and consolidated crossings at Roswell, Soap Creek, and farther downstream.

Confederate troops completed an orderly withdrawal

Arthur Manigault described muffled artillery wheels, pontoon bridges covered with corn stalks, and a withdrawal without loss of men or equipment.

Atlanta residents began leaving the city

Wagons and trains carried civilians away as the U.S. armies approached.

Johnston recommended evacuating Andersonville prisoners

Johnston feared a U.S. cavalry raid against the prison camp. Davis interpreted the message as evidence that Johnston might abandon Atlanta.

Davis sent Braxton Bragg to Atlanta

Bragg was ordered to determine Johnston’s intentions. His meetings with Johnston were unproductive, while Hood repeated criticism of Johnston’s command.

Davis demanded a specific operational plan

On July 16, Johnston replied that his actions had to depend on Sherman because the U.S. force was about twice his size.

Davis decided to replace Johnston

Davis rejected William J. Hardee as too cautious and selected John B. Hood.

Sherman began the advance on July 17

All three U.S. armies moved from their Chattahoochee bridgeheads toward Atlanta and the Georgia Railroad.

The relief order reached Johnston

At 10 p.m., Samuel Cooper’s telegram ordered Johnston to turn the Army and Department of Tennessee over to Hood.

Many Confederate soldiers opposed the change

Accounts from Arthur Manigault, Albert Willard, and Sam Watkins described anger at Johnston’s removal and confidence that he had preserved the army.

U.S. commanders assessed Hood

Oliver O. Howard called Hood a hard fighter, and John M. Schofield expected him to attack.
