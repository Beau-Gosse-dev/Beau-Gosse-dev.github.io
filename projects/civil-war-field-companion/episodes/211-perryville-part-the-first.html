PERRYVILLE (Part the First)

The initiative passed to the United States. Braxton Bragg paused at Bardstown, failed to obtain the large body of Kentucky recruits he expected, and did not promptly unite with Edmund Kirby Smith. Don Carlos Buell retained command, reorganized the Army of the Ohio into three corps, and marched from Louisville on October 1 with about 55,000 soldiers in three main columns while a fourth column feinted toward Frankfort and Lexington. No battle occurs within this episode; it ends with the armies moving toward the Perryville campaign’s decisive phase
Episode scopeThe strategic and command crisis before PerryvilleThe episode covers the eleven days between Bragg’s departure from Munfordville and Buell’s October 1 advance from Louisville. The first shots near Perryville belong to the next episode.
Bragg’s new baseBardstown, roughly forty miles southeast of LouisvilleThe Army of the Mississippi marched northeast after abandoning Munfordville. Bardstown provided supplies and a friendly reception but did not let Bragg directly threaten Louisville.
Confederate conditionHeat, drought, hunger, and worn-out shoesMany soldiers reportedly marched barefoot. Supplies from Kirby Smith helped restore Bragg’s army after the logistical crisis that forced it away from Munfordville.
A missed opportunityBragg gave up the position between Buell and LouisvilleConfederate soldiers remained eager to fight, but Bragg could not stay at Munfordville without risking starvation. Buell therefore regained an open road to his supply base.
Bardstown receptionA warm welcome did not become mass enlistmentThe army paraded past Bragg, Simon Bolivar Buckner, Leonidas Polk, and Patton Anderson, but enthusiasm in town did not solve the campaign’s central political problem.
Recruiting expectation15,000 rifles brought for prospective Kentucky volunteersBragg expected an uprising of Confederate supporters. The podcast reports only about 1,500 enlistments at Camp Breckinridge during this stage and about 5,000 Kentuckians joining Confederate service during the campaign, most in cavalry.
Why recruits hesitatedConfederate control did not appear permanentA large United States force remained at Louisville, while federal confiscation policies put the property of men entering Confederate service at risk.
Bragg’s September 25 report“Fifteen thousand stand of arms and no one to use them”His dispatch to Richmond expressed sharp disappointment with Kentucky and warned that the campaign could not be sustained without major local support.
A new chief of staffCol. George W. BrentBragg had been acting as both army commander and chief of staff. He appointed Brent in late September to reduce the burden at headquarters.
Confederate strength and separationAbout 30,000 with Bragg; about 18,000 with Kirby SmithThe figures are those stated in the episode. Bragg needed Smith to approach parity with Buell, but Smith remained around Lexington and the two armies were vulnerable to defeat separately.
Strategic pauseBragg went on the defensive at BardstownHe assumed Buell would need weeks to rebuild at Louisville. That pause surrendered the operational initiative when the Army of the Ohio recovered in less than one week.
Buell’s returning veteransA 300-mile withdrawal ended at LouisvilleThe Army of the Ohio arrived September 25–28 after retreating from the approaches to Chattanooga without a major battle. It briefly lost cohesion as exhausted soldiers refitted, received pay, or left without permission.
Attempted command changeGeorge H. Thomas asked that Buell remainGeneral-in-Chief Henry W. Halleck ordered Buell relieved, subject to a condition if he was preparing to fight. Thomas considered himself insufficiently informed to assume responsibility and asked that Buell be retained; Halleck suspended the change.
Army of the OhioAbout 75,000 soldiers after reinforcementMany were new recruits. Buell generally distributed green regiments among veteran brigades, although parts of the Eleventh Division and all of the Tenth Division remained inexperienced.
Death at the Galt HouseMaj. Gen. William “Bull” Nelson killed September 29After Nelson slapped Brig. Gen. Jefferson C. Davis during a confrontation in the hotel, Davis obtained a pistol and shot him. Nelson died about an hour later, and Davis was arrested.
New corps structureThree corps of roughly 25,000 soldiers eachAlexander McCook received First Corps, Thomas L. Crittenden Second Corps, and acting Maj. Gen. Charles C. Gilbert Third Corps. George Thomas became second in command of the army.
Command inexperienceSeveral leaders were new to their postsGilbert’s promotion from Regular Army captain was highly unusual. Philip Sheridan had not previously led a division, and James Jackson’s Tenth Division consisted entirely of green units and commanders.
Buell’s objectiveThreaten the Confederate route back to TennesseeA southeastward advance toward Bardstown and central Kentucky would endanger Camp Breckinridge and the Wilderness Road, forcing Bragg and Smith to defend their communications or abandon Frankfort and Lexington.
Four-column advanceAbout 20,000 toward Frankfort; about 55,000 toward BardstownJoshua Sill’s division and Ebenezer Dumont’s recruits formed the eastern feint. Buell’s remaining three corps columns began the principal advance on October 1.
Confederate political projectRichard Hawes installation scheduled for October 4Bragg went to Lexington, assumed formal command over the Confederate forces in Kentucky, and planned a Frankfort ceremony to install the pro-Confederate claimant as governor.
Senior leader casualties mentionedWilliam Nelson killed; Robert L. McCook mortally wounded earlierNelson’s death directly disrupted Buell’s corps organization. The episode also notes that Alexander McCook’s brother Robert had been mortally wounded by Confederate guerrillas in Tennessee in August.

What happened

The episode returns to central Kentucky

After reviewing George Morgan’s escape from Cumberland Gap, the narrative shifts about 200 miles west to Bragg and Buell.

Bragg marches for Bardstown

After leaving Munfordville on September 20, the Army of the Mississippi moved northeast to resupply and seek a junction with Kirby Smith.

Confederate soldiers remain eager to fight

Lt. John Inglis of the Third Florida recorded frustration over failing to stop Buell but said the soldiers still wanted battle.

Bardstown welcomes Bragg’s army

The reception resembled Kirby Smith’s welcome at Lexington, and Bragg reviewed the troops from the courthouse steps with senior generals.

Bragg restores his force

The army covered the roads into Bardstown, received supplies from Kirby Smith, and reincorporated Patrick Cleburne’s division.

Kentucky recruits do not appear

The expected rush of volunteers failed to materialize despite the 15,000 rifles Bragg had brought for them.

Buckner opens recruiting at Camp Breckinridge

Simon Bolivar Buckner was sent to the renamed Camp Dick Robinson, but the episode reports only about 1,500 enlistments there.

Kirby Smith describes Kentucky’s reluctance

Smith complained that Confederate sympathies did not outweigh Kentuckians’ concern for their productive land and livestock.

Bragg reports his disappointment

His September 25 dispatch to Richmond said the army had 15,000 weapons without men to carry them and might have to abandon Kentucky.

Bragg appoints a chief of staff

The strain of exercising both army command and staff supervision led him to appoint George W. Brent in late September.

Bardstown offers few strategic advantages

Bragg could block a southeastward move from Louisville but was too distant to threaten the city or prevent Buell’s buildup.

The Confederate armies remain separated

Bragg and Kirby Smith controlled much of central and eastern Kentucky but had not coordinated their forces, leaving them open to defeat in detail.

Bragg pauses the campaign

Expecting a long United States refit, Bragg consolidated his gains and passed the initiative to Buell.

Buell’s veterans reach Louisville

The Army of the Ohio arrived September 25–28 after withdrawing about 300 miles from the Chattanooga approaches.

The army briefly loses cohesion

Exhausted soldiers refitted, visited the city, or went home without leave, but the force had recovered by September 30.

Orders arrive to replace Buell

General-in-Chief Henry Halleck sent orders naming George Thomas as the new commander, with an exception if Buell was preparing to fight.

The relief order reaches Louisville

On September 29 Buell was already reorganizing and preparing an offensive, yet he immediately began transferring command.

Thomas asks that Buell remain

Thomas told Halleck that he lacked the information needed to assume responsibility; Halleck suspended the relief order.

Reinforcements swell the army

About 75,000 men were present, but the large number of newly enlisted soldiers threatened marching and fighting efficiency.

Buell loses an experienced subordinate

On the same day he survived the attempted command change, Maj. Gen. William “Bull” Nelson was killed in Louisville.

Nelson arrests Jefferson C. Davis

Their dispute over Nelson’s contempt for Indiana troops led Nelson to order Davis to Cincinnati on September 25; Horatio Wright released him.

The generals meet at the Galt House

Davis, Indiana governor Oliver P. Morton, and their party encountered Nelson in the Louisville hotel lobby on September 29.

Davis shoots Nelson

After Nelson slapped him and walked away, Davis obtained a pistol, followed him upstairs, and shot him in the chest. Nelson died about an hour later.

Buell announces a corps organization

The Army of the Ohio was divided into First, Second, and Third Corps, each containing roughly 25,000 infantry, artillery, and cavalry.

McCook receives First Corps

Alexander McCook commanded the divisions of Joshua Sill, Lovell Rousseau, and James Jackson.

Crittenden receives Second Corps

Thomas L. Crittenden commanded divisions under William Sooy Smith, Horatio Van Cleve, and Thomas J. Wood.

Nelson’s death leaves Third Corps open

Buell selected acting Maj. Gen. Charles C. Gilbert over division commander Albin F. Schoepf.

Third Corps contains three divisions

Its divisions were commanded by Schoepf, Robert B. Mitchell, and Philip H. Sheridan.

Green troops suffer during drill

A prolonged review under James Jackson and William Terrill produced numerous heat casualties and four deaths.

Buell chooses the offensive

Remaining at Louisville would allow the Confederates to consolidate; Buell instead prepared to drive into central Kentucky.

The Wilderness Road becomes the objective

A move toward Bardstown and Camp Breckinridge threatened the Confederate supply and retreat route to Tennessee.

Buell forms four columns

Sill and Dumont took about 20,000 men toward Frankfort as a feint while roughly 55,000 marched in three columns toward Bardstown.

Bragg leaves Polk at Bardstown

Bragg rode to Lexington, met Kirby Smith, and assumed formal command of all Confederate forces in Kentucky.

A Confederate government ceremony is scheduled

Richard Hawes agreed to be installed at Frankfort on October 4 as the Confederacy’s claimant to Kentucky’s governorship.

The Army of the Ohio marches

Buell began his advance on October 1, less than one week after the first veterans entered Louisville.

The episode recommends Marshall

Creating a Confederate Kentucky explains how postwar memory recast a state that had remained in the Union.
