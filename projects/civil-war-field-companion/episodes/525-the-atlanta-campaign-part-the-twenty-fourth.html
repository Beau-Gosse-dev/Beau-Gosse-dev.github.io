What happened

Sherman reported the failed attacks

At 8 p.m., Sherman told Henry Halleck that the assaults by McPherson and Thomas had reached but not carried the Confederate works.

Initial casualty estimates were too low

Sherman first reported about 2,500 U.S. casualties. The total was about 3,000, compared with roughly 700 Confederate casualties.

Confederate fortifications determined the result

U.S. troops inflicted limited losses because the defenders remained behind strong breastworks.

Sherman considered two difficult alternatives

Another assault would cost more men, while moving around the Confederate line required leaving the railroad supply route.

Field telegraph connected the U.S. command

From Signal Hill, Sherman exchanged reports with Thomas, McPherson, Schofield, and other officers across the battlefield.

Thomas reported every attacking brigade stopped

At about 11 a.m., Thomas said his troops were exhausted and that Charles Harker and Daniel McCook had been severely wounded.

Sherman asked Thomas to reassess

Sherman wanted another attack only if Thomas believed it could succeed, partly to support McPherson and divert attention from Schofield.

Thomas rejected another direct assault

Jefferson C. Davis and Oliver O. Howard reported that the works and abatis were too strong. Thomas recommended saps and artillery instead.

Thomas warned that more assaults could destroy the army

He told Sherman that the works could be taken only with immense losses, if at all, and that the army had gained no material advantage.

Sherman accepted Thomas’s judgment

Sherman did not order another attack and deferred to the commanders who had examined the front.

Schofield reported progress on the southern flank

Jacob Cox crossed Olley’s Creek, drove back Confederate cavalry, and moved along the Sandtown Road toward Nickajack Creek.

Cox moved beyond Hood’s left

Schofield stopped the division before it became isolated, but its position already threatened the Confederate flank.

Sherman called Cox’s movement the day’s only advantage

He ordered Schofield to hold the ground and Thomas to secure the positions gained by the assaulting troops.

Sherman compared losses and considered the next move

He regretted the loss of Harker and McCook but argued that assaults were sometimes necessary. He then focused on the opening created south of Kennesaw.

Sherman proposed leaving the railroad

That evening he asked Thomas about shifting around the Confederate left and directed him to begin preparations.

The strategy changed from assault to maneuver

Sherman decided to reinforce Schofield, move around Johnston’s left, and force the Confederates either to withdraw or fight outside their works.
