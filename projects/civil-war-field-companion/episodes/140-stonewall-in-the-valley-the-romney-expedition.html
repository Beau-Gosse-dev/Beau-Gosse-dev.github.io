STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: THE ROMNEY EXPEDITION

Temporary Confederate operational success. Jackson drove the United States detachments out of Bath and induced the evacuation of Romney, but did not capture Hancock. His occupation disrupted the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad and Chesapeake and Ohio Canal corridor only temporarily. The decision to leave Loring at Romney produced a command crisis; Richmond ordered Loring back to Winchester, and United States troops reoccupied Romney on February 7
Core operationJanuary 1–24, 1862Jackson left Winchester on January 1, occupied Bath on January 4 and Romney on January 14, then departed Romney for Winchester on January 23 and arrived the next day.
Why Romney matteredA western approach to Winchester and a junction in the South Branch Potomac ValleyThe Northwestern Turnpike connected Romney to Winchester. Other roads linked the town to the Staunton–Parkersburg route, while nearby Federal positions covered repaired sections of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad.
Jackson’s force on taking commandAbout 1,650 militia, 485 cavalry, and two artillery piecesThese are the episode’s figures for early November 1861. The militia was dispersed, short of ammunition, and often armed with converted flintlocks.
Federal pressure described in the episodeBanks: 18,000; Rosecrans: more than 22,000; Kelley at Romney: 5,000These figures describe separate regional commands, not a single force concentrated against Jackson. They are the estimates used by the episode.
ReinforcementsStonewall Brigade and three brigades from Loring’s Army of the NorthwestLoring retained immediate command of brigades led by Samuel R. Anderson, William Gilham, and William B. Taliaferro. Jackson’s former brigade was commanded by Richard B. Garnett.
Strength before the marchEpisode count: 7,500 regular Confederate troops, 2,200 militia, and 650 cavalryThe episode totals the assembled categories at about 10,350. Other accounts commonly describe roughly 9,000 men actually marching and about 11,000 in the wider command. The categories and effective strengths varied by date.
Dam No. 5 operationDecember 17–21, 1861; damage repaired within two daysJackson tried to lower the Chesapeake and Ohio Canal by breaching the Potomac dam. His men worked at night under intermittent fire. The Official Records date the operation December 17–21; the episode says the column left before dawn on December 16.
March began6 a.m., January 1, 1862The weather began mild, then turned to wind, sleet, snow, and falling temperatures. Men who had discarded or packed blankets and overcoats could not recover them because wagons lagged behind.
Route choiceWinchester to Bath by rough roads, not directly to RomneyJackson used an indirect route to conceal his objective and to remove the Federal post at Bath from the flank of a later move toward Romney.
Bath garrisonAbout 1,400 United States troops in the episodeJackson hoped to trap the garrison, cross the Potomac, destroy supplies at Hancock, and cut the telegraph. Delays and failed Confederate movements allowed the troops to escape west or across the river.
Bath occupiedJanuary 4, 1862Gilham’s brigade did not press into town on January 3, and Loring countermanded Jackson’s renewed instruction. On January 4 Confederate cavalry and Jackson entered after the Federal infantry had withdrawn.
Hancock engagementJanuary 5–6; indecisiveJackson demanded surrender, bombarded the town from the Virginia side, and explored a crossing. Frederick W. Lander refused. Jackson abandoned the effort after the garrison was reinforced and moved toward Romney.
Extreme-weather claimThe episode reports temperatures near −20°F on the return to Unger’s StoreThe exact temperature is difficult to establish. What is not in doubt is the severe ice, snow, frostbite, sickness, and loss of mobility among men, horses, wagons, and artillery.
Romney evacuatedJanuary 10; occupied by Jackson’s force on January 14Lander withdrew the United States garrison before Jackson arrived. Confederate cavalry entered first; the Stonewall Brigade arrived on January 14, and parts of Loring’s command required up to two additional days.
Episode casualty totalConfederacy: 4 killed and 28 woundedJackson reported these totals for the expedition. No general or other senior commander was killed or mortally wounded in the operations covered by the episode.
Winter dispositionsLoring at Romney; militia at Bath and Martinsburg; Jackson and the Stonewall Brigade at WinchesterJackson intended these separated posts to cover the frontier while keeping his most trusted brigade centrally placed as a reserve.
Romney petitionSigned by 11 brigade and regimental officers on January 25The petition described exposure, sickness, inadequate food and shelter, and the position at Romney as unfavorable. Loring endorsed it; Taliaferro carried a copy directly to Jefferson Davis while on leave.
Richmond intervenesLoring ordered back to WinchesterSecretary of War Judah P. Benjamin transmitted the order without first resolving it through Jackson and Joseph E. Johnston. Jackson complied, then requested transfer to VMI or acceptance of his resignation.
Resolution of the command crisisJackson withdrew his resignation on February 6; charges against Loring did not proceedGovernor John Letcher and Congressman Alexander R. Boteler helped mediate. Jackson then charged Loring with neglect and conduct subversive of discipline. Loring was promoted and transferred rather than court-martialed.

What happened

Jackson assumes the Valley mission

The narrative resumes on November 5, 1861, as Jackson arrives at Winchester to command the Valley District and protect the Shenandoah Valley.

The valley’s strategic value

The lower valley pointed toward the Potomac and Washington, while its farms supplied the Confederacy and its mountain passes opened routes between the valley and Piedmont Virginia.

Jackson finds the district exposed

Banks held western Maryland, Rosecrans operated in western Virginia, and Kelley occupied Romney. Jackson had dispersed militia, a small cavalry force, and only two guns.

The Stonewall Brigade is sent west

Secretary of War Judah P. Benjamin assigned Jackson his former brigade. Jackson intended to use it offensively rather than hold a static defensive line.

Jackson proposes an offensive

In a November 20 letter to Benjamin, Jackson argued that action in the valley could mislead Washington, support Joseph E. Johnston near Manassas, and eventually help recover northwestern Virginia.

Romney becomes the immediate objective

Jackson identified Romney as the key to his western flank and the South Branch Valley, as well as a point from which the Federals could advance on Winchester.

Johnston and Benjamin approve

Jackson’s superiors approved a move against Romney but Johnston warned that the broader proposal sought more than the available force could probably accomplish.

Discontent in the Stonewall Brigade

The brigade reached the Winchester area expecting access to home and family. Jackson instead restricted furloughs and movement while sickness spread during cold, wet weather.

Jackson targets Dam No. 5

With the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad damaged, the Chesapeake and Ohio Canal carried important coal traffic. Jackson marched to the Potomac to try to breach one of the canal’s regulating dams.

The dam detail works under fire

Federal artillery from Maryland forced the Confederates to conceal themselves by day. Work parties entered the cold water at night and eventually opened a breach after a militia diversion drew attention upstream.

The canal damage proves temporary

The episode reports that Federal workers repaired the breach within two days, limiting the practical result of the December operation.

Loring finally concentrates at Winchester

By December 26 all three of Loring’s brigades had arrived. The episode counts 7,500 regular troops, 2,200 militia, and 650 cavalry in Jackson’s enlarged command.

The expedition leaves Winchester

At 6 a.m. on January 1, 1862, Jackson’s column began moving toward an undisclosed destination. White hatbands were prescribed as a battlefield recognition measure.

Mild weather turns severe

A cold front brought wind, snow, and sleet after troops had shed winter equipment. Wagons fell behind, leaving many men without overcoats, blankets, or timely rations.

The column halts at Unger’s Store

The army covered only about eight miles on each of its first two days. Ice, a bogged column, delayed wagons, hunger, and exposure damaged its readiness before contact with the enemy.

Jackson turns toward Bath

Instead of taking the westward road through Bloomery Gap toward Romney, Jackson continued north to remove the Bath garrison from the flank of his planned advance.

Loring protests the march

Loring objected to orders that kept his hungry troops moving in extreme conditions. The dispute exposed a widening break between Jackson and his principal subordinate.

Jackson confronts Garnett

Richard B. Garnett halted the Stonewall Brigade so men who had gone as long as thirty hours without food could cook. Jackson ordered the movement continued and treated the halt as evidence that Garnett indulged his men.

The first attack on Bath stalls

Jackson sent militia around Warm Springs Mountain and ordered William Gilham toward town. Felled trees stopped the militia, Gilham pulled back after meeting skirmishers, and Loring countermanded a renewed advance.

Jackson forces the entry into Bath

After more delays on January 4, Jackson sent cavalry directly into Bath and followed. Federal infantry had already escaped west across the mountain or north across the Potomac.

Hancock refuses surrender

Jackson sent a demand across the river, briefly bombarded the town after its rejection, and ordered work on an upstream bridge. He did not have a secure crossing.

Jackson abandons the Hancock crossing

On January 6 Jackson concluded that the reinforced garrison could not be taken without losses he was unwilling to accept. He turned back toward Unger’s Store and Romney.

Ice disables the column

Snow-covered ice produced repeated falls, frostbite, broken movement, and severe suffering among horses that had not been properly shod for winter roads.

The army pauses at Unger’s Store

By January 8 one brigade reported 500 sick and another 300. Jackson stopped for four days so the troops could recover and farriers could shoe the horses.

Romney is reported abandoned

On January 13 Confederate cavalry informed Jackson that the United States garrison had evacuated after overestimating his strength.

The Stonewall Brigade reaches Romney

The brigade entered late on January 14 after another sleet-covered march. Loring’s brigades took as much as two more days to complete the movement.

Jackson reports casualties and disperses the command

Jackson reported four killed and 28 wounded, stationed Loring at Romney, assigned militia to Bath and Martinsburg, and returned with the Stonewall Brigade to Winchester.

Loring’s officers petition

Eleven officers complained of the Romney position and the command’s condition. Loring endorsed their statement while a copy also went outside the normal chain of command to Jefferson Davis.

Benjamin orders Loring back

Citing intelligence that Loring might be cut off, the War Department ordered an immediate return to Winchester. Jackson obeyed the order.

Jackson asks to leave field command

Jackson argued that political interference made effective command impossible. He requested assignment to VMI or, failing that, acceptance of his resignation.

Johnston tries to retain Jackson

Joseph E. Johnston delayed forwarding the request and urged Jackson to reconsider, noting that the War Department had also bypassed Johnston’s authority.

The terms of reconciliation remain uncertain

Governor John Letcher negotiated with Benjamin and sent Alexander R. Boteler to Jackson. Neither man recorded the exact terms, and any promise of future noninterference is unconfirmed.

Jackson withdraws the resignation

Jackson authorized Letcher to withdraw it. The next day he filed charges against Loring, but Davis did not permit a court-martial; Loring was promoted and transferred.
