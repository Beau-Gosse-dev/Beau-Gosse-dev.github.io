What happened

The episode asks what the casualties achieved

Soldiers and civilians evaluated the campaign while its outcome was still unknown. Their conclusions affected military morale, public support, and the 1864 election.

U.S. soldiers initially thought the campaign was succeeding

Many soldiers believed they had performed well in the Wilderness, remained in the field against Lee, and gained a clear success at the Mule Shoe.

The Mule Shoe shaped early opinion

U.S. forces broke part of Lee’s line, captured more than 3,000 prisoners and 20 cannon, and forced the Confederates to build a new position farther back.

Lincoln approved of Grant’s decision to continue

John Hay recorded Lincoln’s view that another commander probably would have withdrawn across the Rapidan after the Wilderness. Grant instead moved toward Spotsylvania.

Republican newspapers reported a major victory

The New York Times, Herald, and Tribune treated incomplete reports as evidence that Lee was retreating and that Confederate military power had been seriously damaged.

Democratic newspapers used a more cautious line

They could not ignore apparent U.S. gains, but they warned that reverses remained possible and criticized the Lincoln administration’s conduct of the war.

Newspapers relied on correspondents and official dispatches

Each major New York paper had several correspondents with the Army of the Potomac. Edwin Stanton also supplied reports based on Charles Dana’s observations and Grant’s dispatches.

Grant’s May 11 dispatch became a public slogan

Grant reported six days of heavy fighting, heavy losses on both sides, 5,000 Confederate prisoners, and his intention to continue the campaign through the summer if necessary.

The Mule Shoe produced another surge of confidence

After news of the May 12 attack arrived, both Republican and Democratic papers described it as a major U.S. success and a step toward Richmond.

Newspapers also reported the high casualty cost

Published estimates varied widely. By May 18, the New York Herald estimated about 35,000 Army of the Potomac casualties, excluding Sheridan’s cavalry.

Confidence declined when Lee remained in the field

Readers learned that the Confederate army had withdrawn to another strong line rather than collapsed. Reports of U.S. defeats at New Market and Drewry’s Bluff added to the change in opinion.

Northern opinion became more pessimistic after May 12

Soldiers, officials, and civilians noted that the campaign had produced heavy losses without destroying Lee’s army or ending the fighting.

The gold market reflected changing expectations

The greenback price of $100 in gold fell from $179.50 on May 4 to $168 on May 10, then rose to $184 on May 18 as confidence in U.S. military success weakened.

A false presidential proclamation disrupted the market

Joseph Howard Jr. supplied newspapers with a fabricated Lincoln proclamation that claimed Grant’s campaign was stopping and called for 400,000 additional troops.

Two newspapers printed the false document

The New York Journal of Commerce and New York World published it before learning it was fraudulent. Both retracted it, but Stanton ordered their editors arrested.

Other publishers demanded the editors’ release

Republican newspaper publishers joined the appeal. The editors were released on May 23, while Howard spent about three months at Fort Lafayette.

Confederate public opinion remained confident

Many Confederates interpreted Grant’s continued movement as refusal to admit defeat, not evidence that Lee had lost control of the campaign.

Southern newspapers explained Grant’s persistence as necessity

Editorials argued that Grant could not withdraw without political and military consequences, even though they claimed Lee had already defeated him.

The image of Grant as a butcher began during the campaign

The Richmond Examiner portrayed Grant’s strategy as a deliberate exchange of U.S. casualties for proportionate Confederate losses.

Lee was also accepting losses his army could not easily replace

The episode notes that Confederate forces had fewer soldiers available and that Lee continued spending those lives to keep the Confederacy in the war.

The campaign’s value was not yet known

At the end of May, neither side knew the campaign’s final outcome. The casualties therefore could not yet be measured against a completed military result.
