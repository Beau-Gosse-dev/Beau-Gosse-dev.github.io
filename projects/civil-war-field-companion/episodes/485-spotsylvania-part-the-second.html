Episode 485 · May 8–11, 1864 · Laurel Hill, Po River, and Mule Shoe salient, Spotsylvania Court House, Virginia

SPOTSYLVANIA (Part the Second)

Lee retained the Spotsylvania crossroads and repulsed four separate U.S. attacks on May 10. Upton’s 5,000-man column briefly broke the Mule Shoe defenses, captured prisoners and guns, and withdrew when the supporting attacks failed. Grant used that temporary breakthrough as the model for a larger assault

What happened

The armies converged on Spotsylvania

Lee’s troops had stopped Warren’s V Corps at Laurel Hill on May 8. Meade brought Sedgwick’s VI Corps forward for another attack.

The evening attack on May 8 failed

Only one V Corps division and one VI Corps brigade advanced. Other units missed their orders, some troops became lost, and a Confederate counterattack ended the effort.

Both armies extended their lines

Anderson held the Confederate left, Ewell the center, and A. P. Hill’s corps the right. U.S. corps formed north and west of the Confederate semicircle around the courthouse.

Edward Johnson created the Mule Shoe

He placed his division along a low ridge that projected north from the main line. The resulting salient lengthened the defenses and could be attacked from three sides.

Lee accepted the salient after engineer review

Lee disliked the shape, but engineers advised that concentrated artillery could defend it.

John Sedgwick was killed on May 9

A Confederate sharpshooter shot the VI Corps commander while he inspected the line near Brock Road. Horatio Wright succeeded him.

Jubal Early temporarily replaced A. P. Hill

Hill was ill and asked to leave command. Lee transferred Early from Ewell’s corps to lead the Third Corps.

Lee and Grant assessed the new position

Lee intended to remain between Grant and Richmond without accepting an unnecessary general battle. Grant still sought direct contact with Lee’s army away from Richmond’s defenses.

Grant ordered reconnaissance of both Confederate flanks

Hancock was sent toward the Confederate left and Burnside toward the right. Reports from Burnside’s sector led Meade and Grant to different conclusions about Lee’s intentions.

Grant ordered Hancock across the Po River

Grant believed the Confederate left might be weak. II Corps crossed west of Laurel Hill and moved toward Blockhouse Bridge.

Hancock planned a May 10 attack toward Laurel Hill

He intended to cross at Blockhouse Bridge from the west while Warren attacked from the north, but new Confederate works blocked the crossing by morning.

Lee prepared to strike Hancock’s isolated corps

William Mahone faced Hancock near Blockhouse Bridge while Henry Heth moved around the U.S. position to attack from the south.

Grant changed to a general attack

Grant assumed Lee had weakened Laurel Hill to attack Hancock and ordered the main Confederate line assaulted at 5 p.m.

Heth attacked Barlow at 2:30 p.m.

Confederate infantry and artillery struck the II Corps rear guard south of the Po while Barlow’s division withdrew toward its crossings.

Fire endangered the II Corps rear guard

Burning woods forced the remaining U.S. troops to run roughly 1,000 yards through smoke to the Po River. Hancock’s corps escaped destruction.

Warren attacked Laurel Hill early

He argued the position was vulnerable and received permission to move before the coordinated assault. The attack was repulsed and delayed the larger plan.

Mott attacked at the original time

His division did not receive the delay notice. Only about 1,250 men were available after maintaining the long skirmish line between VI and IX Corps.

Confederate artillery broke Mott’s attack

The small force crossed an open glade toward the Mule Shoe and came under canister at about 400 yards. It withdrew without reaching the works.

Wright sent a twelve-regiment task force

David Russell and engineer Ranald Mackenzie identified a wooded approach. Russell selected Emory Upton to command regiments drawn from all three VI Corps divisions.

Upton assigned four attack lines

The first would breach and clear the works laterally, the second would hold the opening and fire toward the salient’s base, the third would wait for orders, and the fourth remained in reserve.

Upton prohibited firing during the advance

He believed stopping to load and shoot would destroy the assault’s momentum. The column was to cross the open ground with fixed bayonets and fire only after entering the works.

Upton attacked at 6:35 p.m.

About 5,000 soldiers moved from the woods, crossed the abatis, absorbed two Confederate volleys without stopping, and broke through the earthworks.

Confederate artillery stopped firing

Gunners mistook Confederate prisoners moving with Upton’s troops for a counterattack. The pause helped the U.S. column deepen its penetration.

Ewell contained the breakthrough

He sent reinforcements into the salient while Upton reorganized. Mott had already been repulsed and Wright did not send sufficient additional troops.

Upton withdrew from the salient

His troops fell back to the exterior of the works and fought off a Confederate counterattack. Russell then authorized a full withdrawal.

The 7 p.m. attacks at Laurel Hill failed

Warren made only a limited effort. Gibbon’s division did not advance, and J. Hobart Ward’s brigade briefly entered the Confederate line before being driven back.

Four uncoordinated attacks produced no lasting gain

The attacks by Barlow’s rear guard, Warren, Mott, and Upton occurred separately, allowing Lee to move troops against each one.

Grant believed Lee’s army was weakening

Grant estimated U.S. losses since crossing the Rapidan at about 20,000 but thought Confederate losses were proportionally worse.

Grant promised to continue the campaign

In his May 11 dispatch to Henry Halleck, he wrote that he intended to fight on the current line through the summer if necessary.
