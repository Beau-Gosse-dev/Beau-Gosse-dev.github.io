What happened

The episode defines why Pea Ridge matters

Pea Ridge was fought in northwest Arkansas on March 7–8, 1862. The Confederate army outnumbered the United States army, included a substantial contingent of American Indian troops, and forced Curtis to reverse the direction of his defensive line.

Price and McCulloch remained divided after Wilson’s Creek

After their August 1861 victory at Wilson’s Creek, Sterling Price took the Missouri State Guard north to Lexington while Ben McCulloch returned with Confederate troops to northwest Arkansas.

Price could not hold Lexington

A strong United States column advanced from St. Louis after Price captured Lexington. Price withdrew to Springfield in southwest Missouri.

A pro-Confederate Missouri government voted to secede

Claiborne Fox Jackson assembled a rump legislature at Neosho. Its secession ordinance was disputed, but the Confederate government recognized Missouri and added a star for the state to its flag.

Jefferson Davis created a new command above Price and McCulloch

The Confederate government formed the Trans-Mississippi District and appointed Major General Earl Van Dorn to direct both commanders.

The episode introduces Earl Van Dorn

Van Dorn graduated from West Point in 1842, served and was wounded in Mexico and on the Texas frontier, resigned from the U.S. Army after Mississippi seceded, and reached major general in the Confederate army in September 1861.

Van Dorn took command at Little Rock

Van Dorn arrived at Little Rock on January 29, 1862. He worked to make Price and McCulloch cooperate and planned an offensive into Missouri.

Van Dorn initially planned a March 20 offensive

He told Price that reinforcements would create a force of at least 15,000 men. Van Dorn hoped to defeat United States forces in Missouri and eventually capture St. Louis.

Price’s retreat changed the plan

On February 22, Van Dorn learned that Price had abandoned Springfield ten days earlier and retreated to northwest Arkansas. Price’s camp was now close to McCulloch’s winter quarters.

Halleck wanted Price removed from Missouri

Major General Henry Halleck took command of the Department of the Missouri in November 1861. Driving out Price would let Halleck transfer garrison troops to operations on the Mississippi, Tennessee, and Cumberland rivers.

Curtis received the southwest Missouri assignment

On December 25, Halleck placed Brigadier General Samuel R. Curtis in command of the new District of Southwest Missouri and ordered him to defeat, disperse, or destroy Price’s force.

The episode introduces Samuel Curtis

Curtis graduated from West Point in 1831, became a lawyer and engineer, served in the Mexican War, represented Iowa in Congress, organized Iowa troops in 1861, and became a brigadier general in August.

Curtis established his headquarters at Rolla

Rolla was the railhead and the starting point for an advance into southwest Missouri. Franz Sigel objected to serving under Curtis, briefly resigned, and then returned as second in command.

Halleck ordered Curtis forward

On January 13, 1862, Curtis began pressuring Price. He advanced from Rolla to Lebanon along the Telegraph Road and organized the Army of the Southwest there.

Curtis organized four divisions

Peter Osterhaus commanded the 1st Division and Alexander Asboth the 2nd under Franz Sigel. Jefferson C. Davis commanded the 3rd Division and Eugene Carr the 4th under Curtis’s direct control.

The Army of the Southwest concentrated at Lebanon

Curtis had just over 12,000 men and 50 artillery pieces. Captain Philip Sheridan managed the long supply line back to Rolla.

Curtis began the final advance on Springfield

The army left Lebanon on February 10. Price had only about 7,000 men and could not expect to hold Springfield against a determined advance.

Price abandoned Springfield

Curtis reached within eight miles of Springfield by February 12. Price left that night and began a roughly 100-mile retreat toward the Boston Mountains in northwest Arkansas.

The armies fought at Little Sugar Creek

On February 17, Confederate troops took a blocking position at James Dunnegan’s farm on the Telegraph Road. After the first Civil War engagement fought in Arkansas, the rear guard withdrew safely.

Price and McCulloch agreed to withdraw farther south

Price initially wanted to make a stand. News that United States cavalry had raided Bentonville convinced him to accept McCulloch’s recommendation to continue the retreat.

Fayetteville was looted and burned during the retreat

Military stores were opened as the Confederates passed through. Soldiers looted military and civilian property, and fires set in buildings used by the army spread through much of the town.

The Confederate forces concentrated near Strickler Station

Price’s Missourians reached the winter camps south of Fayetteville in the Boston Mountains. The retreat placed them close to McCulloch’s troops.

Curtis occupied Cross Hollow and reconnoitered Fayetteville

Curtis took the abandoned camps at Cross Hollow and sent 1,200 men down the Telegraph Road. They reached Fayetteville on February 23 and stayed for two days.

Curtis chose to remain in Arkansas

Curtis had expelled Price from Missouri, but his army was eight miles inside Arkansas at the end of a supply line nearly 200 miles long. He kept the position despite facing a larger combined Confederate force.
