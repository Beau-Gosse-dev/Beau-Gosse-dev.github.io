What happened

Johnston shifted Hood to the Confederate left

Hood’s corps moved from the right of the Kennesaw line on the night of June 21 and reached the southern flank the next afternoon.

Hood chose an offensive response

Johnston’s assignment was to protect the flank. Hood decided to advance along Powder Springs Road and stop the U.S. turning movement.

Hooker and Schofield held the U.S. right

The XX Corps occupied the Kolb farm, and the XXIII Corps extended south along Powder Springs Road.

Five U.S. divisions covered the sector

Butterfield, Geary, and Williams formed the XX Corps line. Hascall and Cox held the XXIII Corps positions to their south.

Two regiments reconnoitered forward

The 123rd New York advanced north of Powder Springs Road and the 14th Kentucky advanced south of it.

Hooker ordered earthworks

By about 3 p.m., Hooker expected an attack and directed his corps to entrench while the two reconnaissance regiments moved forward.

Hooker overestimated the opposing force

At 4 p.m., he told Thomas that the whole Confederate army was moving toward his front and requested support.

Hood deployed two divisions in front

Carter L. Stevenson formed on the left and Thomas C. Hindman on the right. A third division remained in reserve.

Hood attacked without adequate reconnaissance

The corps was not ready until midafternoon. Hood mistook the two U.S. reconnaissance regiments for the opening of a larger attack.

Stevenson advanced around 5 p.m.

The 123rd New York and 14th Kentucky fired and withdrew across open ground to the prepared U.S. line.

The two forward regiments lost more than 100

Both regiments suffered casualties while delaying the Confederate advance and returning to the main defenses.

About 11,000 Confederates entered the attack

Stevenson had roughly 6,000 men, and Hindman’s division brought the attacking force to about 11,000.

Prepared U.S. troops opened fire

Hooker’s preparations placed infantry and artillery behind fieldworks. Artillery firing grape and canister disrupted the Confederate lines before they reached the works.

Hindman was stopped by artillery

U.S. batteries fired canister into the division. Hindman’s troops did not reach the main U.S. line and withdrew.

The attack ended after about 90 minutes

Fighting ran from about 5 to 6:30 p.m. More than 1,000 Confederates and a few hundred U.S. soldiers were casualties.

Stevenson’s division suffered most of the loss

The division reported more than 800 casualties, and several Confederate regiments lost more than half their men.

Williams described a complete U.S. success

The XX Corps division commander reported that the attack was defeated with comparatively low U.S. losses.

Hooker again reported three Confederate corps

Sherman rejected the claim because he knew much of Johnston’s army remained on the Confederate right and center.

Schofield correctly identified Hood’s corps

Schofield reported that Hood alone had attacked and that the Confederate losses were much greater.

Johnston’s official account was incorrect

Johnston told Richmond that Hood had been attacked and had captured part of the U.S. works. The Confederate attack had actually failed.

Stevenson blamed the ground

His report stated that the terrain was unsuitable for the movement and that carrying the U.S. works by assault was impractical.

Manigault criticized Hood

The Confederate brigade commander considered the attack reckless and cited it as evidence that Hood was unfit for corps command.

Schofield found the Confederate flank extended

On June 23, Schofield reported that entrenched Confederates continued well beyond the point his corps could reach.

Sherman decided to order a direct attack

The failed turning movement and continued stalemate contributed to Sherman’s decision to attack the Kennesaw line.
