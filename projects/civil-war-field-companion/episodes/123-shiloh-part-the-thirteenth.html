Episode 123 · April 6, 1862, about 5 p.m. through the night of April 6–7 · Grant’s final line above Pittsburg Landing, Dill Branch, the Tennessee River, and the captured United States camps at Shiloh, Tennessee

SHILOH (Part the Thirteenth)

Chalmers’s and Jackson’s brigades attacked the United States left at about 6 p.m. Artillery on Grant’s ridge, supporting infantry, the gunboats Tyler and Lexington, and the flooded Dill Branch stopped the two-brigade attack. Other Confederate commanders were assembling a larger assault when P.G.T. Beauregard ordered the army to break contact near sundown and return to the captured camps. Beauregard incorrectly believed Grant’s army could be finished the next morning and that Buell remained distant. The 36th Indiana had already crossed at Pittsburg Landing, more Army of the Ohio troops arrived during the night, and Lew Wallace’s division approached from the north. Confederate units remained dispersed and poorly reorganized. Grant rejected retreat and ordered an attack at daylight

What happened

Chalmers and Jackson began the final attack

At about 6 p.m., the two brigades advanced toward the United States left with sunset about twenty minutes away.

Grant’s line opened fire

Webster’s artillery, supporting infantry, and the two timberclads fired into the Confederate advance.

Buell’s first regiment arrived

Colonel Jacob Ammen’s 36th Indiana crossed the Tennessee River with division commander William Nelson after reaching the east bank at about 5 p.m.

The regiment pushed through the landing area

Thousands of disorganized soldiers crowded below the bluff. Nelson cleared space so the regiment could land and move uphill.

Lew Wallace heard the final-line guns

His 3rd Division was about half an hour away when officers recognized the siege guns and timberclads firing near Pittsburg Landing.

The two-brigade attack failed

Chalmers and Jackson crossed the most difficult ground on the front and received concentrated fire without enough support. Both brigades withdrew.

Other Confederate commanders prepared a larger attack

Bragg, Polk, and Withers were moving additional troops forward when units began receiving an order to stop.

Beauregard ordered the army to break contact

Near sundown, he directed units to withdraw to a line near the captured United States camps.

The order rested on incorrect information

Beauregard believed Grant was defeated, Buell was still a day or two away, and gunboat fire was causing more damage than it actually was.

A successful additional assault was uncertain

The podcast concludes that darkness, Confederate exhaustion and disorganization, and the strength of Grant’s position made a breakthrough unlikely.

Cold rain began after the fighting stopped

Thousands of wounded men remained between the lines while a heavy rain continued through much of the night.

The gunboats shelled through the night

Tyler and Lexington alternated shots about every fifteen minutes. Some shells landed near wounded soldiers as well as Confederate positions.

The Confederate army remained disorganized

Many soldiers stayed in captured camps rather than rejoining their units. Beauregard’s overnight reorganization was incomplete.

Forrest reported United States reinforcements

Near midnight, Forrest told Chalmers that troops were crossing the river continuously, but he could not locate Beauregard or most senior commanders.

United States officers considered withdrawal

James McPherson reported that Grant’s army was badly damaged and asked whether he should prepare a retreat.

Grant ordered a counterattack

Grant rejected retreat and stated that the army would attack at daylight on April 7.
