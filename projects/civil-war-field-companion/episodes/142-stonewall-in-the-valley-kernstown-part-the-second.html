STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: KERNSTOWN (Part the Second)

This episode covers only the opening of the First Battle of Kernstown. Nathan Kimball concentrated a much larger United States force around Pritchard’s Hill, checked Turner Ashby’s morning probes, and kept the field command despite suggestions from the wounded James Shields in Winchester. Jackson arrived near noon, attacked on the basis of Ashby’s false estimate, and shifted infantry and artillery west toward Sandy Ridge without fully explaining his plan to his brigade commanders. The episode ends when Sandie Pendleton reports the scale of the United States force and Jackson acknowledges the danger. The later Union counterattack, Confederate retreat, and United States tactical victory are outside this installment
Episode scopeThe March 22 skirmish and the opening phase of First Kernstown on March 23The narrative stops before Erastus Tyler’s main attack on Sandy Ridge, Richard Garnett’s retreat, and the end of the battle. Those events belong to episode 143.
Overall battle resultUnited States tactical victory; later events not covered hereThe National Park Service also describes a Confederate strategic benefit: the battle helped persuade Washington to keep or redirect troops toward the Valley rather than McClellan’s movement against Richmond. That consequence cannot be inferred from the incomplete action heard in this episode alone.
Immediate causeJackson attacked on false intelligenceTurner Ashby reported only four United States infantry regiments and a battery or two near Winchester. In fact, Shields’s division remained in the area. Ashby then encountered evidence of the larger force on March 23 but did not send Jackson a corrective report before the main column arrived.
ForcesRoughly 3,500 Confederates against roughly 8,000–8,500 United States troopsTotals vary with the definition of present versus engaged. The episode estimates that straggling left Jackson about 3,000 men on arrival. Jackson’s official report lists 3,087 infantry present that evening, 2,742 engaged, 27 guns with 18 engaged, and 290 cavalry. Shields reported that the United States force did not exceed 7,000, while the NPS order of battle rounds the division to about 8,500.
Shields woundedShell fragment fractured his left arm on March 22James Shields was taken to Winchester. He continued receiving reports and sending discretionary suggestions, but Colonel Nathan Kimball exercised field command on March 23. Shields survived the wound.
United States field commanderCol. Nathan KimballKimball commanded the First Brigade of Shields’s division. He declined repeated suggestions to advance prematurely because he suspected that additional Confederate forces were approaching.
First shotAbout 9 a.m., March 23One of Ashby’s guns opened the battle after an 8th Ohio reconnaissance identified Confederate cavalry, infantry, and artillery south of Kernstown.
Key United States positionPritchard’s HillThe hill stood west of the Valley Turnpike and commanded the approaches south of Winchester. Kimball placed ten rifled guns there, supported first by the 67th Ohio and 5th Ohio and later by additional infantry.
Morning Confederate forceAshby’s cavalry, artillery, and four companies of the 2nd VirginiaThe infantry detachment was led by Capt. John Q. A. Nadenbousch. Ashby tried both sides of the Valley Turnpike but was pushed back southeast of Kernstown by about 11 a.m.
Jackson’s arrivalNear noon, about two miles south of KernstownThe episode estimates that most of the infantry had marched more than 20 miles on March 22 and at least 12 additional miles on March 23. Jackson allowed the arriving brigades to rest before reconnoitering.
Decision to fight on SundayJackson decided that delay was more dangerous than an immediate attackThe episode quotes Jackson’s later letter to his wife explaining his religious misgivings. The transcript’s word “circumcised” is a speech-recognition error; published transcriptions render the phrase as “circumstanced as on that day.”
Jackson’s planMove west, seize Sandy Ridge, turn the United States right, and reach the Valley TurnpikeThe transcript briefly renders the feature as “Sandy Bridge,” but the battlefield feature is Sandy Ridge. The route was intended to avoid a direct assault on the guns at Pritchard’s Hill.
Barton’s WoodsCovered approach used by Fulkerson and the Stonewall BrigadeFulkerson’s 23rd and 37th Virginia reached the northern edge of the woods about 2 p.m. The Stonewall Brigade followed, except for the 5th Virginia, which Jackson retained near the turnpike.

Later in the episode, eleven Confederate guns unlimbered on the exposed eastern slope of Sandy Ridge.
Command-information failureThe brigade commanders received movements without Jackson’s overall planGarnett later complained that he lacked the information needed to act intelligently. Fulkerson, Garnett, and nearby regimental commanders repeatedly made decisions without knowing which friendly units were moving toward Sandy Ridge.
Fulkerson’s exposed advanceAbout 600 men of the 37th and 23rd Virginia crossed open groundInstead of passing safely around the United States position, the column moved toward a grove at the western base of Pritchard’s Hill. Fire from the hill, Battery L of the 1st Ohio, and the appearance of the 84th Pennsylvania caused Fulkerson to turn left toward Sandy Ridge.

Artillery disadvantageUnited States rifled guns held higher ground and greater effective rangeThe episode places the Pritchard’s Hill guns about one mile away and roughly 100 feet above the exposed Confederate batteries. Jackson told Sergeant Clement Fishburne to reduce fire from his short-ranged 12-pounder howitzer.

It captures Jackson’s late recognition that the initial intelligence was wrong, but the exact wording should not be treated as a contemporaneous transcript.
Important leader deaths in this installmentNoneShields was seriously wounded but survived. The episode mentions one Confederate cavalryman killed during Ashby’s diversion, but no important commander was killed or mortally wounded in the action covered here.

What happened

Soldiers introduce the close-range battle

The episode opens with accounts by Sgt. Frank B. Nickerson of the 8th Ohio and Sgt. Clement Fishburne of the Rockbridge Artillery. Nickerson’s encounter previews fighting completed in the next episode; Fishburne’s artillery experience appears later in this one.

The March 22 situation is recapped

Jackson had marched north after Ashby reported that Banks’s force was leaving the Valley. Ashby was already skirmishing near Kernstown.

Ashby’s shell disables James Shields

A shell fragment broke Shields’s left arm above the elbow. He was carried back to Winchester and could not direct the battle in person.

Nathan Kimball takes field command

As the senior brigade commander present, Kimball assumed control and drove Ashby’s small force south toward Newtown before nightfall.

Both commands misread the skirmish

Banks and Shields still believed Jackson was distant, while Jackson accepted Ashby’s report that only four United States regiments remained near Winchester.

Straggling reduces Jackson’s column

Hundreds had fallen out during the March 22 forced march. The episode estimates that fewer than 3,500 men reached the Strasburg area.

Four infantry companies reinforce Ashby

At first light on March 23 Jackson sent four companies of the 2nd Virginia ahead. The rest of the exhausted command did not start for nearly two more hours.

The 8th Ohio conducts a reconnaissance

Lt. Col. Franklin Sawyer led companies west of the turnpike, crossed Pritchard’s Hill, and identified Confederate guns, dismounted cavalry, and infantry before withdrawing.

The battle’s first shot is fired

At about 9 a.m. one of Ashby’s guns opened fire. The shell caused no damage but prompted Kimball to concentrate his force.

Kimball identifies the key ground

Pritchard’s Hill commanded the Valley Turnpike and the surrounding approaches. Kimball made it the center of his defensive position.

Ten rifled guns occupy Pritchard’s Hill

The artillery could reach Confederate movement along the turnpike and across the open ground to the west.

The 67th Ohio clears the hill’s base

Kimball sent the inexperienced regiment into woods west of the hill, then placed it behind the guns. The 5th Ohio also moved up in support.

Ashby tries the east side of the turnpike

Capt. John Q. A. Nadenbousch moved his four 2nd Virginia companies north, hoping to outflank Pritchard’s Hill.

Kimball sends the 14th Indiana to meet the probe

From Pritchard’s Hill, Kimball saw the Confederate movement and reinforced the United States troops east of the turnpike shortly before 10 a.m.

Ashby recognizes the larger force but sends no warning

He recalled Nadenbousch after encountering much more resistance than expected, yet did not correct his earlier report to Jackson.

Ashby is contained southeast of Kernstown

By about 11 a.m. his force occupied woods roughly half a mile southeast of the hamlet. United States skirmishers halted in the muddy fields to the north.

Jackson expects to reach Winchester

Before marching that morning, he had told Joseph E. Johnston that he hoped to be near Winchester that evening.

Banks leaves for Washington

An aide reported that only the same limited Confederate force seen on March 22 appeared to be present. Banks therefore continued with his planned departure.

Shields sends suggestions from Winchester

The wounded division commander read reports and dictated messages through surgeon H. M. Maccabee, but left tactical execution to Kimball in the field.

Kimball refuses a premature advance

He rejected a suggestion to push up the Valley Turnpike because he suspected that more Confederate troops were near and preferred to strengthen his position.

Jackson reaches the battlefield area

After the advertisement break, the narrative resumes near noon. The Stonewall, Fulkerson, and Burks brigades halted about two miles south of Kernstown to rest.

Jackson reconnoiters with his aides

He rode forward with George Junkin and Sandie Pendleton. He initially preferred to wait until Monday but had fewer than six hours of daylight and believed the visible guns could report his strength.

The visible guns conceal the larger position

Jackson could see the ten guns on Pritchard’s Hill but not all the supporting infantry. He also failed to confer with Ashby about the morning’s fighting east of the turnpike.

Jackson orders an immediate attack

He feared delay would allow the United States rear guard to escape or receive reinforcements. His later letter to his wife described the decision to fight on Sunday as a distasteful duty.

The plan turns toward Sandy Ridge

Jackson intended to move west, turn the United States right at Pritchard’s Hill, reach the Valley Turnpike in the rear, and open the route to Winchester.

Fulkerson enters Barton’s Woods

The 23rd and 37th Virginia led the covered movement north. They reached the woods’ northern edge about 2 p.m.

Jackson centralizes the artillery

He detached batteries from their brigades and placed them under his direct orders. Burks supplied infantry support while the 48th Virginia guarded the trains.

Brigade commanders lack the full plan

Orders passed through Pendleton and Junkin, but Garnett, Fulkerson, and Burks were not told Jackson’s overall purpose or the complete disposition of friendly units.

Kimball again chooses defense

At about 1:30 p.m. another suggestion arrived from Shields to attack. Kimball had seen Confederate movement in Barton’s Woods and held his ground.

Pritchard’s Hill opens on Barton’s Woods

The rifled guns fired on Confederates at the northern edge of the timber as Jackson tried to organize the western movement.

Fulkerson receives an exposed assignment

Jackson ordered him to turn the guns’ flank, requiring the two-regiment brigade to cross roughly three-quarters of a mile of open and obstructed ground.

About 600 Confederates enter the meadow

The 37th Virginia led and the 23rd Virginia followed in a narrow column. Marshy ground, fences, and artillery fire slowed the movement.

Battery L and the 84th Pennsylvania block the route

Capt. Lucius Robinson’s 1st Ohio battery unlimbered northwest of the grove, while the 84th Pennsylvania formed near it. Fulkerson turned left toward Sandy Ridge.

Only the 33rd Virginia follows at first

Garnett accompanied Colonel Arthur C. Cummings and about 275 men into the meadow. The rest of the Stonewall Brigade had not received the movement order.

Garnett also turns toward Sandy Ridge

Concentrated artillery fire and lack of information caused him to redirect the 33rd Virginia west rather than continue toward Pritchard’s Hill.

Garnett considers withdrawing

With Fulkerson out of sight and the rest of his brigade absent, he believed the small force on Sandy Ridge could accomplish little and might be counterattacked.

Jackson starts the remaining regiments forward

He sent Pendleton to move the 2nd and 4th Virginia. They crossed the meadow under artillery fire by about 2:30 p.m.; the 27th Virginia and more guns followed.

Ashby makes a diversion east of the turnpike

About 150 cavalrymen crossed the stream, overran an 8th Ohio skirmish company, and were repulsed with one killed and six wounded. Kimball retained substantial strength on that flank.

Garnett and Fulkerson still lack a common picture

They discussed returning to Barton’s Woods without knowing that additional Confederate infantry and artillery were already moving toward them.

Eleven Confederate guns unlimber on Sandy Ridge

The batteries were exposed on the eastern slope, below and about a mile from the rifled guns on Pritchard’s Hill. United States fire immediately struck men and horses.

Jackson reaches the Confederate batteries

Fishburne’s 12-pounder howitzer could not reach the United States position effectively. Jackson ordered only occasional fire while the crew sheltered behind a stone fence.

Pendleton reports the real scale of the opposition

From higher ground on Sandy Ridge, he saw extensive United States infantry and artillery and estimated at least 10,000 men between Jackson and Winchester.

Jackson says, “We are in for it”

He told Pendleton to keep the information quiet, fearing its effect on the command. The episode ends its battle narrative at this moment of recognition.

The hosts recommend Ecelbarger’s battle study

They recommend Gary L. Ecelbarger’s “We Are in for It!” for a detailed study of the First Battle of Kernstown.
