CLASH OF THE IRONCLADS (Part the First)

The Confederacy rebuilt the burned steam frigate USS Merrimack as the ironclad CSS Virginia. On March 8, 1862, Franklin Buchanan took Virginia into Hampton Roads and attacked the United States blockading squadron, sinking USS Cumberland and destroying USS Congress. What happened

The naval battle coincided with Pea Ridge

On March 8, 1862, the first day of the Hampton Roads battle began while the armies at Pea Ridge were preparing for their second day of fighting.

Confederates recovered Merrimack’s hull

United States forces burned and sank USS Merrimack when they evacuated Gosport Navy Yard in April 1861. The machinery and lower hull remained usable after the Confederacy took the yard.

Merrimack became CSS Virginia

Confederate engineers cut down the frigate and built an armored casemate over the surviving hull. The completed ship was commissioned as CSS Virginia.

Wood and iron protected the casemate

The sloped sides used 24 inches of pine and oak covered by two layers of 2-inch iron plate.

A ram was fixed to the bow

A roughly 1,500-pound iron ram projected about three feet from the bow for attacks below an enemy ship’s waterline.

Virginia was a large ship

The rebuilt vessel was 275 feet long, slightly more than 51 feet wide, and carried a crew of about 320.

Ten guns filled the battery

Virginia carried two 7-inch Brooke rifles, two 6.4-inch Brooke rifles, and six 9-inch Dahlgren smoothbores.

Virginia entered Confederate service

The Confederate Navy commissioned the ship on February 17, 1862.

Buchanan left Gosport on March 8

Franklin Buchanan took Virginia down the Elizabeth River on what was expected to be a trial run, then continued into Hampton Roads to attack the blockading squadron.

Virginia broke the wooden-ship line

Virginia sank Cumberland with gunfire and its ram, forced Congress to surrender and burn, and helped drive Minnesota, Roanoke, and St. Lawrence aground.

Virginia withdrew at about 5 p.m.

The falling tide and damage to the ship led Buchanan’s command to leave the scene and return toward the Elizabeth River.

Monitor reached Hampton Roads that night

USS Monitor arrived from New York and took position near the grounded Minnesota. The ironclads fought on March 9.

Stephen Mallory became Confederate Navy secretary

Jefferson Davis appointed Mallory in February 1861. The Confederacy began without a national navy or a shipbuilding system.

Mallory had maritime and Senate experience

Mallory was born in Trinidad, grew up in Key West, practiced maritime law, and won election to the United States Senate in 1851.

Mallory had directed naval legislation

As a member and later chairman of the Senate Naval Affairs Committee, he supported fleet expansion and technical improvements before secession.

The Confederacy started with ten small vessels

Its initial force consisted of four revenue cutters, an old side-wheel steamer, and several tenders and tugs. None could match a major United States warship.

The United States fleet expanded rapidly

The United States Navy had more than 100 warships early in the conflict and 264 by December 1861.

Mallory chose several ways to offset the fleet imbalance

His department pursued new weapons, bought and built ships abroad, and used commerce raiders and privateers against United States shipping.

The Confederate Navy developed new weapons

The department supported electrically detonated mines, then called torpedoes, as well as submarines and armored ships.

Mallory made iron armor a priority

He argued that armored ships could withstand the guns of wooden warships and reduce the United States Navy’s numerical advantage.

Armored vessels already existed

Korean, Spanish, French, and British ships had used armor before the Civil War. The French Gloire and British Warrior showed that seagoing armored warships were practical.

The design team selected Merrimack

John M. Brooke, John L. Porter, and William P. Williamson met Mallory on June 23, 1861, and recommended converting the wreck at Gosport.

Congress funded the conversion

The Confederate Congress appropriated $170,000 for the project.

Tredegar supplied the armor

Tredegar Iron Works in Richmond was the only Southern mill able to roll the required plates. It delivered about 725 tons of iron for the ship.

Conditions aboard were poor

The casemate had limited ventilation, heat, smoke, and crowded accommodations. Catesby ap Roger Jones described the ship as unhealthy for its crew.

Draft and engines limited the ship

Virginia drew more than 22 feet of water and used the unreliable engines condemned before Merrimack’s loss. It made only about five or six knots and turned slowly.

Buchanan entered Confederate service

The veteran United States Navy officer resigned in 1861 when he expected Maryland to secede. Gideon Welles refused his attempt to withdraw the resignation, and Buchanan later joined the Confederate Navy.

The ship went directly into combat

Buchanan reported aboard on March 4. Four days later, Virginia left on its first powered trip with untested guns and yard workers still aboard, then attacked the blockade.
