What happened

The campaign imposed continuous stress

Soldiers marched, fought, dug earthworks, and stood picket duty for weeks with few long pauses. Grant and enlisted men counted the consecutive days of combat in their letters.

Rain on May 17 created a needed pause

One soldier wrote that his unit had worked day and night for about two weeks, far longer than the two- or three-day battles he had experienced before.

Soldiers had to maintain themselves

Besides fighting and marching, individuals found water, cooked food, cleaned clothing, built shelter, dug works, and buried the dead. Exhaustion made many of those tasks impossible.

Many soldiers fought without regular meals

After the first day in the Wilderness, some men slept instead of eating. Fighting resumed before they could prepare breakfast.

Confederate rations were below the official allowance

John Worsham recalled roughly half a pound of meat and limited bread or meal each day. Real coffee was issued rarely and in very small quantities.

U.S. rations were larger but repetitive

Army of the Potomac soldiers commonly received salt pork and hardtack. Marches and mud sometimes separated units from their supply wagons for one or two days.

The Army of the Potomac moved cattle with it

More than 1,000 cattle supplied occasional fresh beef, but soldiers also had to herd, slaughter, and butcher the animals.

Safe drinking water was difficult to obtain

Streams in the Wilderness were few and could be distant from units under fire. Later in the campaign, soldiers described drinking contaminated swamp water.

Vegetation and insects caused additional problems

Poison ivy, ticks, mosquitoes, horseflies, and lice affected daily life. Soldiers often had no time to wash clothes or remove lice.

Uniforms went unchanged for weeks

Rufus Dawes wrote on May 25 that he had worn the same clothing since May 3. Boiling uniforms killed lice but could shrink the clothing.

Shelter and sleep were limited

Many men slept on the ground or in trenches under a rubber blanket. One officer estimated soldiers averaged only two to four hours of sleep per day.

Illness removed thousands from duty

Poor food, bad water, insects, mud, and exhaustion produced diarrhea, fever, and other disease. Records list 4,225 sick soldiers evacuated from Fredericksburg to Washington.

Both armies wanted rest

Confederate general Bryan Grimes wrote on May 19 that his men had fought or skirmished for fifteen days and were nearly exhausted.

Repeated danger produced psychological strain

Civil War battles usually exposed troops to intense danger for hours rather than weeks. During the Overland Campaign, soldiers remained under threat with little return to safety.

Soldiers relied on duty, belief, and expectations of survival

The episode describes two common supports: a conscious belief that remaining in danger was required and an expectation that the individual might survive even when others did not.

Religion and fatalism helped soldiers manage fear

Many soldiers relied on Christian belief or accepted that they could not control the outcome. Religious revivals had spread through both armies before the campaign.

Physical fear did not always prevent action

Accounts describe trembling and dread before combat, followed by effective action once fighting began. The body’s immediate stress response could help temporarily.

Constant exposure reduced the ability to function

After many days, anxiety and sleep loss made stress harder to manage. Soldiers reported that remaining in a continuous state of excitement was exhausting.

The North Anna pause restored some effectiveness

For about two days the armies were not in close contact. Soldiers slept and briefly recovered before fighting increased again.

Veteran units became less reliable under accumulated strain

Observers saw reduced willingness to complete attacks and sudden collapses in units that previously had fought well, including the Iron Brigade at Jericho Mill.

Army of the Potomac veterans faced additional concerns

They distrusted some replacements and knew many experienced regiments were close to the end of their enlistments. Those perceptions affected confidence even when the short-term soldiers continued to fight.
