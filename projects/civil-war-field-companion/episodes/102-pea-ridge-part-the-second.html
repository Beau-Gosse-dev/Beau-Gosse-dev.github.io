PEA RIDGE (Part the Second)

What happened

Curtis held his position in northwest Arkansas

Curtis had driven Price out of Missouri. By March 1, he decided to remain in Arkansas, concentrate his divided army, and prepare for a Confederate advance.

Van Dorn left Little Rock for the army

News of Price’s retreat reached Van Dorn on February 22. Within two days he left Little Rock with a small staff and traveled toward the Confederate camps in the Boston Mountains.

Van Dorn became ill on the journey

After falling into the Little Red River, Van Dorn reached Van Buren on March 1 with chills and fever. McCulloch reported that the army was ready to march.

Van Dorn reached Price’s headquarters

Van Dorn arrived on March 2. Price’s men had spent a week resting and drawing supplies in the Boston Mountain camps after their retreat from Missouri.

Confederate cavalry reported the United States army dispersed

Price and McCulloch briefed Van Dorn on March 3. Their cavalry had tracked Curtis’s divisions across northwest Arkansas.

Van Dorn decided to strike Sigel at Bentonville

Van Dorn believed he could reach the two divisions around Bentonville with a three-to-one advantage, defeat them, and then turn against Curtis’s remaining divisions.

Speed replaced logistical preparation

Van Dorn ordered the army to leave tents and baggage behind. Each man carried a musket, 40 cartridges, one blanket, and three days’ rations; the ammunition and supply train would follow.

The Confederate march began in a snowstorm

On March 4, the army left its camps for Fayetteville and Bentonville. Snow, sleet, mud, hunger, and little sleep weakened men and animals during the march.

Curtis had already ordered Sigel to withdraw

On March 3, Curtis directed Sigel’s divisions to leave Bentonville and join the prepared position at Little Sugar Creek. Sigel initially moved without urgency.

Van Dorn’s army advanced from Fayetteville

Price’s command led the March 5 movement up the Elm Springs Road. Van Dorn, still ill, traveled in an ambulance while the troops marched through freezing conditions.

Sigel withdrew the Elm Springs outpost

As Sigel prepared to leave Bentonville, his cavalry outpost at Elm Springs returned to town. The withdrawal temporarily left the Confederate approach unguarded.

The Confederates camped at Elm Springs

Van Dorn’s troops reached Elm Springs late on March 5. The 3rd Texas Cavalry continued north and encountered pickets left by Colonel Frederick Schaefer.

Curtis learned that the Confederate army was moving north

A United States supporter from Fayetteville and one of Curtis’s spies independently reported the advance. Curtis sent Sigel an urgent order to march for Little Sugar Creek.

Sigel ordered the Bentonville withdrawal

At 1 a.m. on March 6, Sigel sent Alexander Asboth’s division and the wagon train first, followed by Peter Osterhaus’s division. Most of the column cleared town by mid-morning.

A missed order cut Sigel’s rear guard in half

Sigel intended to combine 600 men in town with Schaefer’s 2nd Missouri. Schaefer did not receive the instruction and marched away, leaving Sigel with about 600 men.

Van Dorn split his approach to Bentonville

He sent most of the cavalry northwest by Osage Mills while the infantry continued up the Bentonville Road. The intended two-direction attack began after most of Sigel’s force had escaped.

Confederate cavalry converged south of Bentonville

At about 10 a.m., Sigel learned his planned reinforcement had left and that Confederates were close. James McIntosh’s and Elijah Gates’s cavalry commands joined south of town.

Sigel fought a rear-guard action eastward

McIntosh sent Gates directly after Sigel while taking Texas cavalry through and around Bentonville. Cold, fatigue, and exhausted horses slowed the attempted encirclement.

Sigel escaped to Curtis

Sigel managed the retreat under repeated cavalry attacks and reached the main army. By the evening of March 6, Curtis had concentrated all four divisions.

Vandever completed a forced march

Colonel William Vandever’s detachment reached Little Sugar Creek after marching 42 miles in 16 hours over icy roads without losing a man.

Curtis prepared the Little Sugar Creek position

United States infantry and artillery occupied bluffs on the north bank above the creek. A direct attack would have required Confederates to cross the valley under downward fire.

Van Dorn reached Bentonville with an exhausted army

After three days of marching, food and forage were gone and straggling was widespread. Van Dorn still believed Curtis was retreating rather than concentrating to fight.

Albert Pike’s command arrived

The 1st and 2nd Cherokee Mounted Rifles and a squadron of Texas cavalry joined Van Dorn late on March 6.

McCulloch proposed turning the United States right

At a 5 p.m. command meeting, McCulloch advised against attacking Little Sugar Creek. He proposed using the Bentonville Detour to force Curtis out of the position.

Van Dorn ordered an immediate night march

McCulloch asked to let the exhausted army rest. Van Dorn refused and ordered the column onto the detour that evening.

Van Dorn changed the flanking move into an envelopment

Price and McCulloch wanted a limited move that would force Curtis toward Missouri. Van Dorn instead aimed to reach the Telegraph Road in Curtis’s rear and compel the entire army to surrender.
