What happened

Johnston waited at Dalton

The Army of Tennessee strengthened its Rocky Face Ridge defenses and left Sherman to choose where and how to open the campaign.

Parallel ridges divided northwest Georgia

The southern ends of the Appalachian Mountains ran diagonally across the region and restricted east-west movement.

Rocky Face Ridge covered Dalton from the west

The ridge began northwest of town, stretched nearly 25 miles toward the Oostanaula, and rose as much as 800 feet above nearby valleys.

Only three passes could carry an army

Mill Creek Gap carried the railroad and main road, Dug Gap crossed the ridge south of Dalton, and Snake Creek Gap opened near Resaca.

Snake Creek Gap approached the Confederate railroad

The pass opened into Sugar Valley west of Resaca, where the Western and Atlantic crossed the Oostanaula River.

The defensive problem extended to Rome

Johnston had to cover Crow Valley, Rocky Face Ridge, the Oostanaula River, and Rome rather than only the fortified position at Dalton.

Rome was a transportation and industrial point

It was a river port and connected by a branch railroad to Kingston on the Western and Atlantic.

U.S. troops had several routes to Rome

They could march south from Chattanooga through western valleys or move southeast from the Tennessee River across the ridges.

A U.S. force at Rome would threaten Dalton

From Rome, it could move toward Kingston or the Etowah crossing and force Johnston to attack, retreat east, or withdraw rapidly south.

The next strong railroad position was far south

If Johnston left Dalton, he had no suitable stop north of the Etowah crossing about 45 miles away.

Johnston made few preparations south of Dalton

He did little to fortify Rome or coordinate the defense of northwest Georgia with Leonidas Polk’s command in Alabama and Mississippi.

Crow Valley left the position open from the north

U.S. troops could enter from east Tennessee or move around the north end of Rocky Face Ridge and flank the Confederate line.

The railroad ran parallel to the defensive line

Any U.S. force passing through Dug Gap or Snake Creek Gap could quickly reach Johnston’s supply and retreat route.

The ridge also gave Johnston interior lines

Protected roads and the railroad behind the ridge allowed Confederate troops to shift between Crow Valley, Resaca, and Rome.

Johnston later called Dalton a weak position

He said pressure from Richmond kept the army there, but he did not explain his concerns to Jefferson Davis or Secretary of War James Seddon.

Grant assigned Sherman two connected tasks

Sherman was to defeat Johnston or keep his army occupied so it could not reinforce Robert E. Lee in Virginia.

Sherman preferred maneuver to major battle

He considered battlefield results uncertain and often insufficient to destroy an opposing army.

Sherman aimed beyond battlefield victory

He emphasized attacks on morale, transportation, and economic support as well as the opposing army.

Sherman rejected a direct assault at Dalton

His knowledge of northwest Georgia and study of the map led him to focus on Rome and the Confederate railroad.

McPherson received the original flanking assignment

The Army of the Tennessee would move from northern Alabama toward Rome while Thomas and Schofield demonstrated against Dalton.

Sherman expected Johnston to withdraw

A strong U.S. force near Rome would threaten the railroad and could force the Confederates to fall back toward Atlanta.

Sherman moved headquarters to Chattanooga

He left Nashville on April 28 as both armies prepared to begin operations in early May.
