THE OVERLAND CAMPAIGN'S WOUNDED, PRISONERS, & DEAD

What happened

Early field returns showed the scale of the losses

By mid-May the Army of the Potomac, including IX Corps but excluding cavalry and some recent reinforcements, reported 56,124 men present and 36,872 casualties since crossing the Rapidan.

Ambulances filled the roads behind the armies

Wagons and ambulances carried wounded soldiers away from the battlefields. The Confederate system had fewer casualties to move and shorter routes to established Virginia hospitals.

Richmond hospitals received Confederate wounded

Confederate patients usually moved from an aid station to a field hospital, then by ambulance and rail to permanent hospitals. Chimborazo Hospital outside Richmond had about 8,000 beds.

Thomas McParlin directed the U.S. medical system

The Army of the Potomac medical director had 60 officers, about 2,300 enlisted personnel, nearly 800 ambulances, and support from hundreds of regimental and brigade surgeons.

U.S. evacuation used three main levels of care

A wounded soldier moved through a regimental or brigade station, a division or corps hospital, and then a general hospital. Washington had sixteen general hospitals and Alexandria had seven.

Changing the supply base disrupted evacuation

After the Wilderness, the army abandoned the shorter Culpeper rail route and established a base at Belle Plain. Wounded soldiers first had to pass through Fredericksburg.

The army improvised a depot hospital

Fredericksburg became a fourth stage between field hospitals and transportation to Washington. No organization was ready when the first wounded arrived.

About 14,000 wounded accumulated in Fredericksburg

Damaged buildings, heavy rain, few surgeons, poor roads, missing bridges, and competition with supply traffic delayed treatment and movement by May 12.

Civilian relief organizations supplied staff and material

The U.S. Sanitary Commission and U.S. Christian Commission sent doctors, nurses, ambulances, food, bedding, and other supplies to Fredericksburg.

Cornelia Hancock organized immediate improvements

Hancock found wounded men lying in water inside damaged churches. Her work party cut church pews into improvised cots to lift patients off the wet floors.

Clara Barton carried the emergency to Washington

After seeing conditions in Fredericksburg, Barton informed Senator Henry Wilson. Wilson pressed Secretary of War Edwin Stanton to intervene.

Montgomery Meigs reorganized the depot

Stanton sent Quartermaster General Meigs with broad authority. Meigs opened civilian buildings, added a pontoon bridge, accelerated railroad repairs, and requested supplies.

Evacuation improved by May 23

Patients moved by wagon to Belle Plain and by rail to Aquia Creek. The army then shifted its depot hospitals to Port Royal and White House Landing as the front moved south.

Anesthesia was available but infection control was not

Surgeons used ether and chloroform and could perform complex operations. Germ theory and effective antiseptic practice were not yet part of routine care.

Captured wounded could remain without adequate supplies

Union surgeons sometimes stayed with patients who fell into Confederate hands, but food and medical material were limited. William Watson cared for about 275 men in the Wilderness and repeatedly requested supplies.

More than 19,000 soldiers became prisoners

The formal exchange system had broken down over the treatment of Black U.S. soldiers and disputed Confederate paroles. Capture therefore meant transport to a prison camp rather than prompt exchange.

The Wilderness produced the largest captures

Dense woods separated individuals and units. Soldiers often had only seconds to decide whether escape was possible or surrender was the safer choice.

Breakthroughs captured entire formations

The U.S. assault at the Mule Shoe took about 3,000 Confederates. Prisoners first went to provost guards and holding areas before marching away from the battlefield.

Prisoners entered the armies’ transport systems

About 7,500 Confederates moved through Grant’s supply corridor toward Point Lookout. Many U.S. prisoners marched to Orange Court House and went by rail toward Andersonville.

The campaign left at least 12,000 dead

At Spotsylvania and Cold Harbor, soldiers occupied trenches near unburied bodies for days. Immediate burial was often incomplete because the armies moved or remained under fire.

Postwar details recovered scattered remains

In 1865 Captain J. M. Moore’s detail found bodies that had been buried temporarily, exposed, or never buried. Identification was often impossible after a year outdoors.

Permanent cemeteries replaced battlefield graves

Union remains were moved to Fredericksburg and Cold Harbor national cemeteries. The current National Park Service count is 15,243 burials at Fredericksburg, at least 85 percent unidentified. Confederate remains went to separate cemeteries established by local memorial associations.
