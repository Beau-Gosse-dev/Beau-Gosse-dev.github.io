LEE STRIKES NORTH

The Maryland Campaign opened. Robert E. Lee kept the strategic initiative after Second Manassas and moved the Army of Northern Virginia north of the Potomac before the United States army had fully reorganized. No battle occurred in this episode; the campaign’s military, political, diplomatic, and emancipation consequences remained undecided

Lee faces a post-victory decision

People and command

United States

Commander in chief and national war policyPresident Abraham LincolnMade the decision to issue an emancipation proclamation under his war powers, then accepted William H. Seward’s advice to wait for military success before publication
General in chief, United States ArmyMaj. Gen. Henry W. HalleckCoordinated the armies from Washington while Federal forces reorganized after Second Manassas and Chantilly
Army of the Potomac and forces defending WashingtonMaj. Gen. George B. McClellanAbsorbed units from John Pope’s discontinued Army of Virginia and began moving the reorganized field army out of Washington on September 5
State and Navy DepartmentsSecretaries William H. Seward and Gideon WellesHeard Lincoln’s emancipation decision on July 13; Seward advised withholding the proclamation until it could follow a military success

Confederacy

Confederate commander in chiefPresident Jefferson DavisReceived Lee’s September 3 statement of intent and supported the Maryland movement; Lee began the crossing before a reply arrived
Army of Northern VirginiaGen. Robert E. LeeChose to retain the initiative by entering Maryland and seeking battle away from the Washington fortifications
Right Wing, Army of Northern VirginiaMaj. Gen. James LongstreetLed one of Lee’s two principal infantry groupings during the movement from northern Virginia into Maryland
Left Wing, Army of Northern VirginiaMaj. Gen. Thomas J. “Stonewall” JacksonLed the other principal infantry grouping; his staff and trains crossed amid the episode’s account of the wagon jam at the ford
Cavalry DivisionMaj. Gen. J. E. B. StuartScreened Confederate movements; staff officer Maj. Heros von Borcke later described the cavalry crossing the Potomac
