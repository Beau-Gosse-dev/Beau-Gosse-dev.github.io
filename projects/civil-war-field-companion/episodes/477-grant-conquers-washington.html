Episode 477 · March 8–26, 1864 · Washington, D.C.; Brandy Station and Culpeper, Virginia; Nashville, Tennessee

GRANT CONQUERS WASHINGTON

Grant arrived in Washington on March 8, received his lieutenant general’s commission on March 9, and became general-in-chief on March 10. Halleck moved to the new position of army chief of staff, and Sherman replaced Grant as commander of the Military Division of the Mississippi. After meeting Meade, Grant kept him in command of the Army of the Potomac. Grant also abandoned his plan to direct the war from the West, established field headquarters near Culpeper, and maintained direct access to Lincoln and Stanton while preparing coordinated offensives

What happened

Grant arrived on March 8

Secretary of War Edwin Stanton summoned Grant from Nashville. He reached Washington with his son Fred and staff officers John Rawlins and Cyrus Comstock.

No delegation met the train

A mix-up left Grant unrecognized at the station, so he and his son walked to Willard’s Hotel while his staff reported to the War Department.

Willard’s staff initially failed to recognize him

Grant accepted a small room until the desk clerk read his signature; the hotel then moved him to its best available suite.

The public recognized Grant

Hotel guests cheered the general, surrounded his table, and prevented him from finishing dinner.

Grant’s military reputation was national

His record included Forts Henry and Donelson, Shiloh, Vicksburg, and Chattanooga.

Congress honored Grant

The House and Senate thanked Grant and his army and authorized a gold medal after Chattanooga.

Congress revived the lieutenant general rank

Elihu Washburne introduced legislation for a rank previously associated with George Washington, with Grant understood as the intended appointee.

Political groups considered Grant for president

Some Democrats promoted him as a possible candidate in 1864, although Grant repeatedly rejected political office.

Lincoln evaluated Grant’s political intentions

Lincoln supported the promotion after David Hunter reported that Grant did not seek the presidency and backed the administration.

Grant was confirmed and summoned

After the House passed the bill on February 29, Lincoln nominated Grant and the Senate confirmed him.

Grant planned to avoid Washington headquarters

He told Sherman that he would not accept a role requiring him to remain in the capital and initially intended to continue directing operations from the West.

Grant went to the White House

Simon Cameron and James Morehead escorted him from Willard’s to Lincoln’s public reception on March 8.

Grant met Lincoln

The president greeted him in front of the assembled crowd and Secretary of State William Seward introduced him to Mary Lincoln.

The East Room crowd surrounded Grant

Visitors pressed forward to shake his hand, and he reluctantly stood on a sofa so the crowd could see him.

Lincoln prepared Grant for the ceremony

Lincoln asked Grant to acknowledge other generals and build good relations with the Army of the Potomac in his acceptance remarks.

Grant received the commission on March 9

Lincoln presented the lieutenant general’s commission in front of the cabinet and emphasized the responsibility attached to the rank.

Grant accepted the appointment

He credited the armies that had fought for the Union and accepted responsibility for meeting the government’s expectations.

Grant became general-in-chief

On March 10, Lincoln formally placed Grant over all U.S. armies. Sherman took Grant’s western command and Halleck became chief of staff.

General headquarters moved out of Washington

Unlike Halleck, Grant would locate his headquarters wherever he was operating rather than maintain a permanent War Department office.

Grant visited Meade at Brandy Station

He and Rawlins went to Army of the Potomac headquarters immediately after leaving Washington.

Grant decided to remain in the East

Direct meetings with Lincoln and Stanton and closer supervision of military administration made proximity to Washington valuable.

Grant reconsidered removing Meade

Meade offered to step aside if Grant preferred a western commander, and Grant treated the offer as evidence of Meade’s character.

Meade retained command

Grant kept Meade at the head of the Army of the Potomac and planned to place his own headquarters with the field army.

Grant informed Lincoln of the arrangement

He told the president and cabinet that he would remain in the East but left that night to confer with Sherman and other western generals.

Grant returned from Nashville

He came back to Washington on March 23 after discussing the upcoming campaigns with western commanders.

Headquarters opened near Culpeper

On March 26, Grant established a separate headquarters near Meade and refined the coordinated operations scheduled to begin in early May.

Grant briefed Lincoln and Stanton

He made repeated trips to Washington and gave civilian leaders the main outline of the eastern and western plans.

Lincoln delegated operational responsibility

Lincoln wanted Grant to take responsibility and act decisively rather than require the president to approve and own every military decision.
