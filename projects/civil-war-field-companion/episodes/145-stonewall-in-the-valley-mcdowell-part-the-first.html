STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: McDOWELL (Part the First)

Operational prelude; no battle is fought in this episode. Jackson left Richard Ewell near Swift Run Gap to watch Nathaniel Banks, moved his own force by a circuitous march and rail journey to Staunton, joined Edward “Allegheny” Johnson, and advanced west against Robert Milroy. On May 7, Milroy withdrew his outposts toward McDowell and called Robert Schenck for support, setting the conditions for the Battle of McDowell on May 8
Episode scopeJackson’s movement from Elk Run Valley to the approaches of McDowell, April 19–May 7, 1862The episode ends with the opposing forces in position on the night of May 7. The Battle of McDowell itself is covered in the next episode.
Operational resultJackson concentrated against Milroy while Ewell watched BanksThe movement applied Jackson’s preferred concept of using separate Confederate forces against isolated United States columns in sequence.
April 19 positionElk Run Valley above Conrad’s Store, beside Swift Run GapJackson’s men crossed the South Fork of the Shenandoah at Conrad’s Store after covering about 50 miles in three days. Conrad’s Store is now Elkton.
Why Swift Run Gap matteredConcealment, a defensible position, a route across the Blue Ridge, and a base for striking Banks’s flankThe location also kept Jackson in communication with Ewell east of the Blue Ridge.
Banks’s assessmentJackson had left the ValleyBanks told Secretary of War Edwin Stanton on April 22 that Jackson had permanently abandoned the Valley and on April 30 that nothing more remained for his command to do there. Jackson was still concealed near Swift Run Gap.
Banks’s forward positionHarrisonburg by April 25United States troops had occupied New Market and Harrisonburg while Jackson yielded the central Valley without offering battle.
Staunton’s importanceRail and supply center for the southern Shenandoah ValleyThe Virginia Central Railroad connected Staunton eastward, while the Virginia and Tennessee Railroad system and roads farther south tied the region to the Confederate interior. A Federal advance on Staunton threatened those communications.
Edward Johnson’s forceAbout 2,800 men west of StauntonPressed by Milroy and threatened by Frémont’s Mountain Department, Johnson withdrew from Camp Allegheny to the West View area on April 20.
Ewell’s divisionRoughly 8,500 men east of the Blue RidgeThe episode uses 8,500. A U.S. Army historical study gives 8,884. Ewell was the large mobile Confederate force available to support Jackson or the Rappahannock line.
Lee’s roleMilitary adviser in Richmond, not yet an army field commanderRobert E. Lee encouraged Jackson and Ewell to seek opportunities that might keep United States forces away from Fredericksburg and Richmond, but overlapping lines of authority involving Joseph E. Johnston remained unclear.
Jackson’s April 29 optionsThree possible offensivesHis preferred option was to leave Ewell near Swift Run Gap, join Edward Johnson, defeat Milroy before Frémont could reinforce him, then return east and combine against Banks. Other options involved an attack near New Market or a move through the Luray Valley toward Front Royal.
Lee’s May 1 replyJackson could choose the planLee left the selection to Jackson’s judgment and cautioned him against operations that would not improve the larger strategic situation.
Uncertain plan transmissionEwell knew his immediate assignment, but not Jackson’s full designThe episode, following Peter Cozzens, says it is uncertain how much Jackson disclosed in their April 29 and April 30 meetings. A later message also leaves room for doubt about when Jackson finally committed to the Milroy operation.
April 30 substitutionEwell moved into Jackson’s vacated camps near Swift Run GapThis maintained a Confederate presence opposite Banks while Jackson moved south toward Port Republic.
Jackson’s routeConrad’s Store → Port Republic → Brown’s Gap → Mechum’s Station → StauntonMost of the force returned west by the Virginia Central Railroad from Mechum’s Station; some units marched. The transcript’s “Meacham Station” is normalized here to the historical spelling “Mechum’s Station.”
Mud delayOnly about five miles for much of the army on April 30Jackson and his staff covered about 13 miles, but torrential rain turned the unimproved road toward Port Republic into a quagmire. Artillery and wagons lagged behind the infantry through May 2.
Intent of the circuitous marchDeception was useful, but its exact timing is uncertainThe episode describes a deliberate attempt to make Banks think Jackson was leaving for Richmond. The U.S. Army study notes that Jackson’s May 3 message to Ewell creates doubt about whether the Milroy operation had been fixed from the beginning.
Federal intelligenceWilliams and Banks inferred Jackson’s real direction by May 2According to the episode, Alpheus Williams concluded that Jackson was moving to join Edward Johnson. This undercuts the idea that the exhausting detour achieved complete operational surprise.
Washington’s May 1 decisionBanks ordered back toward Strasburg; Shields ordered eastLincoln accepted Banks’s judgment that the Valley mission was complete. The removal of Shields reduced the force Banks would later have available against Jackson and Ewell.
Staunton concentrationJackson’s force assembled by the evening of May 5Jackson reached Staunton on May 4. Ashby’s cavalry restricted movement into and out of town in an effort to preserve secrecy.
VMI cadetsAbout 200 arrived at Staunton on May 5The corps was attached temporarily to the Stonewall Brigade. The cadets were not expected to fight at McDowell, but they could perform support duties and gain field experience.
Advance on May 7Approximately 10,000–11,600 Confederates moved westThe episode describes about 10,000 men in the combined commands. The National Park Service gives 11,600 for Jackson after joining Johnson. Strength accounting varies by date and by whether all troops present or only effective troops are counted.
First contactRogers Tollgate, 13 miles east of McDowellJohnson’s advance encountered Milroy’s pickets on the Staunton–Parkersburg Turnpike during the afternoon of May 7.
Milroy’s responseFall back to McDowell and summon SchenckMilroy understood that Jackson and Johnson were combining against him. Schenck was at Franklin, about 30 miles away.
Night of May 7Johnson at Shaw’s Fork; Jackson at Buffalo GapThe episode places Johnson about six miles east of McDowell and Jackson about ten miles away. These are approximate camp locations, not precise unit plots.

What happened

The episode resumes after Kernstown

Banks followed Jackson cautiously after March 23. When Banks pressed harder in mid-April, Jackson abandoned Rude’s Hill and yielded New Market, Harrisonburg, and the Valley north of the North River.

Jackson turns toward Conrad’s Store

On April 19 the Valley force crossed the South Fork of the Shenandoah and camped in Elk Run Valley between the river and Swift Run Gap after marching about 50 miles in three days.

Banks loses track of Jackson

Jackson’s shift east of the main Valley corridor concealed his location and intentions from the advancing United States command.

Banks reports that Jackson has permanently left

On April 22 Banks confidently told Secretary of War Stanton that Jackson had abandoned the Shenandoah Valley.

United States troops occupy Harrisonburg

Banks’s advance reached Harrisonburg on April 25. He believed Jackson was moving east to reinforce Joseph E. Johnston against McClellan.

Banks says the Valley mission is complete

His April 30 message to Stanton stated that nothing more remained to be done in the Valley, an assessment that helped prompt Washington to remove troops.

The logic of Jackson’s position is explained

Elk Run Valley offered concealment and defense, controlled Swift Run Gap, kept open contact across the Blue Ridge, and put Jackson near Banks’s eastern flank if the Federals moved on Staunton.

Staunton is identified as the southern Valley key

The town’s rail and road connections made it a major Confederate supply and communications center. Its loss would expose the upper Valley and rail links farther south.

Edward Johnson guards the western approaches

The episode reviews the approximately 2,800-man command that had fought at Allegheny Mountain and then withdrawn to West View under pressure from Milroy and Frémont.

Ewell gives Jackson another option

Richard S. Ewell’s division, about 8,500 strong and east of the Blue Ridge, could reinforce Jackson or help protect the Confederate line toward Richmond.

Lee favors mobility and concentration

From Richmond, Robert E. Lee judged that Jackson’s location preserved freedom of maneuver and the possibility of uniting with Ewell while McClellan threatened the capital.

Lee’s limited authority is noted

The hosts describe Lee as Jefferson Davis’s senior military adviser rather than a field army commander. He could strongly recommend operations, but the command structure remained complicated.

Lee links Valley action to Fredericksburg

His April 21 communication emphasized that McDowell’s force at Fredericksburg threatened Richmond and that any removal of Banks’s troops from the Valley could strengthen that advance.

Jackson proposes three offensive plans

On April 29 he offered alternatives to Lee. Jackson preferred leaving Ewell to watch Banks while joining Edward Johnson to attack Milroy before Frémont could concentrate.

Lee leaves the choice to Jackson

The May 1 reply authorized Jackson to select among the three plans. Jackson had already begun putting his preferred general concept into motion.

Ewell is summoned to Swift Run Gap

Jackson met Ewell on April 29 and again on April 30. Ewell learned that he would replace Jackson near the gap while Jackson moved toward Staunton.

How much Ewell knew remains uncertain

The episode states that Jackson discussed the Banks threat only in general terms. Ewell was unsure whom Jackson meant to attack and did not yet have confidence in him.

The episode pauses for an Ewell profile

The hosts describe the 45-year-old West Point graduate as experienced, eccentric, profane, personally modest, and respected by his men. The automated transcript repeatedly renders his surname as “Yule.”

Ewell reaches Jackson’s empty camps

On the evening of April 30 Ewell’s men crossed the Blue Ridge expecting to meet the Valley force, but Jackson had already departed and disclosed little about his destination.

Jackson starts the circuitous march

The Valley force left Elk Run Valley for Port Republic, intending to cross Brown’s Gap and reach the Virginia Central Railroad at Mechum’s Station.

Rain destroys the timetable

Torrential rain turned the unimproved road to Port Republic into deep mud. Jackson and his staff covered 13 miles, but much of the army managed only about five.

A 10th Virginia private records the strain

Joseph Coffman wrote that Jackson was a hard master, that his feet had failed, and that he wished peace would be declared. The diary entry anchors the march in a soldier’s immediate experience.

The artillery and trains fall behind

Wind and rain continued on May 1. Some infantry reached Port Republic by nightfall, but the guns and wagons remained stretched out on the road.

The weather finally clears

The force continued struggling on May 2; sunshine returned late that day, and May 3 opened clear.

The column crosses Brown’s Gap

On May 3 the exhausted force passed over the Blue Ridge and reached Mechum’s Station by evening on a dry turnpike.

Federal officers infer Jackson’s purpose

The episode says Alpheus Williams concluded by the afternoon of May 2 that Jackson was moving to join Edward Johnson; Banks agreed.

Lincoln orders Banks back

On May 1 Washington instructed Banks to withdraw down the Valley toward Strasburg, accepting his report that the forward mission was finished.

Shields is detached toward Fredericksburg

Orders on May 2 sent Shields’s division east to join McDowell, leaving Banks with substantially less strength in the Valley.

The trains turn west

Soldiers who thought they were leaving the Valley for Richmond discovered that the railroad cars were carrying them back through the Blue Ridge toward Staunton.

Jackson reaches Staunton

He arrived on May 4 and reassured residents who had heard rumors that his army was abandoning the Valley. The automated transcript incorrectly renders Staunton as “Danton” here.

Ashby closes the town

Jackson ordered Turner Ashby’s cavalry to restrict movement into and out of Staunton in an attempt to conceal the concentration.

The Valley force is assembled

By the evening of May 5 most units had arrived by rail, while some had completed the trip on foot.

The VMI cadets arrive

About 200 cadets came to Staunton with engineer Thomas H. Williamson and were attached temporarily to the Stonewall Brigade for support service.

Jackson and Johnson advance west

At dawn on May 7 the combined force left Staunton on the Staunton–Parkersburg Turnpike, with Edward Johnson’s men leading.

Johnson reaches Milroy’s pickets

The Confederate advance encountered Federal outposts at Rogers Tollgate, near the foot of Shenandoah Mountain and about 13 miles east of McDowell.

Milroy recognizes the concentration

Scouts told him that Jackson and Johnson had united and were moving against his brigade.

Milroy asks Schenck for help

He sent word to Robert C. Schenck, whose brigade was at Franklin roughly 30 miles from McDowell.

Milroy falls back to McDowell

Expecting Schenck to reinforce him, Milroy withdrew his command from the forward positions and concentrated near the town.

The Confederates camp short of town

On the night of May 7 Johnson camped at Shaw’s Fork, about six miles east of McDowell, while Jackson remained farther back near Buffalo Gap.

The stage is set for May 8

The episode stops before the Battle of McDowell. No leader death or mortal wound occurs within this installment’s narrative scope.
