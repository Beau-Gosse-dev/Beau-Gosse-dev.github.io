ANTIETAM (Part the Third)

The armies concentrated for battle without a general engagement on September 16. George B. McClellan postponed his main attack until September 17 while Robert E. Lee strengthened a four-mile defensive line west of Antietam Creek. Thomas J. “Stonewall” Jackson and John G. Walker reached Lee during the day; late in the day, elements of the United States I and XII Corps crossed the creek and made contact with the Confederate left
Episode scopeThe armies at Sharpsburg on Tuesday, September 16, 1862The episode explains McClellan’s decision not to attack on September 15 or 16, Lee’s decision to stand, and the terrain and Confederate positions. McClellan’s full battle plan is reserved for the next episode.
Morning conditionsHeavy fog concealed the Confederate positionAt 7 a.m. McClellan informed Henry W. Halleck that the fog had prevented him from determining Lee’s strength and that he would attack after the situation developed.
McClellan’s expectationLee might have withdrawn during the nightWhen the fog cleared, the Army of Northern Virginia remained on the heights west of Antietam Creek.
Morning force estimate in the episodeAbout 40,000 United States troops near roughly 15,000 ConfederatesThese are the episode’s estimates for troops immediately available early on September 16, not final battle strengths. Strength figures vary according to time and whether present, effective, or engaged personnel are counted.
McClellan’s estimate of LeeAt least 30,000 at Sharpsburg and 40,000–50,000 at Harpers FerryThe estimate greatly overstated both parts of Lee’s divided army and made a Confederate reinforcement or flank attack through Pleasant Valley appear more dangerous than it was.
Franklin’s positionVI Corps remained in Pleasant Valley for most of September 16McClellan retained William B. Franklin west of South Mountain to watch for a Confederate move from Harpers Ferry. He ordered the corps toward Sharpsburg at 6 p.m.
Jackson as the perceived threatReinforce Lee, strike Franklin, or attempt bothMcClellan did not know how quickly Jackson could move after the surrender of Harpers Ferry and treated the Confederate wing as a possible threat to either front.
United States concentrationNearly 60,000 troops by the end of September 16 in the episode’s estimateMost of the Army of the Potomac was within supporting distance by Tuesday evening, apart from Franklin’s VI Corps moving from Pleasant Valley.
Confederate concentrationNo more than about 25,000 after Jackson’s arrival in the episode’s estimateJackson and Walker reached Sharpsburg during September 16. McLaws and A. P. Hill were still returning from Harpers Ferry and would arrive on September 17.
Decision by noonThe general assault was scheduled for September 17After reconnaissance and consideration during the morning, McClellan circulated orders shortly after noon for an attack the next day.
Operational consequence of delayMcClellan lost a chance to attack a smaller part of Lee’s armyThe delay also allowed the Army of the Potomac to concentrate and placed nearly all of Lee’s army within reach on September 17. This was an unintended consequence, not McClellan’s stated reason for waiting.
Lee’s decisionStand at Sharpsburg instead of withdrawing across the PotomacLee sought to recover the initiative after South Mountain and believed McClellan would return to cautious methods. The episode presents this as a calculated risk rather than a position forced by geography alone.
Risk to LeeA divided, depleted army with the Potomac at its backConfederate troops were exhausted and reduced by straggling and desertion. Boteler’s Ford near Shepherdstown was the principal nearby route of retreat.
Defensive lineAn arc about four miles long from northwest of Sharpsburg to the lower AntietamLee used the ridges west of the creek. Jackson commanded the left, Longstreet the right, and the Hagerstown Turnpike corridor linked the northern battlefield to Sharpsburg.
Northern anchorNicodemus HeightsJ. E. B. Stuart’s cavalry and horse artillery occupied the high ground northwest of the Dunker Church. Jubal A. Early’s infantry brigade also supported this critical sector.
Dunker Church plateauKey high ground at the Hagerstown Turnpike and Smoketown RoadUnited States artillery placed there could fire along the Confederate line north of Sharpsburg. The church had been built for the German Baptist Brethren in 1852–53 on land donated by Samuel Mumma.
Northern approachA 400–500-yard corridor between the East and West WoodsMiller’s cornfield obstructed much of the open approach north of the church. Hollows, rises, stone outcrops, crops, and woodlots limited observation across terrain that appeared open from the Federal side.
Center of the lineThe Roulette farm marked the bend between sectorsFrom Jackson’s left the Confederate position turned south toward D. H. Hill’s and Longstreet’s troops covering the roads and high ground around Sharpsburg.
Southern outpostRobert Toombs’s brigade above the Rohrbach BridgeThe brigade was positioned forward to delay a United States crossing. Snavely’s Ford farther south offered a route around the bridge position.
Evening contactI and XII Corps crossed the upper Antietam and skirmished with Lee’s leftThe National Park Service timeline places contact at about 6 p.m. This limited action set the northern approach for the battle but did not become a general engagement on September 16.
McClellan’s command testHis first major battle planned and directed from start to finishEarlier major engagements under McClellan had generally placed the Confederates on the tactical offensive or left detailed battlefield direction to subordinate commanders.
Pressure from WashingtonLincoln urged McClellan to destroy Lee’s army if possibleA September 15 telegram congratulated McClellan but emphasized the unfinished task. Winfield Scott’s message the next day likewise treated South Mountain as only part of the campaign.
Senior leader casualties in scopeNo senior leader was killed or mortally wounded on September 16Jesse L. Reno’s mortal wound at Fox’s Gap on September 14 remained operationally important because Jacob D. Cox commanded IX Corps at Antietam.
Recommended book“The Battle of Antietam: The Bloodiest Day” by Ted AlexanderThe episode recommends the Antietam National Battlefield historian’s study for its attention to the battle, Sharpsburg’s civilians, and battlefield preservation.

What happened

The September 15 situation is recapped

Harpers Ferry has surrendered, Lee has chosen to stand at Sharpsburg, and the Army of the Potomac has reached Antietam Creek.

Fog hides Lee’s position

The morning of September 16 opens quietly under heavy fog, and McClellan initially considers whether Lee has withdrawn.

McClellan reports to Halleck

At 7 a.m. McClellan says he cannot yet determine the Confederate strength and will attack after the situation develops.

The early numerical opportunity is stated

The episode estimates about 40,000 United States troops near the field against perhaps 15,000 Confederates early Tuesday.

Franklin remains in Pleasant Valley

VI Corps watches for a Confederate movement from Harpers Ferry instead of joining the main concentration at Sharpsburg.

McClellan overestimates both Confederate forces

He places at least 30,000 with Lee and Longstreet and another 40,000–50,000 with Jackson at Harpers Ferry.

Actual concentration is contrasted

The episode estimates that McClellan has nearly 60,000 by day’s end while Lee has no more than about 25,000 after Jackson arrives.

The attack is postponed

After reconnaissance and deliberation, McClellan circulates orders shortly after noon for a general assault on September 17.

Cox expects battle

Jacob D. Cox, commanding IX Corps after Reno’s death, later remembered September 16 as a day of tedious inaction.

The delay’s unintended effect is explained

Waiting allows both McClellan’s own army and most of Lee’s separated army to concentrate for the battle the next day.

Lee’s decision to fight is examined

The episode presents criticism of Lee’s choice to stand with the Potomac behind him and a badly depleted army.

Lee bets on McClellan’s caution

Lee expects McClellan to return to the deliberate methods that had characterized earlier operations.

Lee commits to Sharpsburg

Despite the risk and his army’s condition, Lee chooses battle in an effort to preserve the campaign’s purpose and initiative.

The battlefield’s ridges and streams are introduced

The terrain between the Potomac and Antietam Creek appears ordinary from a distance but contains folds, woods, roads, and fields that restrict observation.

The four-mile Confederate arc takes shape

Lee’s defensive line extends from northwest of Sharpsburg south toward the lower Antietam.

Jackson reaches Sharpsburg

Jackson arrives around midday after the severe march from Harpers Ferry and takes command of the Confederate left.

Nicodemus Heights anchors the left

Stuart’s cavalry and horse artillery occupy the high ground, supported by Early’s infantry brigade.

The Dunker Church becomes the key landmark

The episode locates the church at the Hagerstown Turnpike and Smoketown Road and explains the artillery value of its plateau.

The northern approach is defined

A narrow open corridor, the East and West Woods, and Miller’s standing corn shape the approach to Jackson’s position.

The line turns south toward Longstreet

The Roulette farm marks the bend toward the roads and high ground covering Sharpsburg and the lower battlefield.

The lower bridge outpost is described

A single Confederate brigade guards the heights above Rohrbach Bridge while Snavely’s Ford presents a route around it.

McClellan’s tactical problem is framed

He must attack ground chosen by Lee with an army whose corps and senior commanders have limited experience operating together.

Antietam becomes McClellan’s command test

The episode identifies it as the first major battle that he would both plan and personally direct from beginning to end.

Lee’s stand cancels an easy vindication

McClellan can no longer treat South Mountain alone as proof that the campaign has been won; he must confront Lee again.

Lincoln presses for destruction of Lee’s army

The president’s telegram blesses McClellan’s success but emphasizes that the Confederate army remains the objective.

Winfield Scott echoes the unfinished task

Scott’s reply also declines to treat South Mountain as a complete victory while Lee still stands in Maryland.

The book recommendation closes the episode

The hosts recommend Ted Alexander’s “The Battle of Antietam: The Bloodiest Day.”
