Episode 483 · May 6–7, 1864 · Orange Plank Road, Brock Road, Orange Turnpike, and the Wilderness of Spotsylvania, Virginia

THE WILDERNESS (Part the Fifth)

Longstreet’s flank attack drove Hancock’s II Corps back to the Brock Road defenses, but Confederate friendly fire then disabled Longstreet and halted the attack. Lee’s later frontal assault failed. Gordon’s evening attack broke two U.S. brigades but came too late and with too few troops to change the result. Grant treated the battle as a draw and ordered the army southeast toward Spotsylvania Court House

What happened

Longstreet prepared a flank attack

Martin L. Smith found an unfinished railroad south of Orange Plank Road that led toward the unguarded left of Hancock’s II Corps.

Sorrel received four brigades

Longstreet ordered G. Moxley Sorrel to collect four scattered brigades, move them along the railroad bed, and wait until all were ready before attacking.

The attack struck Hancock’s left

The Confederates came from the south against U.S. troops facing west. Two brigades on the exposed flank broke, uncovering the units nearer Orange Plank Road.

Longstreet attacked from the front

Troops astride Orange Plank Road advanced as the flanking column hit, forcing Hancock’s command back toward the Brock Road intersection.

II Corps rallied behind fieldworks

Soldiers stopped at the Brock Road defenses. Officers and color bearers reorganized the units in roughly half an hour.

Friendly fire stopped the Confederate advance

The pressure eased after soldiers of the 12th Virginia mistook Longstreet’s mounted party for U.S. troops and fired into it.

Micah Jenkins was killed and Longstreet wounded

The volley killed Jenkins, a staff officer, and a courier. A bullet passed through Longstreet’s shoulder and injured his neck.

Longstreet left the field for months

Surgeons stopped the bleeding, but he could not return to the Army of Northern Virginia until October 1864.

Lee resumed the attack after 4 p.m.

After taking control of Longstreet’s corps, Lee ordered a frontal assault on the Brock Road defenses.

The frontal assault failed

Confederates entered the works at a few points but were driven out by U.S. musket and artillery fire.

Hancock canceled the planned U.S. attack

His troops were exhausted and short of ammunition after stopping Lee’s assault, so a proposed 6 p.m. advance did not occur.

Gordon proposed a northern flank attack

John B. Gordon identified the exposed end of Sedgwick’s VI Corps above the Orange Turnpike and sought permission to attack it.

Scouts confirmed the U.S. flank was open

Gordon’s scouts found no U.S. line opposite his brigade and reported that the Federal works ended farther south.

Gordon personally checked the position

He approached close enough to see U.S. soldiers at the end of their breastworks and confirmed that no line protected their flank.

Early opposed the proposal

Jubal Early argued that Gordon lacked enough men and might be outflanked by Burnside or other U.S. forces reported nearby.

Ewell initially refused permission

Richard Ewell accepted Early’s objections and delayed the attack until the situation became clearer.

Robert Johnston’s brigade arrived

Johnston extended the Confederate left beyond the U.S. flank. Reports also established that Burnside was not positioned behind Sedgwick.

Three Confederate brigades moved at sunset

Gordon attacked with his own brigade, Johnston’s brigade, and John Hoffman’s brigade—about 4,000 men in total.

Shaler’s brigade collapsed

The surprise attack struck Alexander Shaler’s brigade first, then forced Truman Seymour’s neighboring brigade to withdraw.

Shaler and Seymour were captured

Both brigade commanders rode toward the fighting and attempted to rally their troops before Confederates took them prisoner.

The attack did not change the battle

Only Gordon’s brigade struck the flank directly. Dense woods and darkness disrupted the other two brigades while Sedgwick formed a new defensive line.

A second attack failed

Gordon tried again around 10 p.m., but the reinforced U.S. position stopped the advance.

Grant assessed the battle as a draw

He concluded that neither army had won a clear advantage and rejected another attack against prepared Confederate fieldworks.

Grant ordered a march to Spotsylvania

At 6:30 a.m. on May 7, he directed Meade to prepare the Army of the Potomac for a night movement southeast around Lee’s right.

U.S. soldiers saw Grant riding south

Troops along Brock Road understood that the army was continuing the campaign instead of withdrawing north and cheered as Grant passed.
