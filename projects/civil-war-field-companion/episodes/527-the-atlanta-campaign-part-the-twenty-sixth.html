What happened

Sherman ruled out another attack at Kennesaw

After the June 27 assaults, Sherman decided to move south around the Confederate left even though the maneuver temporarily separated much of his army from the railroad.

Final orders were issued on July 1

Schofield was to secure the ground toward Nickajack Creek, McPherson was to move south and join him, and Thomas was to continue holding the existing U.S. line.

Schofield opened the movement

At 6 a.m., Milo Hascall’s division advanced along the Sandtown Road, pushed back Confederate cavalry, and occupied a road junction near the Moss House.

Jacob Cox reinforced the road junction

XXIII Corps troops built earthworks. Hascall reported that heat and dust caused many men to collapse.

Thomas and McPherson concealed the movement

Skirmishers and artillery demonstrated against the Kennesaw lines while Schofield extended south.

Morgan L. Smith marched to Schofield

Smith’s XV Corps division left at 4 a.m. on July 2, completed an 11-mile march, and joined Schofield near the Moss House by 11 a.m.

Schofield secured the advanced position

About 13,000 men held ground four miles beyond the Confederate left and six miles from the Chattahoochee.

U.S. troops increased pressure along the original line

John Newton’s division bombarded and skirmished while 40 to 50 U.S. guns fired on Little Kennesaw.

U.S. cavalry crossed Sweetwater Creek

George Stoneman and Edward McCook pushed to the Chattahoochee southwest of Schofield’s position.

McPherson prepared a night march

Supply trains moved first. XVII Corps was to lead, followed by XVI Corps and XV Corps, with only one baggage wagon per regiment and battery.

McPherson began leaving the U.S. left

Thomas shifted troops into the vacated positions and Benjamin Scribner’s brigade built a refused flank to protect the north end of the line.

Johnston detected the movement

Confederate observers saw large wagon movements. Johnston knew the U.S. armies were moving south and decided the Kennesaw position could not be held.

Johnston ordered a withdrawal

At 1:30 p.m. on July 2, army headquarters issued detailed orders. Artillery would leave at dusk, Loring at 10 p.m., Hardee and Hood at 11, and rear guards at 1 a.m.

Confederate artillery left Little Kennesaw

George Storrs cleared the mountain roads and removed every gun while brush screens concealed the empty embrasures.

Infantry rear guards concealed the retreat

Patrick Cleburne’s division withdrew quietly. Covered trenches, scattered skirmishers, and burning turpentine balls hid movements around Cheatham Hill.

The Army of Tennessee reached Smyrna

The dark, dry night and familiar roads allowed most units to arrive on schedule and begin new earthworks before U.S. troops appeared.

McPherson briefly misread the Confederate activity

He paused his own withdrawal because he thought the movement and reduced campfires might precede a night attack.

U.S. troops confirmed the works were empty

A Confederate deserter crossed the lines. George Thomas informed Sherman at 2:45 a.m. on July 3 that Johnston had withdrawn.
