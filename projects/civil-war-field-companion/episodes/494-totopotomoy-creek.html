What happened

Haw’s Shop clarified the armies’ locations

Confederate prisoners showed Sheridan where Anderson and Ewell were positioned. U.S. prisoners confirmed to Wade Hampton that V and VI Corps had crossed the Pamunkey.

Lee reported an incomplete picture

Lee told Secretary of War James Seddon that Grant appeared to be concentrating near Hanovertown, but he did not yet know the exact U.S. position or strength.

Grant had three possible routes

The U.S. Army could move northwest toward the Virginia Central Railroad, southeast around Lee’s right, or directly against the Confederate line at Totopotomoy Creek.

Lee had little room left to maneuver

Richmond was about nine miles behind the Confederate position. A successful flank movement or breakthrough threatened the capital.

Lee sought troops from Bermuda Hundred

Breckinridge, Pickett, and one Hoke brigade had joined the army, but Lee still faced greater U.S. numbers. He asked for more of Beauregard’s force.

Davis rejected Beauregard’s earlier combined plan

Beauregard had proposed joining Lee for a concentrated blow after Lee withdrew behind the Chickahominy. Davis considered the plan too risky and Beauregard remained reluctant to release troops.

Beauregard initially refused the request

He reported that moving troops away from Bermuda Hundred could expose Richmond and its rail connections to Butler’s Army of the James.

Grant removed troops from Butler instead

A War Department inspection found that about 10,000 men could hold the Bermuda Hundred lines. Grant ordered William F. Smith’s XVIII Corps to join the Army of the Potomac.

Smith boarded transports

On May 28 sufficient vessels arrived before Butler could begin a Petersburg operation. Smith’s command started moving around the Virginia Peninsula to White House Landing.

The Army of the Potomac approached the creek cautiously

Hancock and part of Warren’s corps neared Totopotomoy Creek while Wright remained farther north. Burnside and most of Warren’s corps were still near Haw’s Shop.

Lee formed a line south of Totopotomoy Creek

From west to east the line contained Hill, Breckinridge, Anderson, and the Second Corps under Jubal Early.

Early replaced Ewell

Richard Ewell left because of illness on May 26. Lee made Early the temporary Second Corps commander and did not later return the corps to Ewell.

Lee identified the U.S. left as an opportunity

When VI Corps left the Hanover Court House area, Lee concluded that Grant would move southeast. He authorized Early to attack Warren if the chance looked favorable.

Early shifted his corps toward Bethesda Church

Rodes led the march, followed by Ramseur and Gordon, using new military roads from Shady Grove Church Road toward Old Church Road.

Rodes broke the first U.S. positions

George Doles’s brigade struck west of Bethesda Church. Rodes drove back a skirmish line, a full brigade, and two brigades of Samuel Crawford’s division.

Warren restored the Fifth Corps line

U.S. artillery and arriving infantry stopped the Confederate advance. Early lacked enough troops on the field to exploit the initial retreat.

The planned flank attack was not coordinated

Early wanted Anderson to advance along Old Church Road against Warren’s flank. Communications failed, Anderson did not make the supporting movement, and Early waited.

Willis attacked the U.S. guns

Edward Willis’s brigade cleared one battery, then came under canister from three more batteries and rifle fire from Pennsylvania infantry.

Willis was mortally wounded

His brigade reached within about fifty yards of the U.S. line but suffered casualties approaching sixty percent. Hundreds surrendered after the attack stopped.

Hancock demonstrated along the creek

Grant ordered attacks to reduce pressure on Warren. Hancock’s three divisions advanced near dark, gained little ground, and withdrew after about thirty minutes.

Cavalry fought near Old Cold Harbor

Matthew Butler’s Confederate brigade moved northeast and met Alfred Torbert’s cavalry near Matadequin Creek, threatening the U.S. route to White House Landing.

Torbert drove Butler back

Torbert committed all three brigades of his division against Butler’s one and pursued the Confederates nearly to the Cold Harbor crossroads.

Grant chose another southeastern movement

The Totopotomoy line was too strong for a direct attack. Grant ordered Sheridan to clear the route for Smith and prepared to move around Lee’s right.

Smith added a fifth U.S. infantry corps

About 10,000 XVIII Corps troops had begun landing at White House. Their arrival increased the pressure on Lee’s three infantry corps.

Lee warned that delay would cause disaster

Lee bypassed normal procedure and asked Beauregard directly for reinforcements, then told Davis that Smith would reach Grant before Hoke’s division could arrive.

Hoke’s division received orders to join Lee

Davis ordered Beauregard to send troops. Beauregard had already reconsidered and issued the transfer shortly before the government order arrived.
