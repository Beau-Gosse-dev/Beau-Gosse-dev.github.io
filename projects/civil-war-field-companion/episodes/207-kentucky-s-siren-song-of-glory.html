Episode 207 · July 31–August 25, 1862 · Chattanooga and Knoxville in Tennessee; Cumberland Gap, Rogers Gap, Barbourville, London, Big Hill, Richmond, Lexington, and the Kentucky River line in Kentucky

"KENTUCKY'S SIREN SONG OF GLORY"

Edmund Kirby Smith abandoned the preliminary reduction of Cumberland Gap and led the newly styled Confederate Army of Kentucky north from Knoxville. By bypassing the gap, detaching Carter Stevenson to contain George W. Morgan, and advancing through Barbourville and London, Smith cut the United States garrison’s communications and opened the Old State Road toward Richmond and Lexington. The Confederate movement forced a hurried United States reorganization in Kentucky, but John Hunt Morgan’s promise of mass recruiting proved false in the mountain counties. At the episode’s end Smith had resumed his advance with Patrick Cleburne and Thomas Churchill while Henry Heth remained near Barbourville and Stevenson watched Cumberland Gap

Command relationshipCooperation between separate departmentsBraxton Bragg outranked Smith, but Smith commanded the independent Department of East Tennessee. Bragg could not exercise ordinary continuous command over Smith while their forces operated apart.

First ManassasWounded while leading a late Confederate arrivalSmith took command of his brigade after arriving from the Shenandoah Valley and was wounded during the Confederate counterattack. His performance strengthened his public reputation.
East Tennessee assignmentMilitary defense and internal controlSmith assumed command in East Tennessee in early 1862. He guarded a strategically important railroad region with a large pro-Union population.
John Hunt Morgan’s July raidAbout 900 cavalrymen and an exaggerated recruiting forecastMorgan reported that 25,000 or 30,000 Kentuckians would join a Confederate advance. Smith’s experience in the mountain counties quickly contradicted that claim.
Cumberland Gap garrisonAbout 9,000 United States troops under George W. MorganThe position was supplied well enough to resist a quick reduction. A prolonged siege would have destroyed the speed on which the proposed Nashville operation depended.
Smith’s August 9 proposalBypass the gap and move directly toward LexingtonSmith argued that a northward advance would isolate Morgan while producing more useful strategic and political results than a slow investment of the pass.
Change of objectiveKentucky and Lexington replace Middle Tennessee and NashvilleBragg accepted Smith’s proposal rather than enforcing the original sequence. The decision left the western offensive divided between commanders and routes.
Lexington’s appealPolitical symbol, supply center, and road hubThe city was associated with Henry Clay, John C. Breckinridge, and Mary Todd Lincoln. Confederate planners also expected supplies and recruits in the Bluegrass region.
Army nameConfederate Army of KentuckySmith adopted the name before the advance. Later in the episode, the emergency United States force forming around Lexington received the same name.

Entry into KentuckyRogers Gap, August 16The episode’s route is Rogers Gap, not Cumberland Gap. Smith crossed west of Morgan’s fortified position and moved toward Barbourville.

Immediate operational aimCut Morgan’s supply line to LexingtonStevenson fixed the gap garrison while the main body moved northwest through Barbourville and London, placing itself between Morgan and central Kentucky.
Campaign weatherExtreme heat and a prolonged droughtDust, scarce water, and difficult mountain roads burdened the march and limited the supplies the Confederates expected to gather locally.
Local responseHostile in the mountain countiesSmith wrote that the people were bitterly opposed to his army. The early march supplied no evidence for Morgan’s prediction of tens of thousands of recruits.
Barbourville and LondonSmall United States detachments driven offThe Confederates used superior numbers to clear the route. These actions were not major battles, but they helped isolate Cumberland Gap.

United States vulnerabilityFew trained troops between Smith and LexingtonThe 18th Kentucky Infantry guarded the railroad corridor, while new regiments from Ohio and Indiana arrived with little training.

What happened

Buell’s advance remains tied to the railroad

Don Carlos Buell repairs and guards the Memphis and Charleston Railroad while Confederate cavalry threatens the long supply connection back to Louisville.

Bragg shifts the main Confederate army east

The successful long-distance movement from northern Mississippi places roughly 30,000 Confederates at Chattanooga before Buell can reach the city.

Bragg and Smith meet at Chattanooga

The two major generals discuss cooperation between their separate commands on July 31.

Smith presses for a preliminary attack on Cumberland Gap

He argues that George W. Morgan must be removed before he can join Bragg for an advance against Nashville.

Smith reconsiders the agreement

After returning to Knoxville, he begins moving away from the plan to reduce the gap before joining Bragg.

The episode introduces Edmund Kirby Smith

Smith is 38, Florida-born, and a graduate of the United States Military Academy’s class of 1845.

Smith resigns after Florida secedes

Although personally opposed to secession, he leaves the United States Army and accepts a Confederate commission.

First Manassas establishes Smith’s reputation

His late-arriving brigade joins the Confederate counterattack; Smith is wounded but emerges with increased fame.

East Tennessee offers responsibility but less glory

Smith must defend the region and control a substantial pro-Union population from his Knoxville headquarters.

Kentucky becomes the tempting objective

Political appeals and the prospect of independent success pull Smith away from the Nashville plan.

John Hunt Morgan predicts mass recruitment

After his July raid, Morgan reports that 25,000 or 30,000 Kentuckians will join a Confederate invasion.

Cumberland Gap cannot be reduced quickly

George W. Morgan’s roughly 9,000-man garrison is too strong and well supplied for the short operation Bragg’s timetable requires.

Lexington offers political and material rewards

The Bluegrass city promises supplies, symbolic value, and—in Confederate estimates—new recruits.

Smith proposes the direct move on August 9

He tells Bragg that an advance toward Lexington can isolate Morgan without spending weeks against Cumberland Gap.

Bragg accepts Kentucky as the objective

His reply permits Smith’s independent northern axis and shifts the campaign away from the earlier Nashville concentration.

Smith renames his command

The Army of East Tennessee becomes the Confederate Army of Kentucky as preparations for the invasion conclude.

The army leaves Knoxville

Smith leads the column north on August 14, beginning the Kentucky campaign.

The Confederates enter Kentucky through Rogers Gap

On August 16 Smith bypasses the fortified route through Cumberland Gap while Bragg remains at Chattanooga.

Four unequal divisions make up the field army

Stevenson commands the largest formation, followed by Heth, Churchill, and Cleburne, with Scott’s cavalry screening the movement.

Cleburne commands Bragg’s borrowed brigades

The Irish-born Arkansas officer leads the makeshift 4th Division of about 3,000 men.

The movement targets Morgan’s supply line

Stevenson approaches Cumberland Gap while the other divisions move toward Barbourville and London.

Drought shapes the march

The army enters a region suffering from months of dry weather, scarce water, oppressive heat, and dusty roads.

Smith reports local hostility

His letter from the mountain region contradicts the expectation that Kentuckians will immediately rally to the Confederacy.

Cumberland Gap is isolated

By August 20 Stevenson has Morgan pinned while Smith’s main body controls the roads toward central Kentucky.

Scott wins the skirmish at Big Hill

His cavalry drives away inexperienced United States horsemen on August 23 and scouts the road toward Richmond.

Smith’s advance shocks the United States command

The main force east of the Tennessee River is trapped at the gap, leaving few organized defenders between the Confederates and Lexington.

Halleck reorganizes the Department of the Ohio

Maj. Gen. Horatio G. Wright receives the expanded command as new Ohio and Indiana regiments move south.

A second Army of Kentucky forms at Lexington

The improvised United States force shares the name of Smith’s Confederate army and initially comes under Lew Wallace.

Wallace selects the Kentucky River line

He plans to defend the limited crossings and high bluffs south of Lexington with advice from Cassius M. Clay.

Buell sends commanders north

William Nelson, Charles Cruft, and Mahlon D. Manson leave the Army of the Ohio to help organize Kentucky’s defense.

William “Bull” Nelson enters the crisis

The Kentucky-born former naval officer brings senior rank, local connections, and an aggressive temperament.

Nelson replaces Wallace at Lexington

After quarreling with Wright in Louisville, Nelson assumes control and abandons Wallace’s command arrangement.

Nelson reorganizes the field force

Cruft and Manson receive infantry brigades, while Brig. Gen. James S. Jackson takes the cavalry.

The United States infantry moves south

Nelson orders Manson across the Kentucky River toward Richmond and sends the cavalry toward the Camp Dick Robinson supply area.

Smith decides to press forward

Scott’s report that the way toward Lexington appears open confirms Smith’s intention to continue.

Only part of Smith’s army moves on Richmond

Stevenson stays at Cumberland Gap and Heth at Barbourville while Cleburne, Churchill, and Scott advance.

Smith compares the expedition to Cortés

Writing to his wife, he presents the deep independent advance as a gamble that could establish his military genius.

The main column resumes the march

On August 25, in heat and drought, Smith sets out for Richmond and Lexington.

The episode recommends Joseph H. Parks’s biography

“General Edmund Kirby Smith, C.S.A.” is presented as a useful, if imperfect, book-length study of the commander.
