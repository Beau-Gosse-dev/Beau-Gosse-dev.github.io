What happened

Sherman commanded about 100,000 men

The force was spread from Nashville to Chattanooga, northern Alabama, northwest Georgia, and Knoxville before concentrating for the campaign.

The U.S. command functioned as an army group

Sherman directed the Army of the Cumberland, Army of the Tennessee, and Army of the Ohio.

Thomas commanded the largest army

The Army of the Cumberland contained IV, XIV, and XX Corps plus a cavalry corps.

The Army of the Cumberland had 72,900 men

Each infantry corps had about 21,000 men, the cavalry about 9,000, and the army fielded 130 guns.

McPherson commanded the Army of the Tennessee

Its field organization drew on XV, XVI, and XVII Corps, but several divisions were absent.

Two XVII Corps divisions had not reached the front

Francis P. Blair’s veterans remained in northern states after furloughs.

McPherson had 24,380 men at hand

Five divisions brought 96 guns but almost no mounted troops.

Schofield commanded the smallest army

The Army of the Ohio had about 12,800 men in XXIII Corps and a cavalry division, with 23 field pieces.

The three armies were badly unbalanced

Thomas’s army could be slow to move, while McPherson and Schofield often lacked enough strength for their assignments.

Temporary attachments caused command disputes

Sherman often sent parts of Thomas’s army to support the smaller armies, but seniority disputes complicated control.

Hooker and Palmer resisted subordinate roles

Both disputed orders or movement priorities when placed under officers they believed they outranked.

Sherman controlled four cavalry divisions separately

About 12,000 troopers guarded flanks, the supply line, and the reserve instead of operating as a unified striking force.

The organization only needed to outperform the opponent

The campaign would test whether the unequal U.S. structure could defeat the Army of Tennessee.

Johnston wanted three infantry corps

The Confederate War Department rejected his proposal, leaving two infantry corps and a cavalry corps at the opening.

Hood received a corps

John B. Hood replaced Thomas C. Hindman as a corps commander; Hindman returned to division command.

Johnston rebuilt the rank and file

Improved supply, furloughs, restored unit relationships, drill, inspections, and mock battles raised morale.

The Confederate army reached about 55,000 men

Recruits and soldiers returning from hospitals increased strength by late April.

Senior command relationships remained poor

The former pro- and anti-Bragg factions continued to distrust each other after Bragg left the army.

Bragg retained influence from Richmond

As Davis’s military adviser, Braxton Bragg received back-channel reports from former supporters in the army.

Johnston did not trust Hardee

Earlier disputes and conflicting reports about the army’s condition led Johnston to exclude Hardee from important consultations.

Johnston relied on Hood

Hood privately criticized Johnston to contacts in Richmond while implying the army needed more aggressive leadership.

Wheeler also communicated against Johnston

The cavalry commander remained connected to the pro-Bragg faction and sent critical letters to Richmond.

The U.S. high command had greater political support

Grant trusted Sherman and Lincoln increasingly trusted Grant, limiting the effect of subordinate complaints.
