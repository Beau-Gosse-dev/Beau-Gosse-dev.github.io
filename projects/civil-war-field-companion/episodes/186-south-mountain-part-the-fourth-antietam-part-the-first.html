Episode 186 · September 14–15, 1862 · Crampton’s Gap and Pleasant Valley near Burkittsville, Maryland; South Mountain through Boonsboro and Keedysville to the Antietam Creek line outside Sharpsburg; and Harpers Ferry

SOUTH MOUNTAIN (Part the Fourth) / ANTIETAM (Part the First)

Decisive United States tactical victory at Crampton’s Gap and a United States victory at South Mountain, but without the immediate operational exploitation McClellan intended. Confederate resistance delayed VI Corps, Harpers Ferry surrendered on September 15, and Robert E. Lee halted his withdrawal to begin assembling the Army of Northern Virginia behind Antietam Creek near Sharpsburg
Episode scopeCrampton’s Gap, the Confederate withdrawal from South Mountain, and Lee’s first position at SharpsburgThe episode completes the September 14 battle by following William B. Franklin’s VI Corps at Crampton’s Gap, then traces the campaign through noon on September 15. It ends before the armies are fully concentrated and before the Battle of Antietam begins.
Crampton’s Gap dateSunday, September 14, 1862Crampton’s Gap was the southern sector of the Battle of South Mountain, about six miles south of Turner’s Gap.
Federal objectiveCross South Mountain, enter Pleasant Valley, and relieve Harpers FerryMcClellan also expected Franklin’s movement to separate Lee’s forces near Boonsboro from McLaws and the other Confederate commands around Harpers Ferry.
VI Corps strengthNearly 13,000 men in Franklin’s two divisionsThe National Park Service rounds the corps to about 12,000. Henry W. Slocum’s division made the principal assault; William F. Smith’s division supported the operation.
Morning delayFranklin halted about one hour for IV Corps reinforcementsThe delay came despite McClellan’s instruction not to wait for those troops. The corps then paused again outside Burkittsville while the Confederate defense was reinforced.
Two threatened passesCrampton’s Gap and Brownsville PassBrig. Gen. Paul J. Semmes expected the main Federal effort at Brownsville Pass and kept three regiments there. Crampton’s Gap received Mahone’s brigade under Col. William A. Parham and cavalry under Col. Thomas T. Munford.
Initial Crampton’s Gap defenseAbout 800 cavalry and infantryThe episode gives Parham 520 men in four Virginia regiments, one of which was detached, and places Munford’s cavalry with him. The thin line formed behind a stone wall along Mountain Church Road west of Burkittsville.
Defensive frontageSome Confederate soldiers stood eight feet apartThe line was too long for the available troops. Its stone wall offered strong cover, but Slocum’s much larger formation could overlap both flanks.
Confederate reinforcementHowell Cobb’s brigade, about 1,300 menLafayette McLaws sent Cobb from the Harpers Ferry operation with an order to hold the gap. The 10th Georgia from Semmes’s brigade was also directed toward the threatened sector.
Contact near BurkittsvilleAbout noonFederal skirmishers drove Munford’s horsemen through Burkittsville and met the stronger cavalry-and-infantry line behind the stone wall at the mountain’s eastern base.
Assault beginsAbout 4 p.m.Slocum formed his division for attack beginning around 3 p.m. The lead regiments moved against the wall an hour later after repeated urgent messages from McClellan.
Initial Federal methodMassed fire and separate charges failed to move the wall lineThe lead regiments took cover behind a rail fence with little protection. Confederate fire inflicted heavy losses while Slocum’s brigades entered the action gradually.
United chargeAbout 5:30 p.m.With bayonets fixed, the Federal line surged across the fields, broke the Mountain Church Road position, and pursued the defenders up the mountain.
Cobb’s arrivalToo late to establish a coherent second lineCobb’s brigade met Parham’s and Munford’s retreating men while it was still deploying on the eastern slope. The partly formed line checked the pursuit only briefly.
Gap securedAbout 6:45 p.m.Slocum’s soldiers drove the remaining Confederate groups from the summit and held Crampton’s Gap at dark.
United States losses at Crampton’s Gap533 killed, wounded, or missingThe transcript gives 113 killed, 418 wounded, and 2 missing. Franklin’s official report and the Army of the Potomac casualty table give 115 killed, 416 wounded, and 2 missing; the total is the same.
Confederate losses at Crampton’s GapRoughly 900 to 1,000, most of them captured or missingThe episode says losses neared 1,000 and more than 600 were prisoners. Returns are incomplete and later totals vary, but all show a severe defeat for the small defending force.
Pleasant Valley lineMcLaws blocked the valley about one mile south of the gapKershaw’s and Barksdale’s brigades came off Maryland Heights and joined Wilcox, the remnants of Cobb, Semmes, and Parham, and other available troops. Harpers Ferry still blocked McLaws from withdrawing south.
Lee’s first decisionAbandon the Maryland Campaign and cross at ShepherdstownAfter hearing Hill and Longstreet’s reports from the northern gaps, Lee ordered a retreat through Sharpsburg. At 8 p.m. he told McLaws that the day had gone against the army and directed the Harpers Ferry detachments to rejoin him.
Withdrawal from South Mountain10 p.m. September 14 to 4 a.m. September 15Hill’s and Longstreet’s commands withdrew in darkness. Exhausted stragglers fell behind and many were collected by the Federal pursuit.
Lee halts near KeedysvilleTo avoid abandoning McLaws in Pleasant ValleyThe automated transcript repeatedly says “Kittiesville.” The Maryland place is Keedysville. Lee’s temporary halt placed Hill and Longstreet on the flank of any Federal advance south from Crampton’s Gap.
New defensive positionHigh ground west of Antietam Creek near SharpsburgKeedysville offered poor ground for a stand. Lee shifted Hill and Longstreet farther west while still expecting to retreat after McLaws escaped.
Harpers Ferry surrenderMorning of September 15Confederate bombardment stopped around 8:30 a.m., and United States white flags appeared about 9 a.m. Jackson captured more than 12,500 soldiers, 13,000 small arms, and 47 artillery pieces according to the National Park Service.
Franklin does not attack in Pleasant ValleyHe reported the Confederate position too strong at 11 a.m.The fall of Harpers Ferry removed the relief objective. Franklin’s limited activity allowed McLaws to leave Pleasant Valley and rejoin Lee.
Lee reverses courseThe retreat is called off after Jackson’s successJackson’s messages convinced Lee that the army could reunite at Sharpsburg. Lee strengthened the Antietam line and prepared to receive McClellan instead of continuing immediately to the Potomac.
South Mountain consequenceUnited States battlefield success bought the Confederates timeFederal forces captured the passes, but the day-long defense delayed pursuit long enough for Jackson to complete the Harpers Ferry operation and for Lee to begin reconcentrating.
Recommended book“Antietam 1862: The Civil War’s Bloodiest Day”The episode and official podcast book list identify Norman S. Stevens’s Osprey Campaign volume as the recommendation.
Transcript normalizationsBurkittsville, Paul J. Semmes, Lafayette McLaws, William A. Parham, Thomas T. Munford, Howell Cobb, Keedysville, and Harpers FerryThe automated transcript uses forms including “Brooketsville,” “Sims,” “McClellan” for McLaws, “Kittiesville,” and other phonetic renderings. This guide uses the historical spellings.

Cobb’s BrigadeBrig. Gen. Howell CobbGeorgia and North Carolina regiments sent from the Harpers Ferry operation; reached the eastern slope while the original defense was breaking
Harpers Ferry operationMaj. Gen. Thomas J. “Stonewall” JacksonCoordinated McLaws, John G. Walker, and A. P. Hill around the garrison; confirmed its surrender and directed most of his force toward Sharpsburg
South Mountain withdrawal and first Sharpsburg lineMaj. Gen. James Longstreet and Maj. Gen. D. H. HillReported Turner’s Gap untenable, withdrew through Boonsboro, and formed on the high ground west of Antietam Creek
