What happened

The episode leaves Kennesaw Mountain

Sherman had lost about 3,000 men in the June 27 assaults. On July 3, he learned that Johnston had withdrawn because of the U.S. movement around the Confederate left.

Thomas pursued through Marietta

XIV Corps followed the railroad, IV Corps advanced on its left, and XX Corps on its right. McPherson and Schofield continued south on the Sandtown Road.

A Confederate rear guard slowed the advance

Skirmishing south of Marietta delayed Thomas. By late afternoon, resistance near Smyrna Station forced a full U.S. division to deploy.

Johnston occupied the Smyrna Line

The east-west position crossed the railroad at Smyrna, with Rottenwood Creek protecting the right and Nickajack Creek protecting the left.

The Confederate corps formed the line

William W. Loring held the right, William J. Hardee the center, John B. Hood the left, and Georgia militia extended farther west.

Skirmishing continued on July 4

The opposing armies exchanged fire along the Smyrna Line while Sherman prepared to continue the pursuit.

Alfred J. Vaughan was severely wounded

A U.S. artillery shell struck the Confederate brigade commander’s left foot. His leg was amputated, and he did not return to field service during the war.

McPherson crossed Nickajack Creek

U.S. troops drove back Georgia militia and threatened to separate them from Johnston’s army.

Johnston abandoned the Smyrna Line

During the night, the Army of Tennessee moved six miles south into the Shoup Line.

U.S. troops encountered the Shoup Line

Francis A. Shoup designed the six-mile defensive system. About 1,000 enslaved laborers had worked on it for two weeks.

Redoubts and stockades strengthened the position

Earth-and-log redoubts up to 12 feet thick stood about 80 feet apart and were connected by log stockades. Both ends rested on the river.

Sherman saw Atlanta from near Vinings

On July 5, Sherman and Thomas viewed the city about eight miles away and examined possible routes across the Chattahoochee.

James A. Connolly described the view

The 123rd Illinois officer wrote that U.S. soldiers cheered when they saw Atlanta and that artillery soon fired across the river.
