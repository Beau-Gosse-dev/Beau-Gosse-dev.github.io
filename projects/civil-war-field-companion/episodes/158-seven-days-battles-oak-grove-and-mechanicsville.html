SEVEN DAYS' BATTLES: OAK GROVE AND MECHANICSVILLE

Oak Grove ended inconclusively after George B. McClellan gained about 600 yards without disrupting Robert E. Lee’s planned offensive. At Beaver Dam Creek the next day, Fitz John Porter and George A. McCall repulsed direct Confederate attacks from a prepared position. Lee’s army suffered substantially heavier losses, but McClellan ordered Porter to withdraw during the night, surrendered the battlefield, and yielded the operational initiative to Lee
Episode scopeThe first two engagements of the Seven Days BattlesThe episode covers Oak Grove on June 25 and Beaver Dam Creek, also called Mechanicsville or Ellerson’s Mill, on June 26.
Oak Grove objectiveAdvance the United States line toward high ground near Old TavernMcClellan wanted ground suitable for siege guns that could threaten Richmond. The episode places the intended advance at about one and one-half miles.
Oak Grove openingAbout 8:30 a.m. on June 25Joseph Hooker sent Daniel Sickles’s brigade on the right and Cuvier Grover’s brigade in the center; Philip Kearny sent John C. Robinson’s brigade on the left.
Terrain at Oak GroveWooded and swampy ground disrupted the advancing lineSickles’s brigade fell behind in the difficult terrain, leaving Grover unsupported on his right when Grover met Ambrose R. Wright’s Confederates.
Remote directionMcClellan first directed the action by telegraph from about three miles awayHe ordered a withdrawal after receiving reports of heavy fighting, reached the front at about 1 p.m., assessed the field, and ordered the lost ground retaken.
Oak Grove resultIndecisive; the United States line advanced about 600 yardsThe limited gain did not interrupt the Confederate offensive scheduled for June 26.
Oak Grove casualtiesUnited States 626; Confederacy 441The National Park Service gives 1,067 total casualties. The standard breakdown is United States: 68 killed, 503 wounded, 55 missing; Confederacy: 66 killed, 362 wounded, 13 missing. The automated transcript drops a digit and renders the Confederate wounded as “36.”
Lee’s riskMost Confederate striking power shifted north of the ChickahominyThe episode states that about 25,000 Confederates remained south of the river to defend Richmond. Strength figures vary by source and method of counting.
McClellan’s intelligenceReports indicated that Jackson was moving toward the exposed Federal rightMcClellan correctly suspected the direction of Lee’s intended blow but did not reinforce the right before Lee attacked.
Planned Confederate triggerJackson turn the Federal right early on June 26Lee expected Jackson’s movement to set Branch, A. P. Hill, Longstreet, and D. H. Hill in motion in sequence.
Jackson’s delayed marchSlash Church at about 9 a.m., roughly six hours behind the planned scheduleThe episode attributes delay to a late start, obstacles cut by United States cavalry, unfamiliar roads, and cautious movement. Exact expectations and responsibility are matters of historical interpretation.
A. P. Hill’s decisionCrossed Meadow Bridge at about 3 p.m. without confirmation from JacksonHill acted without first notifying Lee or obtaining new permission. The episode also notes evidence that Lee had independently concluded that a crossing should occur before dark.
Porter’s defensive choiceWithdrew outposts through Mechanicsville to Beaver Dam CreekPorter intended to fight from the prepared east-bank position rather than hold the village.
Beaver Dam Creek positionSteep, swampy approach, felled-tree obstacles, earthworks, and supporting artilleryThe National Park Service describes a strong position with open fields of fire. Its vulnerable point was the northern flank that Jackson was supposed to turn.
Forces at Beaver Dam CreekAbout 14,000 United States troops and roughly 10,000–11,000 Confederates engagedThe episode uses 14,000 and 10,000. The National Park Service states that five A. P. Hill brigades, perhaps 11,000 men, pushed toward the creek. These are approximate battlefield strengths.
Jackson at Hundley’s CornerReached the Federal flank area late and stopped for the nightThe episode places his arrival at about 5 p.m. and his bivouac order at 6 p.m. The National Park Service describes arrival at the creek headwaters late on June 26. Precise timing differs among accounts.
Main Confederate engagementA. P. Hill’s brigades fixed McCall’s division along the creekField and Archer engaged from the west bank. Anderson briefly crossed in the upper sector. Pender and later Ripley attacked near Ellerson’s Mill.
Ellerson’s Mill attacksPender’s and Ripley’s attacks failed under concentrated infantry and artillery fireThe movement occurred over difficult, exposed ground against Truman Seymour’s brigade. The schematic map does not reconstruct exact regimental routes or formations.
Ripley’s brigade lossesNearly 600 killed and woundedThe National Park Service states that Ripley’s brigade incurred almost half of Lee’s losses in the battle.
44th Georgia losses335 killed and wounded from 514 reported engagedThe episode gives these figures and identifies the regiment’s first battle as the attack at Ellerson’s Mill. Contemporary reporting varies in how detached men and later deaths were counted.
1st North Carolina lossesAbout 142 casualties in the episode’s accountPublished figures vary slightly by source and category. The regiment also lost much of its field and company leadership.
Beaver Dam Creek casualtiesApproximately 1,400 Confederates and 350 United States troopsThe National Park Service uses these rounded totals. The episode gives about 1,500 Confederate casualties and 361 United States casualties; estimates vary by source and categorization.
Tactical resultUnited States victory at Beaver Dam CreekNo Confederate force broke McCall’s east-bank line on June 26, and the direct attacks failed at heavy cost.
Night withdrawalPorter moved east before dawn on June 27Concern that Jackson could turn the northern flank led McClellan to order the position abandoned despite the tactical victory.
Operational consequenceMcClellan yielded the initiative after winning the June 26 battlefield fightThe withdrawal carried Porter toward the Gaines’s Mill position and began the Army of the Potomac’s movement away from the Richmond front.
Command failureThe Confederate plan operated without timely coordination among separated columnsJackson, Branch, A. P. Hill, and Lee lacked an effective flow of current information. The episode assigns ultimate responsibility for army-level staff coordination to Lee while distinguishing that judgment from the observed movements.
Recommended book“Davis and Lee at War” by Steven E. WoodworthThe podcast page and episode closing identify this title as the recommendation.

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Col. Robert A. Smith

Commander, 44th Georgia Infantry. Mortally wounded while leading the regiment’s attack at Ellerson’s Mill. Contemporary reporting recorded 335 killed and wounded in the regiment; counts vary slightly on the number actually in the attacking line.

Confederacy

Col. Montford S. Stokes

Commander, 1st North Carolina Infantry. Mortally wounded in the attack near Ellerson’s Mill and died in July 1862. Later sources disagree on the exact date of death.
