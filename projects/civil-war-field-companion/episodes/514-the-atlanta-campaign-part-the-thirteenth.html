What happened

Three changes reshaped the campaign

The episode covers extensive fieldworks, Johnston’s changing relationships with Hood and Hardee, and the dispute over cavalry raids against Sherman’s railroad.

Both armies expanded their field defenses

Close contact after New Hope Church produced continuous sharpshooting, artillery fire, limited attacks, and immediate digging.

Soldiers built trenches under fire

They used available tools, plates, boards, bayonets, and their hands, piling excavated earth toward the enemy.

Head logs protected firing positions

Green logs rested on skids above the parapet, with openings beneath for soldiers to fire through.

Obstacles covered the approach

Defenders cleared fields of fire, built abatis from felled trees, sometimes added telegraph wire, and placed forward rifle pits.

Johnston lost confidence in Hood

Hood had not produced the expected results and criticized Johnston to contacts in Richmond.

Johnston’s opinion of Hardee improved

Johnston praised Hardee’s battlefield conduct and advice. The two later became professional allies.

Johnston’s original strategy had failed

The army repulsed some attacks but won no decisive battle and repeatedly withdrew as Sherman maneuvered south.

Johnston adopted an inflated casualty estimate

His staff estimated more than 45,000 U.S. casualties, more than twice the actual figure, and concluded Sherman was being worn down.

Returning to the railroad solved U.S. supply problems

Shortages during the movement near Dallas ended once Sherman reconnected with the Western and Atlantic.

Johnston focused on breaking the railroad

He believed a long interruption might force Sherman to retreat or attack prepared defenses directly.

Johnston did not detach his own main cavalry force

He believed the army was outnumbered and wanted cavalry in Alabama and Mississippi to attack the railroad instead.

Stephen D. Lee controlled the western cavalry

Johnston lacked authority over forces in Alabama and Mississippi and needed Lee or Richmond to approve the operation.

Sherman kept Forrest occupied

U.S. raids from Memphis drew Nathan Bedford Forrest into repeated defensive operations in Mississippi.

Richmond and Johnston disputed who should raid

Davis and Bragg questioned why Johnston’s cavalry could not do the job. Johnston provided little operational detail.

Most Confederate cavalrymen lacked horses

Of more than 8,000 reported present, only about 2,400 were classified effective. Joseph Wheeler nevertheless claimed he could attack the railroad.

The dispute produced no major raid

Distrust between Johnston and Davis prevented a coordinated decision, and Sherman’s railroad remained usable.
