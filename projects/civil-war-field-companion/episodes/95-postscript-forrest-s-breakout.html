POSTSCRIPT: FORREST'S BREAKOUT

Nathan Bedford Forrest rose from a Tennessee and Mississippi businessman and slave trader to lieutenant colonel of Confederate cavalry. At Fort Donelson, his cavalry helped open Forge Road during the Confederate attack on February 15, 1862. The senior commanders then ordered the troops back into their defenses. Before the surrender on February 16, Forrest led roughly 500 mounted men out on the old Charlotte Road, crossed flooded Lick Creek, and reached Nashville on February 18

What happened

Forrest was born in Tennessee

Nathan Bedford Forrest was born in July 1821 in Bedford County, Tennessee. His family moved to northeastern Mississippi in 1834, and his father died three years later.

Forrest moved to Memphis and traded enslaved people

By 1851 Forrest had married, started a family, and moved to Memphis. He became a slave trader, invested in real estate and cotton plantations, and accumulated substantial wealth.

Forrest enlisted as a private

After Tennessee seceded, the 39-year-old Forrest joined Josiah White’s Tennessee Mounted Rifles in June 1861. Memphis residents then petitioned Governor Isham Harris to commission him.

Forrest raised and equipped mounted troops

The governor authorized Forrest to raise mounted rangers. Forrest used personal funds to buy pistols, saddles, and other equipment and became a lieutenant colonel in August.

Forrest won his first engagement at Sacramento

In December 1861, Forrest divided about 200 men into three elements and attacked a larger U.S. detachment from the front and both flanks near Sacramento, Kentucky.

Forrest was ordered to Fort Donelson

After Fort Henry fell, Forrest reached the Cumberland River opposite Fort Donelson on February 10 with about 500 cavalrymen and completed the crossing the next morning.

Pillow placed Forrest over the cavalry

Gideon Pillow gave Forrest command of the mounted troops and sent him to scout the roads between Forts Henry and Donelson.

Forrest delayed McClernand’s advance

On February 12, Forrest ambushed U.S. cavalry on the lower road with two companies armed with Maynard carbines. He withdrew after Simon Buckner declined to reinforce him.

The first breakout attempt was canceled

Forrest formed his men for a planned attack on February 14, but Pillow stopped the movement. Confederate river batteries later forced the U.S. gunboats to withdraw.

The main breakout began on February 15

Forrest’s cavalry supported the left of the Confederate attack. Pillow’s troops drove back McClernand’s division and exposed part of Forge Road.

Forrest captured U.S. artillery

Forrest charged Battery E of the 2nd Illinois Light Artillery, struck the 11th Illinois Infantry, and later captured guns from Battery D of the 1st Illinois Light Artillery.

Confederate commanders gave up the opened road

Although the morning attack cleared an escape route, Pillow ordered the troops back into the defenses. Grant then ordered counterattacks by C. F. Smith and Lew Wallace.

Forrest rejected surrender

At the night council, Forrest argued that the old Charlotte Road remained open. Floyd and Buckner relied on reports that U.S. troops blocked the roads and decided the infantry could not cross the flooded creek in freezing weather.

Forrest received permission to leave

After Buckner assumed command and accepted that the garrison would surrender, Forrest obtained permission to take out his cavalry before a flag of truce was raised.

About 500 mounted men assembled

Forrest gathered about 400 cavalrymen by 4 a.m. on February 16. Approximately 100 additional soldiers found horses and joined the column.

A fence was mistaken for U.S. infantry

Scouts reported a battle line ahead. Forrest and his brother Jeffrey checked the report and found a fence in the mist and darkness, then confirmed with wounded U.S. soldiers that no large force had passed.

The column crossed Lick Creek

The mounted column reached the ford without being fired on. Forrest tested the icy, flooded creek himself, and the men crossed water reported to be about saddle-skirt deep.

Forrest reached Nashville

The column arrived on February 18. Forrest took charge of removing Confederate supplies after John B. Floyd left the city.

U.S. troops occupied Nashville

Don Carlos Buell reached Nashville on February 25 and found that troops sent to reinforce Grant had already occupied the city. Nashville became the first Confederate state capital captured by U.S. forces.
