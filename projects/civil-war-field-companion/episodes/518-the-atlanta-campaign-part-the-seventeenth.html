What happened

Sherman received Thomas’s warning

On June 24, Thomas reported that Howard’s and Palmer’s fronts were already too extended to mass troops easily and that the men were exhausted.

Sherman decided to attack

Schofield could not extend farther south because the Confederate left had matched his movement. Sherman concluded another flanking move was not immediately available.

Sherman planned to break the extended line

He told Thomas that he intended to study the ground, let Johnston stretch his army, and attack on June 27.

Special Field Orders No. 28 set the plan

Each U.S. army was to attack or demonstrate at 8 a.m. If a breach opened, reserves would push through toward Marietta.

Schofield was to press the Confederate left

The Army of the Ohio would move along Powder Springs Road and maintain pressure south of the main battlefield.

McPherson received the northern assignment

The Army of the Tennessee would demonstrate against the Confederate right and attack south of Kennesaw Mountain.

Thomas chose the main attack sector

The Army of the Cumberland would attack the Confederate center. Sherman left the exact point to Thomas.

Thomas examined the Confederate line

On June 25, Thomas went to the picket line and beyond it to inspect the terrain and works.

Thomas selected two divisions

Howard chose John Newton’s IV Corps division. Thomas directed John M. Palmer to use Jefferson C. Davis’s XIV Corps division.

The main assault would be south of Dallas Road

The ground was less steep than Big Kennesaw, Little Kennesaw, and Pigeon Hill, although it remained wooded and broken.

Logan prepared the XV Corps attack

McPherson assigned Morgan L. Smith’s division to lead near Burnt Hickory Road, with Peter J. Osterhaus in support and Charles C. Walcutt available.

Pigeon Hill became McPherson’s target

After consulting engineer Chauncey B. Reese, Logan concentrated the attack on Pigeon Hill and the ground south of it.

Loring’s corps held the northern heights

Featherston, Walthall, and French held Big Kennesaw, Little Kennesaw, and Pigeon Hill. Wheeler’s cavalry guarded the Confederate right.

Hardee’s corps held the central and southern line

Walker and Bate held ground down to Dallas Road. Cleburne and Cheatham held the ridge farther south toward Kolb’s Farm.

Sherman notified Washington

He told Henry Halleck that he intended to attack the Confederate left center after stretching the line.

Supply pressure influenced Sherman

The army group required about 65 railcars of supplies each day. Confederate raids repeatedly interrupted the long railroad from Chattanooga.

Sherman described his options to Ellen Sherman

On June 26, he wrote that he had to attack directly or turn the position and that either choice would cause loss and difficulty.

Sherman believed Johnston’s line had become thin

The U.S. line extended about ten miles. Sherman reasoned that Johnston’s smaller army had to be stretched even more and could be broken at the center.

Army commanders issued final orders

Thomas, McPherson, and Schofield directed their corps commanders to be ready for the coordinated operation on June 27.

McPherson emphasized secrecy

He ordered movements to make as little noise and visible change along the line as possible.

Hardee expected to hold

The Confederate corps commander wrote that his lines were strong and that he expected to defeat any U.S. attack.
