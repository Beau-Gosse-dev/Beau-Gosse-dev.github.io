Episode 205 · February–July 1862, with a preview of the western campaigns of late 1862 · The western theater from Missouri and Arkansas to Kentucky, Tennessee, Mississippi, Alabama, and the Appalachian gateway at Cumberland Gap

"MEANWHILE, OUT WEST..."

By early summer 1862, United States forces had broken the Confederacy’s first western defensive line, occupied Nashville, seized the railroad junction at Corinth, won control of Memphis, reached Helena on the Mississippi, occupied northern Alabama, and taken Cumberland Gap. Those gains did not produce an immediate drive into the Deep South. Henry W. Halleck dispersed forces to secure a long river-and-rail supply network, while distance, weak transportation links, and Confederate positions at Vicksburg, eastern Tennessee, and Chattanooga limited the next advances. The pause gave Confederate commanders time to prepare counteroffensives in Kentucky, Tennessee, and Mississippi
Episode scopeA strategic reset in the western theater after Shiloh and CorinthThe episode surveys the position reached by mid-1862 and introduces the geographic and logistical constraints behind the Kentucky, Corinth, and Stones River campaigns covered next.
Upper South at stakeKentucky and Tennessee linked population, food production, rivers, roads, and railroadsHolding the region protected the Confederate interior; losing it placed United States armies near Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, and the principal east-west railroad through Chattanooga.
Three campaigns previewedBragg in Kentucky, Van Dorn at Corinth, and Rosecrans at Stones RiverThe episode previews Confederate attempts to recover the initiative and the United States response. It does not narrate those campaigns in detail.
Opening victoriesForts Henry and Donelson fell in February 1862The collapse of the Tennessee and Cumberland River positions forced the Confederacy to abandon Kentucky and much of Middle and West Tennessee.
Nashville occupiedFirst Confederate state capital captured by United States forcesAdvance elements of the Army of the Ohio reached Nashville on February 23, and Don Carlos Buell accepted the city’s formal surrender on February 25. Nashville remained an essential United States supply base.
ShilohApril 6–7, 1862; United States victory after a Confederate surprise attackAlbert Sidney Johnston was mortally wounded on April 6. P. G. T. Beauregard assumed command, and the reinforced United States armies drove his force back toward Corinth on April 7.
Why Corinth matteredThe Memphis & Charleston and Mobile & Ohio railroads crossed thereThe east-west line linked the Mississippi Valley with Chattanooga and the Atlantic states; the north-south line connected western Tennessee with central Mississippi and Mobile.
United States concentrationMore than 100,000 soldiers in three armiesHalleck personally assembled Grant’s Army of the Tennessee, Buell’s Army of the Ohio, and Pope’s Army of the Mississippi for the advance on Corinth. National Park Service interpretation gives the combined strength as about 120,000.
Siege of CorinthApril 29–May 30, 1862Halleck advanced slowly, entrenched after successive movements, and approached the Confederate defenses without risking the kind of surprise suffered at Shiloh.
Confederate evacuationBeauregard withdrew to Tupelo during the night of May 29–30Outnumbered and suffering from disease and poor water, the Confederates removed troops and supplies by rail while deceptive cheering, train noise, campfires, and dummy guns concealed the movement.
Strategic gain, incomplete destructionThe United States took Corinth; Beauregard preserved his armyHalleck secured the railroad crossing and northern Mississippi position, but the Confederate field army escaped roughly sixty miles south to Tupelo.
Fort Pillow and MemphisFort Pillow was evacuated June 4; Memphis fell June 6At Memphis, Flag Officer Charles H. Davis’s gunboats and Col. Charles Ellet’s rams destroyed or captured all but one vessel of the Confederate River Defense Fleet. United States forces then occupied the city.
Mississippi River remained contestedMemphis opened another long reach of river, but Vicksburg still blocked complete controlThe episode also names Port Hudson. Confederate forces did not begin fortifying Port Hudson until later in 1862, so it should not be treated as an established strongpoint at the exact moment Memphis fell.
Pea RidgeMarch 7–8, 1862; United States victory in northwest ArkansasSamuel R. Curtis defeated Earl Van Dorn’s larger Confederate army. The victory secured Missouri for the United States and ended the immediate Confederate effort to recover that state.
New Mexico campaignThe Confederate invasion failed after Glorieta Pass, March 26–28United States troops destroyed the Confederate supply train at Johnson’s Ranch. Without those supplies, Henry H. Sibley’s force abandoned its advance toward Fort Union and ultimately retreated to Texas.
Why Halleck pausedThe newly occupied network had to be supplied and defendedHalleck dispersed troops among key towns, railroads, rivers, and depots rather than moving his entire concentration south after Beauregard.
Corinth’s railroad distinctionA junction of two lines, but only one principal rail corridor continued southThe Mobile & Ohio Railroad offered the main rail penetration from Corinth toward Tupelo and central Mississippi. Moving a very large army along that single axis created a different problem from occupying the well-connected Upper South.
Upper South logisticsShorter distances and a denser transportation networkThe Tennessee, Cumberland, and Mississippi Rivers, together with established roads and railroads, allowed United States forces to move inland and receive supplies from northern bases.
Deep South logisticsLonger distances, fewer towns, thinner rail coverage, and limited navigable interior riversThe episode contrasts the Deep South’s landlocked interior with the Upper South. Large armies could not occupy every locality; campaigns had to follow selected rivers, railroads, and strategically valuable corridors.
Narrow axes of advanceThe Mississippi River and the Chattanooga–Atlanta railroad corridorThe map logic of the western war concentrated future operations along transportation lines capable of sustaining armies over great distances.
Curtis’s supply problemA wagon route of more than 200 miles from Rolla, MissouriAt Batesville, Curtis’s Army of the Southwest could not sustain an advance on Little Rock through an increasingly disrupted overland supply line.
Curtis reaches HelenaJuly 12, 1862Curtis cut loose from his Rolla supply line, lived off the countryside, and marched to the Mississippi. Helena restored river supply and became an advanced United States base south of Memphis.
Civilian cost of Curtis’s marchForaging and destruction accompanied the movement to HelenaNational Park Service accounts describe the army taking food and destroying property deemed economically or militarily useful to the Confederacy. Thousands of enslaved people also reached the army and moved toward freedom.
Mitchel in northern AlabamaHuntsville occupied April 11, 1862Ormsby M. Mitchel’s division of the Army of the Ohio seized Huntsville and sections of the Memphis & Charleston Railroad while Buell moved toward Shiloh. The division remained exposed and depended on a long route from Nashville, local forage, and aggressive patrols.
Kirby Smith’s positionA thin Confederate line across eastern TennesseeEdmund Kirby Smith had to cover approaches from Cumberland Gap through Knoxville to Chattanooga with a force the episode estimates at fewer than 10,000.
Cumberland Gap takenGeorge W. Morgan occupied it June 18, 1862Morgan maneuvered through mountain passes to threaten the Confederate position. Carter L. Stevenson withdrew his garrison, allowing Morgan’s 7th Division to enter without a major battle.
What the Gap offeredA route toward Knoxville and the Virginia–Tennessee railroadThe mountain pass could protect Kentucky, support Unionists in East Tennessee, and serve as a staging point for an advance against Confederate rail communications.
Why Chattanooga matteredRiver crossing, railroad junction, mountain gateway, and supply hubLines through Chattanooga connected Virginia with the western Confederacy and opened a route toward Atlanta and Georgia. Its terrain also made direct operations difficult.
Next operational moveHalleck directed Buell east from Corinth toward ChattanoogaThe episode ends in mid-June with the intended seizure of Chattanooga. The slow advance and Confederate response belong to the next phase of the western story.
Recommended book“The New York Times Disunion: A History of the Civil War,” edited by Ted WidmerThe volume collects selections from the New York Times Disunion series, which ran during the Civil War sesquicentennial.
