What happened

Crook returned to West Virginia after Cloyd’s Mountain

His column reached Union on May 14. Averell’s cavalry arrived the next day after abandoning its own objectives in southwestern Virginia.

Averell began his raid from Charleston

The cavalry crossed difficult mountain country and reached Jeffersonville on May 8. Reports exaggerated the Confederate force protecting the Saltville salt works.

Averell turned from Saltville and was stopped near Wytheville

William E. Jones and John Hunt Morgan attacked with about 4,500 Confederates and repeatedly forced the U.S. cavalry back during a four-hour fight.

Averell reached the railroad after Crook

At Dublin Depot he found damage already caused by Crook. He destroyed several miles of track near Christiansburg but did not continue east toward Lynchburg as Crook directed.

Crook reported substantial tactical results

His force destroyed the Dublin bridge, eighteen miles of railroad, depots, and stores, captured ten guns and about three hundred prisoners, and defeated the Confederate force at Cloyd’s Mountain.

The raids no longer supported Grant’s main campaign

By May 11 both western columns were withdrawing. They were no longer threatening Confederate infrastructure or preventing troops from moving to Lee.

False reports influenced Crook’s withdrawal

Captured dispatches incorrectly said Grant had been defeated and was retreating. Crook expected reinforcements from Lee to arrive by railroad and chose not to continue.

Sigel advanced up the Shenandoah Valley

His force of nearly 9,000 used the Valley Turnpike. The campaign threatened the region’s food production and the railroad facilities at Staunton.

Sigel left Cedar Creek on May 11

Captured messages at Woodstock told him that Breckinridge was approaching with roughly 4,000 to 4,500 troops. John D. Imboden’s cavalry and local home guards delayed the advance.

A reconnaissance advanced twenty miles to New Market

August Moor led 2,350 troops unfamiliar to him and reached the town on May 14 after pushing back Imboden’s cavalry.

Breckinridge’s infantry arrived south of New Market

U.S. patrols reported the concentration around 7 a.m. on May 15. Julius Stahel took command of the 3,350 U.S. troops then at New Market while Sigel approached.

Sigel marched from Woodstock

He left at 5 a.m. on May 15 with the rest of the column but had not concentrated every unit before the main battle began.

Breckinridge assembled a mixed force

His command included Echols’s and Wharton’s infantry, Imboden’s cavalry, home guards, engineers, eighteen guns, and 227 cadets from the Virginia Military Institute.

Breckinridge decided to attack

The North Fork of the Shenandoah and Smith Creek protected his flanks. He concluded that his smaller force could advance without being easily enveloped.

Confederate countermarching exaggerated troop strength

Units moved repeatedly on Shirley’s Hill to appear more numerous. U.S. estimates rose as high as 20,000, increasing uncertainty in Sigel’s headquarters.

Sigel formed two disconnected lines

He placed one line on Bushong’s Hill and another about one thousand yards forward. Some units were still following earlier orders to concentrate at Mount Jackson.

Confederate artillery struck the U.S. cavalry flank

Imboden placed guns east of Smith Creek while Breckinridge advanced his infantry and moved the main artillery forward from hill to hill.

The first U.S. line withdrew

Echols and Wharton overlapped it on both flanks and followed closely toward Bushong’s Hill. Terrain, smoke, and the rapid advance opened a gap in the Confederate center.

Breckinridge committed the VMI cadets

The cadet battalion was his last available reserve. He ordered it into the gap opposite Stahel’s cavalry to prevent the Confederate line from being divided.

The cadets entered the main battle line

Musket and artillery fire caused immediate casualties, but the battalion maintained formation and closed the gap near the Bushong orchard.

Sigel ordered an understrength counterattack

About 1,700 U.S. soldiers attacked between 2,600 and 2,800 Confederates. The failed charge disrupted Sigel’s line and gave Breckinridge the opportunity to advance again.

The Confederate advance captured five guns

VMI cadets took one twelve-pounder Napoleon and other Confederate units captured four additional pieces as U.S. artillery tried to withdraw.

Sigel ordered a general retreat

Imboden did not seize the single bridge over the swollen river, allowing the U.S. force to cross beginning around 4:30 p.m.

The U.S. column retreated thirty-two miles

The withdrawal continued through Mount Jackson to Strasburg and ended the following day.

New Market cost more than 1,300 casualties

The episode counts 841 U.S. casualties and at least 520 Confederate casualties. Nearly one third of Sigel’s full force never entered the battle.

The VMI battalion suffered fifty-five casualties

Ten cadets were killed and forty-five wounded in the episode’s count, about one quarter of the battalion that entered the fight.

Breckinridge sent troops to Lee

With Crook, Averell, and Sigel withdrawing, Breckinridge placed about 2,500 men from Echols’s and Wharton’s brigades on trains at Staunton on May 19.
