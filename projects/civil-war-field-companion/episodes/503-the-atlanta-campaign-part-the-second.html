What happened

The Confederate outlook had deteriorated

The losses of Gettysburg and Vicksburg and the defeat at Missionary Ridge ended the military optimism present at the start of 1863.

Confederate manpower losses were severe

By early 1864 at least 150,000 soldiers had died, with many more wounded, sick, imprisoned, or absent.

About 60,000 troops were absent

Desertion grew as soldiers became demoralized, exhausted, or concerned about families without food.

The home front lacked basic goods

Government control of blockade-running cargo emphasized military supplies while civilians faced shortages of clothing, medicine, coffee, and food.

Railroad capacity declined

Federal occupation, damage, worn track, and shortages of equipment caused slow service, breakdowns, and derailments.

Confederate currency lost value

The government had issued about $1.2 billion; the exchange rate fell from three Confederate dollars per gold dollar in January 1863 to twenty a year later.

Food prices rose sharply

The episode gives Richmond examples of bacon rising from 12.5 cents to eight dollars per pound and flour rising from about seven to two hundred dollars per barrel.

Confederate strategy depended on holding out

Southern leaders expected battlefield resistance and U.S. casualties to weaken Northern support for Lincoln before the November election.

Davis prepared for the 1864 campaigns

He pursued financial and manpower measures and selected a permanent commander for the Army of Tennessee.

The Army of Tennessee had a record of defeats

Failures at Donelson, Shiloh, the Kentucky campaign, Stones River, and Missionary Ridge left U.S. forces on Georgia’s northern border.

The army stopped at Dalton

After retreating from Missionary Ridge, the soldiers entered winter camp in northwestern Georgia with low morale and inadequate supplies.

Previous commanders had not solved the problem

Albert Sidney Johnston died at Shiloh, Davis removed P. G. T. Beauregard, and Braxton Bragg lost the confidence of many subordinates.

Hardee declined permanent army command

William J. Hardee led temporarily after Bragg resigned but told Davis he did not want the position.

Only Johnston remained a practical choice

Robert E. Lee could not leave Virginia, and Davis would not restore Beauregard, leaving Joseph E. Johnston.

Davis and Johnston distrusted each other

They disputed seniority, secrecy, strategy, responsibility for Vicksburg, and the military prospects of the Confederacy.

Davis appointed Johnston

On December 16, Davis ordered him to transfer the Army of Mississippi to Leonidas Polk and take command at Dalton.

Johnston found fewer than 40,000 men present

Only about two-thirds were effective. Many lacked shoes and overcoats, rations were poor, scurvy was present, and animals were scarce.

About 30 men deserted each day

The army’s condition made the winter offensive expected by Davis impractical.

Johnston proposed a defensive opening

He told Davis that the army first had to defeat a U.S. advance before it could move forward.

Davis requested another condition report

James Seddon obtained details from Johnston, but the new report did not resolve the disagreement.

Johnston made food supply the first priority

He warned Richmond and Georgia governor Joseph E. Brown that inadequate railroad deliveries might force the army to retreat.

Railroad deliveries improved

By January 25, additional locomotives and cars allowed the army to meet daily needs and begin stockpiling rations.

Training and discipline resumed

Johnston ordered regular drill, inspections, roll calls, dress parades, and camp sanitation.

Furloughs improved morale

Hardee’s furlough system allowed many soldiers to visit home, and most returned to their units.

Johnston proposed moving to Mississippi

He suggested reinforcing with Polk and advancing into western Tennessee while cavalry delayed any U.S. move toward Atlanta.

No common strategy was adopted

Davis neither approved Johnston’s alternative nor ordered an immediate attack, so the army prepared to react to U.S. movements.
