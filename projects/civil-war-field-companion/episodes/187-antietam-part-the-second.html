Episode 187 · September 14–15, 1862 · From Turner’s and Fox’s Gaps across Boonsboro and Keedysville to the Antietam Creek valley and Sharpsburg, Maryland, with the Confederate escape route running south to Boteler’s Ford on the Potomac River

ANTIETAM (Part the Second)

No general engagement occurred on September 15. Robert E. Lee halted behind Antietam Creek with about 15,000 men then present, while George B. McClellan brought more than twice that number to the opposite side of the creek but deferred an attack. The pause allowed Confederate formations leaving Harpers Ferry to begin rejoining Lee before the Battle of Antietam
Episode scopeThe ground at Sharpsburg and the opposing movements on September 15The episode explains why Lee stood at Sharpsburg, follows the Confederate retreat from South Mountain, and traces McClellan’s pursuit to Antietam Creek. It ends before the September 16 preliminary fighting and the main battle on September 17.
Campaign turning pointHarpers Ferry surrendered on the morning of September 15Jackson’s first message that the surrender was imminent, followed by confirmation around noon, persuaded Lee that most of the detached Confederate army could soon concentrate at Sharpsburg.
Lee’s initial intentionWithdraw across the Potomac after South MountainLee first treated Sharpsburg as a temporary halt. He needed to give Lafayette McLaws a way out of Pleasant Valley and then protect the divided army during a retreat.
Decision at SharpsburgMake a defensive stand and reunite the armyThe fall of Harpers Ferry changed Lee’s calculation. Jackson could march north, McLaws could escape through Harpers Ferry, and Lee could still seek the battle he wanted from the Maryland Campaign.
Antietam CreekA north–south tributary entering the Potomac south of SharpsburgThe creek was not exceptionally broad or deep, but formed a meaningful obstacle for organized infantry and artillery. Bridges and usable fords controlled the easiest crossings.
Four stone bridgesA bridge near the creek mouth, Rohrbach, Middle, and Upper bridgesThe Rohrbach Bridge on the Sharpsburg–Rohrersville Road became known as Burnside Bridge. The Middle Bridge carried the Boonsboro Turnpike, and the Upper Bridge stood near the Samuel Pry gristmill.
Sharpsburg’s importanceA road junction between Antietam Creek and the PotomacThe Hagerstown Pike ran north, the Boonsboro Turnpike ran northeast through Keedysville, and local roads led east toward Rohrersville and south toward Harpers Ferry and the Potomac crossings.
Lee’s lifelineThe road from Sharpsburg to Boteler’s FordThe Shepherdstown bridge had been burned in 1861. Boteler’s Ford, below Shepherdstown, was the nearby practical crossing for Lee’s army and therefore the principal escape route back to Virginia.
D. H. Hill’s withdrawalFive brigades left South Mountain during the night of September 14–15Rodes and Colquitt used the main road toward Sharpsburg. Ripley, George B. Anderson, and Garland’s brigade under Duncan K. McRae used country roads to reach Boonsboro and then followed the same general route.
Hill crosses the creekThe Middle Bridge at daylight on September 15Most of Hill’s division formed on high ground north and east of Sharpsburg. Rodes initially held ground southwest of town, while Colquitt continued toward Boteler’s Ford to cover the Potomac crossing.
Hill’s strengthAbout 3,000 muskets at SharpsburgHill’s infantry had been badly reduced at South Mountain. The episode says he also had 26 divisional guns and roughly 20 guns from the army artillery reserve available in the area.
Longstreet’s withdrawalD. R. Jones’s division followed the army trainsQuartermaster, ordnance, ambulance, and commissary wagons delayed the night movement. Jones’s men went first when the road cleared, followed by John Bell Hood’s division.
Army trainsMoved through Sharpsburg toward the PotomacThe wagons halted about halfway between town and the river. Sending the trains toward the ford preserved the option to withdraw even as Lee prepared a line of battle.
Lee’s force on handAbout 15,000 men by September 15This is the episode’s estimate for D. H. Hill and Longstreet at Sharpsburg. Twenty-six of the Army of Northern Virginia’s forty brigades were still around Harpers Ferry, a hard march away.
Confederate conditionHungry, ragged, shoeless, and depleted by stragglingWeeks of marching and fighting, poor food, illness, and inadequate equipment reduced the effective strength of both the troops already at Sharpsburg and the formations hurrying from Harpers Ferry.
Private Judkins’s accountHunger dominated the soldiers’ experienceW. B. Judkins of the 22nd Georgia described eating grapes and unripe gooseberries during the march, then lacking the strength to climb back over a stone fence. He escaped arrest for straggling and rejoined his regiment.
Lee’s confidenceVeterans and trusted lieutenants could offset weakness in numbersLee considered his soldiers battle tested and relied heavily on Jackson and Longstreet. He also expected McClellan to become cautious again after the unusually aggressive attack at South Mountain.
Federal pursuit beginsAt first light on September 15Federal skirmishers found the South Mountain passes abandoned. McClellan ordered the army forward across the previous day’s battlefield toward Boonsboro and Sharpsburg.
Federal routesI, II, and XII Corps on the National Road; IX Corps and Sykes on the Old Sharpsburg RoadThe columns converged toward Keedysville and Antietam Creek. Sykes’s Regular Division belonged to V Corps; the rest of Fitz John Porter’s corps followed as the army concentrated.
South Mountain burialsFifty-eight Confederate dead were placed in Daniel Wise’s wellA Federal burial party used the farm well as a mass grave. The episode quotes a Pennsylvania officer who recognized how haste and war had dehumanized the men doing the work.
Unreliable field reportsMcClellan forwarded claims that Lee was wounded and had lost 15,000 menThose claims were false. McClellan nevertheless sent the reports to Washington as evidence that the Confederate retreat was a panic and the South Mountain victory complete.
Lincoln’s instructionDestroy the Confederate army if possibleLincoln followed events from the War Department telegraph office and encouraged McClellan after receiving the optimistic South Mountain dispatches.
Welles’s skepticismThe navy secretary doubted the victory was decisiveGideon Welles accepted that McClellan had won at South Mountain but questioned the exaggerated reports and whether the Federal army had actually overtaken Lee.
McClellan after South MountainConfident in his first major offensive victory with the Army of the PotomacHe sent retired general in chief Winfield Scott a message emphasizing that he had defeated an army commanded by Robert E. Lee. The episode connects the message to their personal and professional rivalry.
Federal army compositionA reorganized and partly inexperienced forceThe army combined II, V, and VI Corps from the Peninsula army with I and XII Corps reorganized from Pope’s Army of Virginia and IX Corps built around Burnside’s coastal expedition and the Kanawha Division.
New Federal soldiersAbout 20,000 recent recruitsThe episode identifies eighteen new nine-month regiments totaling about 15,000 men and another 5,000 replacements added to existing regiments shortly before Antietam.
Federal force at the creekMore than twice Lee’s estimated 15,000 immediately availableRichardson’s II Corps division and Sykes’s Regulars reached the front near Keedysville, with Hooker’s I Corps and the rest of Sumner’s II Corps behind them. The episode treats this as a temporary opportunity before Confederate reinforcements arrived.
McClellan’s reconnaissanceBegan about 3 p.m.McClellan and a large mounted party drew Confederate artillery fire. He and Fitz John Porter continued examining Lee’s position, after which McClellan reported that it was too late to attack that day.
Operational consequenceSeptember 15 passed without a Federal assaultLee’s exposed force survived the afternoon. Jackson and Walker arrived on September 16, while McLaws and other detached troops continued toward Sharpsburg before the main battle.
Important leader lossSamuel Garland Jr. had been killed at Fox’s Gap on September 14No senior commander was killed or mortally wounded in the September 15 movements. Garland appears because the episode identifies Duncan K. McRae as the officer leading his brigade during the retreat to Sharpsburg.
Recommended resources“The Maps of Antietam” and the McElfresh Antietam battlefield mapThe main recommendation is Bradley M. Gottfried’s atlas “The Maps of Antietam: An Atlas of the Antietam (Sharpsburg) Campaign, Including the Battle of South Mountain.” The hosts also recommend the McElfresh Map Company battlefield map.

What happened

The episode resumes after South Mountain

The hosts review the September 14 tactical defeat that nevertheless bought Lee time to begin reuniting his divided army.

Jackson reports Harpers Ferry near surrender

The message reaching Lee on Monday morning changes the value of the temporary halt at Sharpsburg.

Lee decides to stand

Instead of continuing directly to the Potomac, Lee orders Jackson toward Sharpsburg and prepares to defend the position.

The battle site emerges from the failed campaign plan

The episode explains that Antietam resulted from delays at Harpers Ferry and McClellan’s faster-than-expected movement across South Mountain.

Antietam Creek is introduced

The creek runs south from Pennsylvania past Sharpsburg and enters the Potomac about three miles below town.

The four stone bridges define the crossings

The Rohrbach, Middle, and Upper bridges are located in relation to Sharpsburg, along with a lower bridge near the creek mouth.

Sharpsburg’s road network is traced

The Hagerstown and Boonsboro turnpikes and secondary roads made the town a natural point of concentration.

Boteler’s Ford becomes Lee’s lifeline

With the Shepherdstown bridge destroyed, the three-mile road south from Sharpsburg led to the army’s practical escape route across the Potomac.

D. H. Hill leaves South Mountain

Hill’s battered division begins its night withdrawal along the Boonsboro–Sharpsburg route.

Rodes and Colquitt separate near Sharpsburg

Rodes takes ground southwest of town while Colquitt continues toward the ford to guard the Potomac crossing.

Hill’s other brigades cross the Middle Bridge

Ripley, G. B. Anderson, and Garland’s brigade under McRae form on the high ground north of Sharpsburg.

Hill counts about 3,000 muskets

His surviving infantry is weak, but divisional and reserve artillery gives the position substantial gun support.

Longstreet waits for the trains

D. R. Jones’s and Hood’s divisions cannot begin the withdrawal until the army wagons clear the South Mountain roads.

Carman describes disorder and straggling

The Confederate retreat succeeds, but exhaustion and the hard march cost Longstreet many men from the ranks.

Lee has about 15,000 men at Sharpsburg

Most Confederate brigades remain near Harpers Ferry, leaving the temporary line badly outnumbered.

Hunger and physical collapse thin the army

The episode uses W. B. Judkins’s search for fruit to show how poor food, diarrhea, bare feet, and fatigue affected Confederate strength.

Lee trusts his veterans

Despite the army’s physical condition, Lee believes its combat experience and senior leadership can withstand the Federal pursuit.

Lee expects McClellan to become cautious

He treats the Union attack at South Mountain as uncharacteristic and assumes McClellan will hesitate before the new position.

Jackson confirms the surrender

A second message around noon strengthens Lee’s decision to remain until the detached formations arrive.

The episode states Lee’s larger motive

Lee still wants a battle with strategic value and accepts a defensive contest as the remaining way to salvage the campaign.

Federal pursuit begins at first light

Skirmishers find the South Mountain gaps abandoned, and McClellan orders I, II, XII, IX, and part of V Corps forward on two roads.

A burial party uses the Wise farm well

Fifty-eight Confederate dead are placed in the well as Federal troops move over the previous day’s battlefield.

False reports inflate the Federal success

McClellan receives and forwards claims that Lee is wounded and that Confederate losses reached 15,000.

Lincoln urges destruction of Lee’s army

The president responds to McClellan’s optimistic telegram from the War Department telegraph office.

McClellan celebrates South Mountain

The victory strengthens his confidence and his sense that he has answered political and professional critics.

McClellan sends Winfield Scott a pointed message

He emphasizes that the defeated army had Robert E. Lee in command, giving the campaign a personal significance.

The Army of the Potomac advances in high spirits

Federal troops see Confederate stragglers and experience the rare morale lift of marching beyond a battlefield they won.

Raw recruits make up about one-fourth of the army

The episode counts about 15,000 men in new regiments and 5,000 recent replacements who lack battle experience.

The Federal army is a patchwork force

Peninsula veterans, former Army of Virginia corps, Burnside’s expeditionary troops, and the Kanawha Division now operate under McClellan.

McClellan remains behind the advance

He leaves headquarters around midday and catches the vanguard west of Keedysville rather than pressing immediately toward the creek.

The reconnaissance begins at 3 p.m.

McClellan and Porter examine the Confederate line after enemy artillery disperses the larger mounted party.

McClellan says it is too late to attack

His official explanation ends the possibility of a general assault on September 15.

The temporary imbalance is measured

The episode contrasts Lee’s estimated 15,000 with more than twice that number immediately available to McClellan.

Lee’s visible stand creates uncertainty

McClellan expected a rear guard protecting a retreat but instead found the Confederate commander deployed in apparent strength.

The episode’s map recommendations are given

The hosts recommend Bradley M. Gottfried’s “The Maps of Antietam” and the McElfresh Antietam battlefield map.
