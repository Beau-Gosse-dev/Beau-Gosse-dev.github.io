Episode 476 · April 25–May 20, 1864 · Alexandria, Yellow Bayou, and Simmesport, Louisiana

RED RIVER CAMPAIGN (Part the Tenth)

Banks returned to Alexandria without capturing Shreveport. Joseph Bailey’s temporary dams raised the Red River enough for Porter’s trapped gunboats to pass the rapids, after which the army and fleet withdrew. Union troops burned much of Alexandria on May 13. Taylor attacked the retreating column but could not prevent it from crossing the Atchafalaya River at Simmesport on May 19–20. The campaign failed to achieve its principal objectives, delayed Grant’s planned operation against Mobile, and ended Banks’s effective field command

What happened

The campaign entered its final phase

Banks had escaped Taylor’s attempted trap at Monett’s Ferry and was retreating toward Alexandria.

The army returned to Alexandria

The lead elements entered Alexandria on April 25, thirty days after leaving the town for the advance toward Shreveport.

Mansfield and Pleasant Hill ended the advance

The defeat at Mansfield on April 8 and the fighting at Pleasant Hill on April 9 led Banks to retreat instead of continuing toward Shreveport.

Grant’s orders conflicted with Banks’s plans

Grant had ordered A. J. Smith’s troops returned and wanted Banks prepared for an operation against Mobile. Banks had written that he intended to push the Confederates toward Texas.

Grant sent Hunter to Banks

Major General David Hunter carried instructions that Mobile mattered more than Shreveport and reached Alexandria on April 27.

Grant sought Banks’s removal

Reports of the campaign’s failure led Grant to ask that Banks be replaced. Lincoln delayed the removal because Banks still had political value in New England.

Smith’s troops remained with the expedition

Grant allowed the troops to stay after learning that Porter needed them to protect the stranded fleet. The delay forced Grant to abandon the immediate Mobile operation.

Canby received authority over Banks

Edward R. S. Canby took command of the new Military Division of West Mississippi. Banks retained the Department of the Gulf in name but became Canby’s subordinate.

Porter’s fleet was trapped

Extremely low water left ten named gunboats above the Alexandria rapids. The vessels might have to be destroyed if the army withdrew before they escaped.

Bailey proposed temporary dams

Lieutenant Colonel Joseph Bailey applied logging experience from Wisconsin to a plan for raising the river above the rapids.

Banks approved the plan

After Franklin arranged a meeting with Banks and Hunter on April 29, Banks authorized construction.

Soldiers built the dams

Maine soldiers with logging experience built a tree dam from one bank. Black troops from the Corps d’Afrique built crib structures from barges loaded with brick, stone, and demolished building material.

The first dams raised the river

By May 8 the structures had produced a deeper pool above the rapids, allowing the shallow-draft vessels to move close to the gap.

Four gunboats passed on May 9

When part of the dam broke, Lexington passed through the opening, followed by Osage, Neosho, and Fort Hindman.

Bailey added wing dams

The first release did not free every vessel, so Bailey repaired the works and added structures that directed more water into the channel.

The remaining ironclads escaped

Guns, armor, and cotton were removed to reduce draft. On May 13 the water was deep enough for the lightened ships to pass.

The army left Alexandria

The first troops marched out at 7 a.m. on May 13. Union soldiers then set fires that destroyed most of the town despite attempts by staff officers and the provost guard to stop them.

Taylor attacked the retreat

Confederate forces harassed the column and fought an artillery action about forty miles below Alexandria on May 16.

The armies fought at Yellow Bayou

A sharp engagement on May 18 failed to stop Banks’s withdrawal because Taylor did not have enough troops.

The army crossed at Simmesport

Bailey built a bridge across the Atchafalaya River. The Union army crossed on May 19–20 and dismantled the bridge, ending Taylor’s pursuit and the campaign.

Canby reduced Banks’s role

Canby met the column at Simmesport and informed Banks of the new command structure. Banks returned to administrative duties in New Orleans.

Taylor broke with Kirby Smith

Taylor blamed Smith for weakening the Confederate force in Louisiana and requested relief. Smith ordered his arrest, but Taylor was promoted and transferred to another department.

Shreveport remained beyond Union control

Federal troops did not enter the city until June 6, 1865, nearly fourteen months after Banks’s failed advance.
