What happened

The podcast begins the Atlanta campaign

The campaign is introduced as a military operation whose outcome also affected Northern support for the war and Lincoln’s reelection.

Grant planned simultaneous offensives

Grant intended to pressure the principal Confederate armies at the same time so they could not send reinforcements from one theater to another.

Sherman received the Atlanta assignment

Sherman’s armies were to move from Chattanooga against Joseph E. Johnston’s Army of Tennessee and into the Confederate interior.

The spring campaign dates are established

Sherman entered north Georgia on May 5 and opened operations against Johnston on May 7, as Grant began the Overland Campaign in Virginia.

The account returns to January 1864

The episode explains the political, military, and logistical conditions that shaped the spring offensives.

The United States held major 1863 victories

Gettysburg, Vicksburg, Port Hudson, and Missionary Ridge improved the military position, but the war remained unfinished.

U.S. losses were already extensive

Nearly two million men had been mustered and at least 250,000 had died; disease caused most noncombat deaths.

War weariness affected Northern politics

Casualties, taxation, conscription, restrictions on civil liberties, and opposition to emancipation increased criticism of the administration.

Democrats opposed Republican war policy

The party’s platform emphasized restoring the prewar Union and Constitution and included substantial opposition to emancipation.

Recent elections still favored Republicans

At the start of 1864 Democrats held the governorships of New York and New Jersey, while Republicans had won the other state elections held in autumn 1863.

Battlefield results determined political support

Military failure and rising casualties could reduce support for Lincoln, while successful campaigns could strengthen it before the November election.

Lincoln needed an effective senior commander

He sought a general who would use U.S. manpower and industry, coordinate military operations with national policy, and produce results before public support collapsed.

Grant became the leading candidate

His record at Fort Donelson, Vicksburg, and Chattanooga convinced Lincoln that he might direct the armies effectively.

A winter advance was impractical

Bad roads, exhausted troops, inadequate rations, and an incomplete transportation system prevented an immediate move into Georgia.

Supply was the central problem

Warehouses at Louisville, Nashville, and Memphis held supplies, but the armies at Chattanooga and Knoxville remained on reduced rations because the railroad could not move enough freight.

Daniel McCallum rebuilt railroad capacity

Grant sent the military railroad director to reopen and improve the route to Chattanooga. The first train carrying crackers arrived there in mid-January.

The rail system lacked equipment

Only 39 locomotives and about 400 cars served 292 miles in Tennessee, compared with 40 locomotives and 800 cars serving about 70 miles for the Army of the Potomac.

Grant centralized railroad control

McCallum received authority over the railroads in the Military Division of the Mississippi and set a goal of 200 locomotives and 3,000 cars.

Longstreet remained near Knoxville

About 20,000 Confederate troops still occupied eastern Tennessee. Grant had to determine whether they could threaten Chattanooga or reinforce Johnston.

Foster’s advance ended without a battle

Grant ordered John G. Foster to drive Longstreet away. Foster moved with about 20,000 men but turned back after finding the Confederate position too strong.

Schofield replaced Foster

Grant placed John M. Schofield in command of the Army of the Ohio and expected him to secure eastern Tennessee before joining the Atlanta operation.

Sherman prepared a strike into Mississippi

Grant authorized Sherman to move from Vicksburg to Meridian and destroy railroads, supplies, and other military infrastructure before the main spring campaign.

The Meridian expedition began

After warning Mississippi residents on January 31, Sherman marched east from Vicksburg on February 3.
