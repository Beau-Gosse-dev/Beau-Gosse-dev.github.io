Episode 161 · June 27, 1862 · Gaines’s Mill battlefield north of the Chickahominy River, near Mechanicsville and Richmond in Hanover County, Virginia

SEVEN DAYS' BATTLES: GAINES'S MILL (Part the Third)

Confederate victory. A late assault by Robert E. Lee’s army broke Fitz John Porter’s reinforced V Corps line behind Boatswain’s Swamp. Porter’s survivors withdrew across the Chickahominy after dark. The victory cost Lee about 8,750 casualties and came too late in the day to destroy Porter’s command
Episode scopeThe final assault, Federal retreat, and command assessment at Gaines’s MillThis episode resumes the June 27 battle after earlier Confederate attacks had failed to break Porter’s position.
Battlefield positionPorter faced west and north behind Boatswain’s Swamp with the Chickahominy at his rearThe National Park Service describes a defensive arc about one and one-half miles long. The episode describes the late line as part of a front extending roughly two miles.
Late Confederate frontD. H. Hill on the left; Ewell, Winder, Lawton, and A. P. Hill in the center; Whiting and Longstreet on the rightThe alignment was assembled after delayed marches, earlier attacks, and piecemeal deployment. Exact brigade boundaries shifted during the fighting.
Whiting enters the lineLee placed Whiting to the right of A. P. Hill after 5 p.m.Whiting’s division, with John B. Hood’s brigade on the left and Evander M. Law’s on the right, filled much of the gap between A. P. Hill and Longstreet.
Jackson’s roleJackson ordered his divisions forward but did not personally place them for the final assaultLee had already directed Ewell and Whiting into the battle. The episode treats Jackson’s delayed march and limited battlefield direction as a serious failure.
Final attack strength in the episodeAbout 32,000 Confederates against roughly 32,000–34,000 United States troopsThese figures describe forces available along the late-day front, not total forces present during the whole battle. The National Park Service lists 34,214 United States and 57,018 Confederate personnel engaged by its broader accounting.
Federal command problemPorter had almost no organized reserve when the final assault developedRegiments from McCall’s and Slocum’s divisions had been inserted where needed, reducing the ability of their division and brigade commanders to control intact formations during a breakthrough.
Porter’s rightGeorge Sykes’s regulars, reinforced by Joseph J. Bartlett’s brigadeSykes’s brigades were commanded by Robert C. Buchanan, Charles S. Lovell, and Gouverneur K. Warren. They faced D. H. Hill and elements of Jackson’s command.
Porter’s leftGeorge W. Morell’s division on the Watt House plateauThe brigades of Charles Griffin, John H. Martindale, and Daniel Butterfield, with attached units from McCall and Slocum, faced Whiting and Longstreet.
D. H. Hill’s advanceA slow attack through wooded, boggy ground fixed Sykes’s divisionHill made limited progress for more than an hour, but the pressure helped prevent Sykes from reinforcing other sectors.
20th North Carolina losses272 casualties in the episode’s accountThe regiment, part of Samuel Garland’s brigade, was in its first battle. The episode gives the loss as about 40 percent of its strength.
Lawton’s brigadeAbout 3,600 men in its first combatLawton’s large Georgia brigade crossed the swamp after a prolonged struggle, gained the Federal side, and paused to restore alignment in smoke and fading light.
Longstreet’s attackFive brigades advanced from the Powhite Creek area toward the Federal leftLongstreet first began a diversion, then changed to a full assault after Lee required stronger action.
Whiting’s attack orderLaw and Hood were to advance rapidly without stopping to fireThe National Park Service confirms that speed and momentum were central to Hood’s instructions. Hood led the 4th Texas and 18th Georgia toward the creek.
George E. Pickett woundedShot near the right shoulder during Longstreet’s advanceThe National Park Service trail guide states that Pickett was hit after his brigade halted under heavy fire near the creek. He survived.
BreakthroughThe Federal line failed in several sectors at nearly the same timeHood’s, Wilcox’s, Lawton’s, and D. H. Hill’s troops later advanced competing claims. The evidence does not establish a precise first breakthrough across the entire front.
Federal capturesNearly 1,000 men from two regiments of George W. Taylor’s brigade were capturedOther pockets of United States troops were also cut off during the retreat, especially near the breakthrough on the Federal left.
Forward artillery lossesTen United States guns were abandoned along the main line in the episode’s accountConfederate infantry closed on the batteries as supporting Federal infantry withdrew and battery horses were shot.
Cooke’s cavalry chargeAbout 250 United States cavalrymen attacked shortly after 8 p.m.Philip St. George Cooke ordered the charge without Porter’s authorization. Its repulse disrupted the reserve artillery position and cost about 50 troopers in the episode’s account.
Reserve artillery lossesNine additional cannon were abandoned after the cavalry chargeThe episode treats the charge as a costly local action, not the cause of Porter’s already developing retreat.
Late reinforcementsTwo brigades from Edwin V. Sumner’s II Corps arrived near 8 p.m.They arrived too late to restore the main position and instead helped cover the withdrawal toward the river.
Night withdrawalPorter’s command crossed the Alexander and Grapevine bridgesDarkness prevented Lee from fully exploiting the breakthrough and allowed the remnants of V Corps to rejoin the main army south of the Chickahominy.
Battle casualtiesUnited States 6,837; Confederacy 8,750These are the National Park Service battle-summary figures. The episode rounds Porter’s loss to nearly 7,000 and Richmond National Battlefield Park rounds the Confederate loss to approximately 9,000. Counts vary by source and method.
Operational consequenceMcClellan abandoned the Richmond and York River Railroad supply line and continued toward the James RiverThe National Park Service identifies Gaines’s Mill as the defeat that confirmed McClellan’s decision to change his base and retreat away from Richmond.
Recommended book“The Richmond Campaign of 1862: The Peninsula and the Seven Days,” edited by Gary W. GallagherThe episode recommends this essay collection for further study of the campaign.

What happened

The episode returns to Gaines’s Mill

The action resumes late on Friday, June 27, with Lee trying to concentrate his army before dark.

Jackson calls for his remaining divisions

A confused message delays Whiting until Robert L. Dabney clarifies the order.

Lee places Whiting after 5 p.m.

Whiting moves between A. P. Hill and Longstreet with Hood on the left and Law on the right.

Whiting and Longstreet coordinate

Their late attacks produce the strongest concentration yet against the Federal line.

Lee meets Jackson

Lee addresses Jackson’s late arrival and discusses the need for a strong attack.

Jackson orders renewed pressure

He sends orders to advance, although Lee has already committed Ewell and Whiting.

The Confederate front is identified

Longstreet and Whiting hold the right, A. P. Hill and Ewell the center, and D. H. Hill the far left.

Winder and Lawton enter the center

Their brigades move near Ewell while other Winder units remain in reserve farther south.

Pressure develops on both flanks

D. H. Hill is already advancing, and Longstreet converts his diversion into a full assault.

Lee has a nearly continuous front

The episode estimates about 32,000 Confederates available across more than two miles.

Porter has no effective reserve

His exhausted command contains attached regiments distributed piecemeal along the line.

The Federal right is described

Sykes’s regular brigades and Bartlett’s reinforcement face the Confederate left and center.

The Federal left is described

Morell’s brigades occupy the Watt House plateau opposite Whiting and Longstreet.

The final attack has no single start

Separate Confederate advances develop into a broad assault rather than beginning at one signal.

D. H. Hill attacks through difficult ground

His brigades make limited progress but keep much of Sykes’s division engaged.

Longstreet expands the diversion

Five brigades prepare for a full attack after Lee requires action beyond the original demonstration.

Winder and Lawton move forward

They attack near the ground where Ewell’s men had been repulsed earlier.

Sykes falls back toward the McGehee House

Confederate pressure and Federal counterattacks shift the line around the house.

The 20th North Carolina takes heavy losses

The episode reports 272 casualties, about 40 percent of the regiment.

Lawton’s brigade enters its first battle

About 3,600 Georgians press across the swamp and reach the plateau.

Longstreet and Whiting begin the coordinated assault

Their brigades cross exposed ground toward Boatswain’s Swamp under heavy fire.

Lee speaks with Hood

Lee explains the position before Hood leads his brigade into the attack.

Whiting orders a rapid advance

Law and Hood are directed to close without stopping to fire.

Hood closes a gap

He leads the 4th Texas and 18th Georgia to connect Law’s brigade with Longstreet’s line.

Hood orders the charge

The two regiments rush the creek as Longstreet’s troops advance in the adjacent sector.

The Federal position breaks

Martindale’s troops give way, and Wilcox breaks into Butterfield’s sector at nearly the same time.

Claims for the first breakthrough are assessed

The episode concludes that the line failed almost simultaneously across several sectors.

The Federal retreat spreads

Some units withdraw in order while others flee or are cut off on the plateau.

Confederates reach the artillery

Federal guns use canister at short range, but ten pieces are abandoned along the front.

Cooke orders a cavalry charge

About 250 Federal cavalrymen attack after 8 p.m. and are driven back through the reserve batteries.

Nine more guns are abandoned

The repulsed cavalry adds to the confusion around Porter’s reserve artillery.

Late reinforcements cover the retreat

Two II Corps brigades arrive near 8 p.m. and help protect the withdrawal rather than restore the line.

Porter moves toward the bridges

The defeated troops crowd the roads to the Alexander and Grapevine bridges as darkness ends pursuit.

Hood receives credit for the breakthrough

The episode notes the 4th Texas’s prominent place in later accounts while recognizing competing claims.

Confederate losses are summarized

The episode distributes severe losses across Whiting, A. P. Hill, Longstreet, D. H. Hill, Ewell, Winder, and Lawton.

Jackson’s performance is assessed

His delayed march and limited direction of his divisions are identified as major command failures.

Lee’s battlefield control is assessed

Despite earlier misjudgments, Lee assembles enough force to break the position before dark.

McClellan’s conduct is assessed

McClellan remains south of the river, sends limited reinforcements, and leaves Porter to hold the exposed wing.

The book recommendation begins

The recommended volume is Gary W. Gallagher’s edited collection on the Richmond Campaign of 1862.

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Col. John Marshall

Commander, 4th Texas Infantry. Killed by a long-range shot during the approach to Boatswain’s Swamp. The National Park Service trail guide places the event about 550 yards north of the creek.

Col. Jesse A. Gove

Commander, 22nd Massachusetts Infantry. Killed while his regiment fought in Martindale’s brigade. His body was not recovered by the regiment after the Federal retreat.
