What happened

Thomas identified Snake Creek Gap

A U.S. reconnaissance from Chattanooga found a possible route to turn the Dalton defenses and reach the Confederate railroad.

Grant placed Sherman in theater command

Sherman took command of the Military Division of the Mississippi in March 1864 with Grant’s full confidence.

Sherman made supply the central planning problem

He used his prior experience as a supply officer to prepare the armies before the campaign began.

Four months of supplies were accumulated

In seven weeks, U.S. depots at Nashville and Chattanooga received enough material for the spring and summer.

The system supported 100,000 men and 35,000 animals

Food, forage, clothing, shoes, medicine, ammunition, and other stores had to move from Nashville to the front.

Railroads replaced river transport south of Chattanooga

The Tennessee, Cumberland, Ohio, and Mississippi Rivers had supported earlier campaigns, but the advance into Georgia required rail supply.

Two routes connected Nashville and Chattanooga

One ran through Decatur and the other directly to Stevenson; both used the same final 30-mile segment through Bridgeport.

The Western and Atlantic served both armies

The Georgia-owned line ran from Chattanooga through northwest Georgia to Atlanta and carried supplies in opposite directions.

U.S. troops rebuilt the feeder railroads

Grenville Dodge supervised repair of 102 miles of track and 182 bridges between Nashville and Decatur.

Daniel McCallum directed military railroads

Adna Anderson managed transportation, while William W. Wright directed construction and repair.

Sherman reserved railroad capacity for the army

He closed the lines south of Nashville to civilian traffic and stopped using military transport to supply nearby civilians.

Federal authorities seized arriving equipment

McCallum retained locomotives and freight cars reaching Nashville to increase daily traffic toward Chattanooga.

Repair material and crews waited on special trains

Rails, ties, bridge material, and construction teams were staged to respond quickly to damage.

U.S. troops guarded the railroad

An infantry division, other detachments, blockhouses, and 100-day regiments protected tracks and important bridges.

Repairs kept pace with the advance

Trains often reached newly occupied areas only one or two days after U.S. troops and construction crews.

The railroad also constrained Sherman

It was his only dependable supply line south of Chattanooga, so the armies could not move far from it for long.

Reduced wagon trains limited off-railroad operations

Sherman cut wagon numbers to increase marching speed but reduced the supplies the armies could carry away from the track.

North Georgia could not feed the armies

Previous consumption, crop failures, sparse population, and Confederate removals left little food or forage available.

Johnston faced a smaller supply requirement

About 45,000 men camped close to Dalton could draw directly from the Western and Atlantic.

Confederate animals wintered farther south

About two-thirds of the cavalry mounts and half of the artillery horses moved toward forage along the Coosa and Etowah Rivers.

River and rail links fed Dalton

Steamboats carried Alabama supplies to Rome, while railroads connected Atlanta with agricultural and industrial regions farther south.

Johnston forced improvements on the railroad

He warned Richmond and Governor Joseph E. Brown that shortages might compel him to abandon Dalton.

The Confederate supply crisis eased

By late January, additional locomotives, cars, and operational changes met the army’s daily requirements and allowed stockpiling.
