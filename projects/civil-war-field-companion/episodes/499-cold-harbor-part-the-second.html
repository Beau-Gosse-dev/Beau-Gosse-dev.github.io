What happened

The June 1 Confederate counterattack had failed

Lee’s attempt to retake the Cold Harbor crossroads ended without dislodging Sheridan’s cavalry.

Wright’s VI Corps replaced Sheridan

VI Corps reached Cold Harbor during the morning and occupied the cavalry breastworks north and west of the crossroads.

Smith’s XVIII Corps marched toward the wrong destination

Two mistaken orders sent Smith first toward New Castle and then New Castle Ferry instead of Cold Harbor.

Smith asked Grant for corrected orders

Finding no V or VI Corps at the ferry, Smith sent an aide to Grant and briefly began bridging the Pamunkey River.

Babcock redirected XVIII Corps

Orville Babcock told Smith that Cold Harbor was the intended destination. The corps reversed course in heat and heavy dust.

Meade gave Smith two incompatible tasks

Smith was ordered to hold the three-mile road to Bethesda Church and cooperate in an attack, though only about 10,000 of his men were present.

Wright waited for Smith

Although Meade ordered an immediate attack, Wright limited his action to strong skirmishing until XVIII Corps arrived.

VI and XVIII Corps formed an arched line

Wright deployed three divisions south and west of Cold Harbor. Smith extended the line north but had to use a full division to protect the exposed flank.

The U.S. attack began about 6 p.m.

Four divisions from VI and XVIII Corps advanced across as much as 1,200 yards toward a partly entrenched Confederate line.

A ravine provided a covered approach

Where the two U.S. corps met, a ravine allowed troops to approach a gap near Thomas Clingman’s Confederate brigade.

Upton reached within 70 yards

Emory Upton’s brigade, led by about 1,500 men of the 2nd Connecticut Heavy Artillery, used the ravine to approach Clingman’s line.

Truex entered the Confederate flank

William Truex’s brigade moved around the position, captured several hundred men, and briefly opened a breach.

The breach received no reinforcements

Kershaw brought parts of four Confederate brigades against the opening. Truex was wounded, and Upton withdrew after dark.

The June 1 attack ended with heavy losses

U.S. losses reached several thousand against about 600 Confederate casualties, and the main Confederate line remained intact.

Grant ordered Hancock to Cold Harbor

Grant directed II Corps to march south, join Wright and Smith, and participate in a larger attack.

Army headquarters blamed staff and corps failures

Grant and Meade were angry about Smith’s misdirection, Warren’s failure to attack an exposed Confederate column, and Wright’s delayed assault.

Smith rejected an immediate renewed attack

He told Meade that ammunition shortages, an extended line, and troop exhaustion made an attack the next morning impractical.

Hancock began a night march

A proposed shortcut narrowed until artillery and wagons could not pass, forcing the lead division to turn back and costing the corps several hours.

II Corps arrived exhausted on June 2

The leading division reached Cold Harbor about 6:30 a.m.; the remaining troops were still spread along the road.

The assault was postponed

Meade first set 5 p.m. on June 2, but Grant delayed the attack until 4:30 a.m. on June 3 so the corps could reconnoiter and coordinate.

Lee attacked the U.S. right instead

Seeing troops shift toward Cold Harbor, Lee ordered Jubal Early to turn the weakened U.S. flank near Bethesda Church.

Heth reinforced Early

Henry Heth committed his full division, giving Early four divisions for the attack.

The attack surprised Burnside

Confederates captured most of a 600-man picket line, flanked two IX Corps brigades, and struck a V Corps division sent by Warren.

Early lacked enough troops to turn the flank

The U.S. line bent but held. Early ended the attack after taking several hundred prisoners.

Grant retained the initiative

With Lee’s third counterattack in four days unsuccessful, the planned U.S. assault at Cold Harbor remained scheduled for the next morning.
