Episode 113 · April 3–6, 1862; ending with the opening shots at about 5 a.m. on April 6 · The roads from Corinth, Mississippi, to the United States camps around Shiloh Church and Pittsburg Landing, Tennessee

SHILOH (Part the Third)

Rain, mud, poor staff work, and inexperienced troops delayed the Confederate approach for two days. Beauregard and Bragg argued for canceling the attack, but Johnston ordered it to proceed. Grant and Sherman received repeated reports of nearby Confederates but did not expect a general attack or fortify the camps. Everett Peabody sent a 400-man patrol before dawn on April 6; it met Aaron Hardcastle’s Mississippi battalion in Fraley Field and opened the Battle of Shiloh

What happened

The armies were twenty-three miles apart

Grant’s army camped around Pittsburg Landing while Johnston and Beauregard assembled the Confederate army at Corinth.

Johnston’s intended attack emphasized the river flank

The strongest push was to drive the United States left away from Pittsburg Landing and trap the army against Owl and Snake creeks.

The written order stacked the corps

Hardee formed the first line, with Bragg, Polk, and Breckinridge following across the same frontage.

The Confederate approach began

The army had to move twenty-three miles from Corinth to its deployment area near the United States camps.

Rain and poor staff work disrupted the march

Units used the wrong roads, blocked one another, and struggled to move artillery and wagons through deep mud.

More than 7,600 sick troops remained behind

Disease in the camps near Corinth reduced the force before the march began.

Two roads carried the army north

The Ridge and Monterey roads connected through smaller lanes and converged about five miles from Pittsburg Landing.

Most of the army reached position

By the evening of April 5, about 40,000 Confederates were prepared to attack the next morning.

Beauregard proposed canceling the attack

He believed the army lacked food and that two days of skirmishing had warned Grant.

The commanders debated withdrawal

Beauregard, Bragg, Polk, Breckinridge, and Johnston met on Saturday evening. Bragg supported cancellation; Polk and Johnston supported attack.

Johnston ordered the attack to proceed

Reports indicated that the United States camps had not prepared for a general attack, so Johnston directed the assault to begin at daylight.

The Confederates bivouacked without fires

The troops slept close to the enemy in cold conditions, and many had little food or rest.

Grant did not expect an attack

He believed the Confederate concentration at Corinth was defensive and remained at Savannah, nine miles downriver.

United States reconnaissance remained limited

Halleck’s order to avoid a general engagement contributed to shallow patrols and the dismissal of skirmishing as routine activity.

The camps had no field fortifications

Grant, Sherman, and C.F. Smith treated Pittsburg Landing as a base for an offensive rather than a position likely to be attacked.

The camp area could become a trap

A successful Confederate drive away from the river could pin Grant’s army against the creeks and cut it off from Pittsburg Landing.

Sherman and Grant discounted the danger

Both sent messages on April 5 saying that they did not expect a major attack.

The Confederate lines moved close to the camps

Hardee deployed first, Bragg formed about 1,000 yards behind him, Polk followed, and Breckinridge came into reserve.

Reports increased along the picket line

United States soldiers in the forward camps saw Confederate cavalry, skirmishers, and other signs of a large force nearby.

Jesse Appler alerted Sherman

The 53rd Ohio formed in Ray Field after reports of a strong skirmish line. Sherman rejected the warning and ordered the regiment back to camp.

Prentiss reinforced his pickets

After seeing Confederate cavalry, he sent four companies of the 16th Wisconsin forward for the night.

Peabody ordered a reconnaissance

At about midnight, Major James Powell reported hearing many troops. Peabody sent five companies, about 400 men, forward at 3 a.m.

Powell entered Fraley Field

The patrol passed the 57th Ohio pickets, crossed the Corinth Road, and exchanged shots with Confederate cavalry before reaching the far fence line.

Hardcastle’s battalion fired a volley

Mississippi infantry opened fire from the edge of Fraley Field. Powell’s men returned fire at about 5 a.m.

Johnston ended the debate

Hearing the firing, Johnston stated that the battle had opened and ordered the army forward.
