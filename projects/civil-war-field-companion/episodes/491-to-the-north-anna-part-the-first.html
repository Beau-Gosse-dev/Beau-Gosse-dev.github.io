What happened

Grant prepared another southeastern movement

Defeats in the Shenandoah Valley and at Bermuda Hundred freed Confederate reinforcements for Lee. Grant concluded that remaining at Spotsylvania offered no further advantage.

The army needed a new supply base

Moving around Lee’s right exposed the road back to Belle Plain. Grant designated Port Royal on the Rappahannock for future supplies and reinforcements.

Grant wanted an open-field battle

The North Anna and Richmond fortifications offered Lee new defensive positions. Grant sought a way to make Lee leave his earthworks.

Hancock’s II Corps became the bait

Grant planned to move II Corps around the Confederate right while the rest of the army remained at Spotsylvania. If Lee attacked Hancock, Grant intended to strike the committed Confederates.

A Confederate movement delayed the plan

On May 19 U.S. formations shifted to a north-south line while Hancock assembled in the rear. Confederate troops appeared near Harris Farm before Hancock could depart.

The Harris Farm action threatened the supply road

Confederates approached the Fredericksburg Road on the U.S. right. Meade ordered Hancock to reinforce the sector and postponed the turning movement.

Grant ordered a counterattack

He sent Horace Porter to the threatened flank with instructions to stop the advance and take the offensive, with Warren’s corps supporting.

Former heavy artillerists entered their first battle

Robert Tyler’s division contained five large regiments trained for Washington’s forts. Grant had recently converted them to infantry duty.

Ewell’s reconnaissance became a battle

Lee ordered Ewell to locate the new U.S. flank. Ewell used his entire six-thousand-man corps, and Ramseur began an attack after mistakenly expecting the U.S. troops to charge.

Three hours of fighting followed

The battle took place in heavy rain. Confederate troops briefly reached a wagon train but could not hold the ground against Tyler and additional heavy-artillery regiments.

Both sides suffered heavy casualties

Ewell withdrew after losing about 900 men. The U.S. heavy-artillery regiments lost about 1,500 but established their ability to fight as infantry.

Grant still believed Lee might attack

The Harris Farm action increased Grant’s hope that an apparently isolated II Corps would draw Lee away from his entrenchments.

Hancock began marching on May 20

II Corps moved east after dark with cavalry protection, reached Guinea Station, and then followed the railroad south.

Hancock reached Milford Station and Bowling Green

Cavalry reached Milford Station by 9 a.m. on May 21. The lead infantry crossed the Mattaponi River near Bowling Green around noon.

II Corps was about twenty miles from support

Hancock threatened Lee’s communications but remained isolated. Prisoners from Pickett’s division showed that reinforcements had already reached the area.

Hancock stopped and fortified

The presence of Pickett’s troops made a farther unsupported advance too dangerous. II Corps entrenched near the Mattaponi and waited.

Lee did not take the bait

He interpreted Hancock’s march as the leading element of a general U.S. movement, not an isolated target, and ordered the Army of Northern Virginia toward the North Anna.

The armies marched on parallel routes

For about thirty-six hours Grant moved through Guinea and Milford while Lee took the Telegraph Road. The armies were out of direct contact for the first time since May 5.

The armies entered largely untouched Caroline County

U.S. soldiers found livestock and crops in a wealthy agricultural area. Unauthorized foraging and pillaging increased during the march.

Officers disagreed about foraging

Some punished pillagers and threatened severe penalties; others considered the behavior inevitable. Grant allowed authorized foraging but treated straggling and theft as threats to discipline.

Lee selected the North Anna as a temporary shield

The river protected Hanover Junction and was crossable at only a few places. Its higher north bank, however, allowed U.S. artillery to command many crossings.

Lee expected Grant to move toward Richmond

Lee thought Grant would cross the Pamunkey and approach from the east or rebuild the railroad for a direct southern advance. He did not expect immediate battle at North Anna.

Lee misidentified Grant’s main objective

Confederate leaders assumed Richmond was the objective. Grant’s operational goal remained the destruction of the Army of Northern Virginia.

Lee asked to concentrate with Beauregard

With more than 10,000 reinforcements and shorter supply lines, Lee told Davis on May 23 that combining forces might permit a decisive attack on Grant.
