Episode 199 · September 17–22, 1862 · The southern and northern ends of the Antietam battlefield around Sharpsburg, Maryland; field hospitals across the Antietam Valley; and the roads through Middletown to Frederick, Maryland, and across the Potomac at Boteler’s Ford near Shepherdstown, Virginia

ANTIETAM (Part the Fourteenth)

A. P. Hill’s Light Division struck the exposed left flank of the Ninth Corps late on September 17. The 8th and 16th Connecticut, 4th Rhode Island, and supporting Ohio regiments were driven back, Isaac Rodman was mortally wounded, and Lawrence O’Bryan Branch was killed. Jacob Cox withdrew the Ninth Corps to defensible ground above the Lower Bridge, which remained in Federal hands. On the Confederate left, concentrated Federal artillery stopped Jackson and Stuart from turning the Federal right. Fighting ended near sunset. Neither army renewed the battle on September 18; Lee withdrew across the Potomac that night. Nearly 23,000 soldiers were casualties, and the Union strategic victory gave Lincoln the opportunity to announce the Preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on September 22
Episode scopeThe final fighting on September 17 and the immediate aftermath through September 20The episode begins with A. P. Hill’s counterattack, follows the close of combat and medical response, and ends with the Confederate withdrawal, limited Federal pursuit, and the battle’s strategic consequences.
Hill’s forced marchAbout 15–17 miles from Harpers FerryNPS interpretation describes roughly 3,500 men leaving Harpers Ferry at 7:00 a.m.; three brigades, about 2,500 men, reached the decisive sector in time to attack. The episode estimates about 2,000 initially available after straggling and trailing brigades reduced the first wave.
Hill’s tactical advantagePosition, surprise, and veteran troopsThe Light Division entered south of the Ninth Corps and struck its open left flank rather than attacking the Federal line frontally.
Exposed Federal flankHarland’s 8th and 16th Connecticut and 4th Rhode IslandThese regiments formed the left of Rodman’s division and the entire Army of the Potomac. Difficult terrain and uneven movement separated the 8th Connecticut from the other two regiments.
16th Connecticut experienceLess than one month in Federal serviceThe regiment mustered on August 24, reached Washington on September 1, and joined the Army of the Potomac only days before fighting at Antietam.
Rodman mortally woundedShot through the chest while reacting to Hill’s arrivalBrig. Gen. Isaac P. Rodman was carried from the field and died on September 30, 1862.
8th Connecticut advanceSeveral hundred yards ahead of the rest of Harland’s brigadeThe regiment advanced with Fairchild’s brigade, captured a Confederate battery near the Harpers Ferry Road, and then lost about half its men when attacked in flank.
Otto’s 40-acre cornfieldHead-high corn and broken ground concealed the attackersThe 16th Connecticut and 4th Rhode Island could not see Gregg’s South Carolinians approaching from the south and west. Smoke, crops, and some Confederate clothing taken at Harpers Ferry added to the identification problem.
Gregg’s attackSurprise fire from two directionsMaxcy Gregg’s veteran South Carolinians struck the inexperienced 16th Connecticut and the 4th Rhode Island in and around the cornfield.
Federal left collapsesAbout 4:30 p.m. in the episodeThe 16th Connecticut routed, the 4th Rhode Island withdrew, and the retreat exposed the supporting Ohio regiments and troops farther north.
Cox orders a withdrawalThe Ninth Corps gives up its gains near SharpsburgCox pulled back the troops near town because Hill’s attack had broken the corps left and threatened to separate or envelop the forward formations.
Three Confederate brigades engagedGregg, Archer, and BranchNPS interpretation places Gregg against the 16th Connecticut and 4th Rhode Island, Archer between Gregg and Branch, and Branch moving north against the 8th Connecticut and Fairchild.
Ohio support lineScammon’s Kanawha brigade changes frontThe 12th, 23rd, and 30th Ohio confronted Hill’s attack. They helped prevent a complete collapse but could not restore the advance toward Sharpsburg.
Lawrence O’Bryan Branch killedShot in the head while leading his North Carolina brigadeBranch died during the final Confederate counterattack and was one of six general officers killed or mortally wounded at Antietam.
Six general officers lostThree United States and three ConfederateJoseph K. F. Mansfield, Israel B. Richardson, and Isaac P. Rodman were the Federal fatalities; William E. Starke, George B. Anderson, and Lawrence O’Bryan Branch were the Confederate fatalities.
Confederate artillery at the final attackMore than forty guns in the episodeLee and his officers concentrated pieces from numerous batteries south of Sharpsburg. Their crossfire supported Hill and helped force Federal batteries west of the creek to withdraw.
Ninth Corps final positionCurved ridge above the Lower BridgeCox’s line held the west-bank bridgehead. Sturgis and Fairchild were on the left, Scammon in the center, and Willcox on the right.
Jackson’s northern counterattack planTurn the Federal right between the army and the PotomacJackson assigned Stuart a small mixed force of cavalry, artillery, and infantry. The movement was abandoned when the strength of the Federal artillery and the extension of the Federal line made the route untenable.
Federal artillery on the rightA concentrated line near the Joseph Poffenberger farmFederal batteries under the I Corps sector suppressed the Confederate guns in about fifteen minutes in the episode’s account and stopped Stuart’s movement before it developed.
Unused Federal reserveMost of Fitz John Porter’s V Corps remained uncommittedMcClellan and Porter retained the infantry near headquarters because they regarded it as the army’s only reliable reserve while both flanks appeared threatened.
McClellan’s order to BurnsideHold the ground and especially the bridgeA courier reported that the Ninth Corps needed men and guns. McClellan sent Miller’s battery but no infantry reinforcement.
Close of combatFiring declined near sunset, about 5:30 p.m.The Ninth Corps retained the Lower Bridge and a west-bank foothold; Hill’s tired brigades did not attempt to drive the corps across Antietam Creek.
Battle casualtiesNearly 23,000 killed, wounded, or missingExact totals vary. Antietam National Battlefield uses an overall estimate of 22,720 and about 3,200 casualties in the final-attack sector.
Deaths after the battleMany wounded soldiers died laterThe episode estimates that 6,300–6,500 men were killed outright or died of wounds. This combines immediate deaths with later mortality and is distinct from the total casualty count.
Letterman ambulance systemOrganized transport and trained personnelThe Army of the Potomac’s new ambulance corps assigned vehicles and trained crews to collect wounded men and move them through aid stations, field hospitals, and permanent hospitals.
Field hospitalsAt least 71 Federal hospitals in the episodeHouses, churches, barns, and sheds across the Antietam Valley held wounded soldiers. NPS educational material describes the Smith barn as one of about seventy-five field hospitals in the area.
Medical supply shortageArmy supplies arrived several days after the battleRail damage, transportation priorities, and the speed of the campaign delayed official supplies. Surgeons relied on what they carried and what civilians and relief organizations supplied.
Clara BartonBrought bandages, food, and lanterns to the northern battlefieldBarton arrived near the Poffenberger farm around noon on September 17 and worked with wounded soldiers and surgeons through the battle.
U.S. Sanitary CommissionIndependent transportation brought relief supplies on September 18The civilian commission’s wagons helped bridge the interval before the Army Medical Department’s larger supply shipments reached the hospitals.
Evacuation to FrederickAbout twenty miles through MiddletownBeginning September 20, ambulances and walking wounded moved east across South Mountain. By September 25, about 600 soldiers considered too badly wounded to travel remained in two battlefield tent hospitals.
Frederick hospital networkThe existing barracks plus six temporary hospitalsChurches, public buildings, and tents expanded capacity. More than half the patients were eventually transferred to Washington, Baltimore, or Philadelphia.
Confederate wounded left behindAbout 2,000 in the episodeLee’s army evacuated those it could but left the most severely wounded in Maryland and near Shepherdstown, sometimes with Confederate surgeons who remained to care for them.
September 18Neither army renewed the battleFranklin proposed another attack on the Confederate left, but McClellan declined. Lee began withdrawing across the Potomac after dark.
September 19–20Limited pursuit followed by the Battle of ShepherdstownV Corps troops crossed the Potomac on September 19. A larger crossing on September 20 was repulsed by A. P. Hill’s division.
Strategic resultLee’s Maryland invasion endedThe battlefield result was tactically inconclusive, but Lee’s withdrawal allowed the United States to claim a strategic victory.
Emancipation consequencePreliminary Emancipation Proclamation announced September 22Lincoln used the end of Lee’s invasion as the military result he had awaited before announcing the proclamation.
Federal force utilizationAbout one-third of McClellan’s available army did not fire a shot in the episode’s estimateThe episode contrasts the heavy losses in I, II, XII, and IX Corps with the limited engagement of V and VI Corps formations.
Recommended booksTwo Gary W. Gallagher essay collectionsThe episode recommends “Antietam: Essays on the 1862 Maryland Campaign” and “The Antietam Campaign.”
Calixa Lavallée4th Rhode Island musician and later composer of the music for “O Canada”The bonus segment identifies the Quebec-born soldier as wounded during Hill’s counterattack. His surname is properly spelled Lavallée.
Transcript normalizationsHarland, Maxcy Gregg, Kanawha, Poffenberger, Pleasonton, Pry House, and Calixa LavalléeThe automated transcript produces forms including “Harlan,” “Maxi Gregg,” “Canaw,” “Paffenberger,” “Pleasanton,” “pry house,” and “Kai Douglas.” This guide uses the historical forms; “Kai Douglas” is normalized to Henry Kyd Douglas.

XII Corps commander. Mortally wounded in the chest near the East Woods during the morning fighting and died on September 18, 1862.

United States

Maj. Gen. Israel B. Richardson

1st Division, II Corps commander. Mortally wounded by a shell fragment after the Sunken Road breakthrough and died on November 3, 1862.

United States

Brig. Gen. Isaac P. Rodman

3rd Division, Ninth Corps commander. Mortally wounded through the chest while changing front against Hill’s counterattack and died on September 30, 1862.

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. William E. Starke

Stonewall Division commander. Wounded three times while leading a morning counterattack and died within the hour.

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. George B. Anderson

North Carolina brigade commander in the Sunken Road. Mortally wounded in the foot, underwent amputation after infection, and died on October 16, 1862.

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. Lawrence O’Bryan Branch

North Carolina brigade commander, Light Division. Shot in the head and killed while leading his brigade in A. P. Hill’s late-afternoon counterattack.
