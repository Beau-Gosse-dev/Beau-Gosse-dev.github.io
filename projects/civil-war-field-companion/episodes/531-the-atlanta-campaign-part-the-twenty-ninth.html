What happened

The armies reached the Chattahoochee

Johnston held the Shoup Line on the north bank. Sherman’s original order had been to destroy Johnston’s army, but Atlanta had become the practical objective.

Atlanta developed around its railroads

The city grew after the Western and Atlantic Railroad reached the settlement in 1845. By 1860, four major rail lines met there.

Four routes made Atlanta a transportation center

Railroads linked Atlanta with Chattanooga, Augusta, Montgomery, Mobile, Macon, the Georgia coast, Wilmington, and Charleston.

Atlanta’s military importance had increased

Earlier Confederate transportation and industrial centers had been captured, leaving Atlanta as a major railroad hub, arsenal, industrial center, and supply depot.

Confederate leaders called for Atlanta to be held

Georgia governor Joseph E. Brown and newspaper editors argued that losing the city would disrupt transportation and supplies across the remaining Confederacy.

Sherman treated the city as a visible military objective

Atlanta’s capture would be clear evidence of U.S. progress while Grant remained outside Petersburg and the presidential election approached.

Sherman chose an upstream crossing

Thomas held the Confederates in place while Schofield moved toward Soap Creek and McPherson demonstrated downstream.

Johnston concentrated against a downstream crossing

Johnston expected Sherman to repeat earlier movements against the Confederate left. Only small Georgia militia detachments guarded the river upstream.

Schofield crossed at Soap Creek

At 3 p.m. on July 8, 50 soldiers crossed on a submerged fish weir. Engineers completed two pontoon bridges after dark, and an entire division followed.

Garrard crossed at Roswell

On July 9, cavalrymen with repeating carbines waded across the river. By 7 a.m. they held a second bridgehead.

The 1st Tennessee Cavalry made another crossing

James P. Brownlow and eight men swam the river with their weapons on a small raft, moved behind Confederate pickets, and captured 12 men.

Johnston abandoned the Shoup Line

During the night of July 9–10, the Army of Tennessee crossed the river, burned the railroad bridge, removed its pontoon bridges, and withdrew toward Atlanta.
