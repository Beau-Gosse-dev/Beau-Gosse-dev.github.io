What happened

The attack opened at 8 a.m.

XIV Corps artillery bombarded the Confederate line. Sam Watkins of the 1st Tennessee described heavy U.S. fire across the sector.

Two U.S. brigades prepared to attack the Dead Angle

Daniel McCook Jr. and John G. Mitchell led brigades from Jefferson C. Davis’s division. James D. Morgan’s brigade remained in support.

McCook formed four regiments in column

The 125th Illinois led, followed by the 86th Illinois, 22nd Indiana, and 52nd Ohio. The 85th Illinois deployed as skirmishers.

A column concentrated force but was vulnerable

The narrow formation reduced the fire U.S. soldiers could return and allowed men to bunch up when the leading regiments stopped.

McCook planned to widen the formation at the works

Allen Fahnestock was to take the 86th Illinois left after the 125th reached the line, clearing the leading regiment and extending the front.

McCook’s brigade advanced

The column moved downhill at double-quick time. Marshy ground, a stream, and brush disrupted the formation.

The skirmishers captured Confederate pickets

The 85th Illinois crossed the valley ahead of the column and overran the Confederate skirmish line.

The 52nd Ohio took its first fatality

Isaac Newton Wyckoff was killed as the rear regiment reformed and began climbing toward the main line.

Eight Confederate guns fired into the assault

Artillery south of the salient fired along the U.S. formation. McCook accelerated to reduce exposure to the guns.

Alfred Vaughan’s brigade opened fire

The consolidated 11th and 29th Tennessee and neighboring regiments fired into the dense U.S. column at close range.

Hidden Confederate guns fired at short range

A two-gun section on Vaughan’s right had been screened with brush and struck the column as it climbed the slope.

The attacking column lost cohesion

Wounded and retreating soldiers moved through the 52nd Ohio. The regiment continued uphill until it stopped about 25 yards from the works.

The 52nd Ohio lost 85 men

Four color bearers were among the casualties. About 300 men from the regiment entered the assault.

A small group tried to penetrate north of the salient

Lansing Dawdy led about 20 men of the 86th Illinois forward where there was less abatis. All were shot down, and Dawdy was wounded near the trench.

U.S. flags reached the parapet

Color bearers planted flags at the Confederate works while soldiers on both sides fought at very short range.

Daniel McCook reached the works

McCook stood at the parapet urging his men forward and was shot through the right side at a range of about one foot.

McCook was mortally wounded

The bullet penetrated the top of a lung. He died on July 17 and had learned of his promotion to brigadier general shortly before his death.

Oscar Harmon took command and was killed

The 125th Illinois colonel led the brigade for only a few minutes before he was shot and died.

Close fighting continued at the parapet

Confederates threw stones and wood at the U.S. troops. One Confederate soldier captured a U.S. flag from the foot of the works.

The brigade fell back into dead ground

After 15 to 30 minutes near the works, survivors moved behind a slight rise 25 to 60 yards away and remained under cover.

Caleb Dilworth assumed command

Dilworth and Fahnestock decided another attack would fail and that a full retreat was also too dangerous. The survivors stayed and dug in.

Some soldiers remained beside the Confederate works

Men too exposed to move waited until they could crawl back. U.S. soldiers scraped out a low defensive line without tools.

The brigade held a position close to the enemy

The misplaced Confederate line left a narrow strip of dead ground. XIV Corps troops used it to remain near the works after the failed assault.
