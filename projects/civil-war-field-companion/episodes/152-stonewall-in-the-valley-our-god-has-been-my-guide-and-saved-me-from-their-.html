Episode 152 · May 30–June 7, 1862 · The Shenandoah Valley and Page Valley in Virginia, from Winchester and Strasburg south along the Valley Turnpike and Luray Road to Harrisonburg, Cross Keys, and Port Republic

STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: "OUR GOD HAS BEEN MY GUIDE AND SAVED ME FROM THEIR GRASP"

Jackson escaped the attempted United States convergence at Strasburg and withdrew south under pressure from Frémont west of Massanutten Mountain and Shields to the east. Confederate bridge destruction, the paved Valley Turnpike, heavy rain, and hesitant Federal movement helped keep the two pursuing columns apart. Jackson then turned southeast at Harrisonburg and concentrated near Port Republic, where he occupied a central position from which he could fight the separated columns in turn. The June 6 rear-guard engagement near Harrisonburg delayed Frémont but cost the Confederacy Turner Ashby, who was killed while leading infantry on foot. The battles of Cross Keys and Port Republic fall outside this episode and begin the next installment.
Episode scopeThe retreat from the lower Valley and approach to Cross Keys and Port RepublicThe narrative resumes on Friday evening, May 30, and ends with the armies positioned near Cross Keys and Port Republic on June 7. It does not narrate the June 8 Battle of Cross Keys or June 9 Battle of Port Republic.
Operational resultJackson escaped the Strasburg trap and kept Frémont and Shields separatedThe United States columns eventually reached the Strasburg area after Jackson had cleared it. Their convergence therefore closed behind, rather than around, the Confederate army.
Strategic geographyMassanutten Mountain separated the two Federal pursuitsFrémont followed the Valley Turnpike west of the mountain. Shields moved south through the Page, or Luray, Valley on the east. Crossings between the valleys were limited, so roads, bridges, and timing controlled whether the columns could unite.
Road advantageJackson used the macadamized Valley Turnpike while Shields faced dirt roadsPersistent rain made Shields’s route especially difficult. The road difference did not alone determine the campaign, but it materially favored Jackson’s retreat and complicated coordination between the Federal columns.
May 31 failureNeither Federal arm reached Strasburg in timeThe transcript says Shields stopped because he accepted an unfounded report that James Longstreet had arrived nearby from Richmond. Frémont’s vanguard ended May 31 about five miles short of Strasburg despite the expectation that it would reach the town that day.
Longstreet reportFalse or unsubstantiated intelligence in the episode’s accountLongstreet had not reinforced Jackson near Front Royal or Strasburg. The guide treats Shields’s belief as a reported explanation for delay, not as an actual Confederate movement.
Confederate impedimentsA seven-mile wagon train and nearly 2,000 prisoners slowed the withdrawalThese are the episode’s figures. The 21st Virginia guarded the prisoners. The exact length of a moving train and number present at any instant cannot be reconstructed with precision.
Forced marchFifty minutes marching and no more than ten minutes rest in each hourThe episode describes Jackson enforcing this pace on May 31. It also says the Stonewall Brigade and 1st Maryland marched about 35 miles without food, while the 2nd Virginia covered about 42 miles and had gone two days without rations.
June 1 at StrasburgJackson held the road open until his detached troops arrivedFrémont’s advance skirmished with the Confederate blocking force west of town beginning about 7:00 a.m. The Stonewall Brigade and 1st Maryland entered Strasburg around noon and passed safely through the junction.
Federal contact dateSources describe the failed convergence with slightly different date languageThe episode emphasizes the June 1 skirmishing and Confederate passage through Strasburg. The U.S. Army campaign brochure says the Federal vanguards met near Strasburg on June 2. These statements can coexist: the arms approached on June 1 and completed contact only after Jackson had escaped.
Shields’s second planMove south through Luray to reach Jackson’s front while Frémont followed his rearMcDowell consented without enthusiasm. The plan was operationally plausible, but rain, poor roads, destroyed bridges, distance, and divided command prevented timely cooperation.
Condition of Jackson’s armySevere straggling and loss of unit cohesionThe episode uses Federal and Confederate accounts describing men, equipment, clothing, and broken vehicles along the road. It quotes a 27th Virginia officer estimating at least 1,000 men of the Stonewall Brigade had fallen out by June 3; that is a contemporary estimate, not a verified casualty return.
Episode title quotationJackson’s June 2 letter to his wifeThe transcript quotes Jackson writing that the enemy tried to reach his rear on both flanks but that God had guided him and saved him from their grasp. The guide paraphrases the letter in the timeline rather than extending the quotation.
Richmond command changeRobert E. Lee replaced the wounded Joseph E. JohnstonJohnston was seriously wounded at Seven Pines on May 31. Lee assumed command of the force defending Richmond on June 1. Jackson did not yet know of the change when he wrote Johnston during the retreat.
Bridge campaignConfederate cavalry destroyed crossings to obstruct a Federal junctionThe transcript says Jackson sent cavalry to burn two bridges and that the bridge at Conrad’s Store was destroyed the next day. It does not name the first two crossings, so the guide does not invent names. On the Valley Turnpike, Ashby’s rear guard also contested bridges as Frémont’s troops tried to save them.
Turner Ashby’s promotionHe received notice of promotion to brigadier general on June 3Contemporary and later sources differ in how they style his rank because the Confederate Senate did not confirm the appointment before his death. The guide uses “Brig. Gen.” in the command display but records the qualification here.
Rain and exhaustionMen marched hungry, barefoot, sleep-deprived, and repeatedly soakedThe episode’s claim that the first four days of June produced more than half a normal June’s rainfall is attributed to an unnamed weather station and has not been independently verified here. The broader fact of sustained heavy rain is supported by the campaign record and eyewitness accounts.
Port Republic decisionJackson chose a central position between the separated Federal columnsControl of the river crossings near Port Republic allowed him to prevent Frémont from readily joining Shields. Brown’s Gap also provided an eastern route over the Blue Ridge toward the Virginia Central Railroad and Richmond if withdrawal became necessary.
June 5 turnThe Valley Army left the turnpike at Harrisonburg for Port RepublicThis southeast movement placed Ewell on the approach from Harrisonburg near Cross Keys and the remainder of Jackson’s force closer to the Port Republic crossings.
Engagement near HarrisonburgA sharp Confederate rear-guard success on June 6Federal cavalry from the 1st New Jersey attacked toward Good’s Farm and was followed by fighting involving the 13th Pennsylvania Reserves. Confederate infantry, including the 58th Virginia and 1st Maryland, checked the advance. Exact casualty totals vary by account and are not stated in the episode.
Ashby’s deathKilled near Good’s Farm while leading infantry on footHis horse was shot, after which he rose and urged the men forward before a bullet struck him in the chest or side. The identity of the person who fired the fatal shot is unknown. Claims for the Pennsylvania Bucktails and suggestions of friendly fire remain unproven.
June 7 reconnaissanceMilroy discovered that Jackson intended to fightFrémont ordered Brig. Gen. Robert H. Milroy toward Port Republic. Near Cross Keys he encountered Ewell’s troops, obeyed orders not to start a general engagement, and withdrew with evidence that the Confederate retreat had ended.
Exposed eastern approachJackson left only a light screen at the South River fordsThe episode identifies one company of the 2nd Virginia Infantry at the fords and three companies of the 7th Virginia Cavalry farther out. This thin security of the Shields approach created the danger that opens the next episode.
Important leader lossesTurner Ashby was killed; Joseph E. Johnston was seriously wounded elsewhereAshby is the only senior commander killed within the episode’s geographic and chronological action. Johnston was wounded at Seven Pines outside Richmond and survived; his wound matters here because it produced the command change to Lee.
Transcript normalizationsStrasburg, Frémont, Luray, Ewell, Henry Kyd Douglas, and James K. EdmondsonThe automated transcript renders these in forms including “Strasbourg,” “Fremont,” “LeRae,” “Yule,” “Henry Kye Douglas,” and “James K. Edmonton.” The guide uses standard historical spellings. “Lante Blackford” is rendered as Lanty Blackford.
Book recommendation“Lee’s Lieutenants: A Study in Command,” by Douglas Southall FreemanThe hosts specifically recommend the one-volume abridgment edited by Stephen W. Sears while noting that the original work is three volumes.

What happened

The race for Strasburg resumes

The episode returns to Friday evening, May 30, with Jackson’s Valley Army trying to reach Strasburg before Frémont from the west and Shields from the east could close the intended trap.

Both Federal columns appear closer

On Friday evening the two Federal forces seemed better placed to reach Strasburg than Jackson, making encirclement appear possible.

Frémont marches twenty miles

The episode credits Frémont’s men with covering about twenty miles in twelve hours on May 30 despite poor condition and heavy rain.

Shields promises activity

Shields told McDowell that rapid action and direct pressure on Frémont could capture or destroy Jackson’s force.

Shields stops on May 31

The episode attributes his inactivity to a false report that Longstreet had arrived nearby from Richmond, although only Ashby’s small cavalry force blocked the immediate road to Strasburg.

Frémont also falls short

Darkness halted Frémont’s vanguard about five miles west of Strasburg after it failed to meet the expected May 31 arrival time.

The Confederate column starts south

Jackson’s men left the vicinity of Stephenson’s Depot before dawn, but a long captured wagon train and nearly 2,000 prisoners guarded by the 21st Virginia slowed the march.

Jackson imposes a relentless pace

The column marched fifty minutes of each hour and rested no more than ten, while Jackson rode with or ahead of the vanguard.

Detached brigades lag near Newtown

The Stonewall Brigade and 1st Maryland, recalled from the Harpers Ferry and Martinsburg demonstrations, stopped near Newtown at 10:00 p.m. with more men strung out behind than remaining in ranks.

Hunger compounds the forced march

The episode says the exhausted men had marched roughly 35 miles without food, while the 2nd Virginia had gone about 42 miles and two days without rations.

Jackson holds at Strasburg

On June 1 Jackson kept the road open until the Stonewall Brigade and 1st Maryland could rejoin the main army.

Frémont’s advance skirmishes

Fighting west of Strasburg began about 7:00 a.m. and grew to include artillery, but Frémont did not force a full-scale action against the Confederate blocking force.

The last detached troops enter Strasburg

At noon the Stonewall Brigade and 1st Maryland marched into town and passed through the narrowing escape corridor before the Federal columns could unite.

Shields proposes a second interception

After the Strasburg opportunity passed, Shields obtained McDowell’s consent to move south by the Luray Road and try to reach Jackson’s front while Frémont pursued from behind.

The Valley Army nears physical collapse

By June 2 sustained marching, hunger, lack of sleep, rain, and straggling had broken down unit cohesion. Federal pursuers found discarded clothing, weapons, equipment, and disabled vehicles along the route.

Jackson rebukes a brigade commander

Henry Kyd Douglas recalled Jackson demanding that a frustrated colonel restore his brigade or yield command to someone who could.

Jackson writes the episode’s title line

In a letter to his wife, Jackson described retiring before enemies moving on both flanks and attributed his escape to divine guidance.

Jackson writes an outdated commander

He reported his withdrawal to Joseph E. Johnston without knowing Johnston had been seriously wounded at Seven Pines and Robert E. Lee had assumed command near Richmond.

Rain makes bridges decisive

Jackson used cavalry detachments to destroy crossings that might let Shields unite with Frémont, including the bridge at Conrad’s Store after two earlier, unnamed spans.

Ashby contests the turnpike bridges

On June 3 the cavalry rear guard tried to destroy successive bridges just before Frémont’s advance could seize them. Ashby received notice of his appointment as brigadier general that morning.

Straggling worsens by New Market

Letters from James K. Edmondson, James Dinwiddie, Lanty Blackford, and Randolph Fairfax describe broken ranks, bleeding feet, hunger, standing water, and only four or five hours of sleep per day.

Port Republic becomes the key position

Jackson recognized that control of the crossings near Port Republic could keep the Federal columns separated and allow him to fight one before the other arrived.

Jackson turns east at Harrisonburg

On June 5 the Valley Army left the Valley Turnpike for the road to Port Republic. Frémont reached Harrisonburg the following day.

Turner Ashby is killed

During the June 6 rear-guard fighting southeast of Harrisonburg, Ashby’s horse was shot. He rose, urged nearby infantry forward, and was killed by a bullet through the chest or side.

Milroy probes toward Cross Keys

On June 7 Frémont sent Robert H. Milroy toward Port Republic. Milroy encountered Ewell’s troops near Cross Keys, avoided a general battle as ordered, and reported that Jackson had stopped retreating.

Jackson divides his defensive responsibilities

Ewell covered the western approach near Cross Keys while Jackson placed the rest of the army above Port Republic, but only a small infantry and cavalry screen watched the South River fords against Shields.

The armies rest within striking distance

The Confederates gained nearly two days of rest around Port Republic while Frémont and Shields moved closer, setting the stage for the next episode’s battles.

The episode recommends “Lee’s Lieutenants”

The hosts recommend Douglas Southall Freeman’s command study, particularly the one-volume abridgment edited by Stephen W. Sears.

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. Turner Ashby

Cavalry commander and rear-guard leader. Killed in action on June 6, 1862, near Good’s Farm southeast of Harrisonburg after his horse was shot and he led infantry forward on foot. The fatal shooter is unknown.

Confederacy

Gen. Joseph E. Johnston

Commander of the Confederate force defending Richmond. Seriously wounded at Seven Pines on May 31, outside this episode’s Valley operations; he survived. His incapacity led to Robert E. Lee assuming command on June 1.
