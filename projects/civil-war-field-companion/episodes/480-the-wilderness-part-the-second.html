Episode 480 · May 4–5, 1864 · Rapidan River crossings, Wilderness Tavern, Orange Turnpike, Orange Plank Road, and Saunders Field, Virginia

THE WILDERNESS (Part the Second)

The Army of the Potomac crossed the Rapidan on five pontoon bridges and halted inside the Wilderness so its supply train could catch up. Confederate signalmen detected the movement before dawn. Lee sent Ewell east on Orange Turnpike, Hill east on Orange Plank Road, and Longstreet over southern roads, intending to hold Grant in terrain that reduced the U.S. numerical advantage. On May 5, Ewell made contact near Saunders Field and entrenched. Incomplete U.S. reports led Meade and Grant to believe they could attack an isolated part of Lee’s army

What happened

U.S. troops crossed the Rapidan

Theodore Lyman watched V and VI Corps cross at Germanna Ford on May 4 at the beginning of the Overland Campaign.

Lee recognized the coming offensive

Increased rail traffic, closed camps, and other activity led Lee to tell Jefferson Davis by April 28 that the Army of the Potomac had completed its preparations.

Lee began the campaign without several formations

Longstreet’s two divisions had returned, but Pickett’s division and the brigades of Robert Hoke and Robert Johnston remained on detached duties.

Grant’s other offensives held Confederate troops away

Simultaneous U.S. operations on several fronts prevented Richmond from returning every detached unit to Lee.

The night march began

Shortly after midnight on May 4, U.S. infantry moved toward the Rapidan while Sheridan’s cavalry cleared Confederate pickets from the fords.

Engineers laid five pontoon bridges

Warren’s V Corps and Sedgwick’s VI Corps crossed at Germanna Ford beginning around 6 a.m.; Hancock’s II Corps used Ely’s Ford four miles downstream.

The supply train used Culpeper Mine Ford

More than 3,400 wagons, almost 600 ambulances, over 24,000 draft animals, and a cattle herd crossed between the infantry routes.

Confederate signalmen detected the crossing

Before dawn, observers on Clark Mountain saw activity at the fords and after sunrise confirmed that the U.S. army was moving east of Lee.

Lee ordered Ewell east

Ewell left a rear guard and moved Second Corps along Orange Turnpike toward the Wilderness.

Hill took Orange Plank Road

Two Third Corps divisions marched east on the road parallel to Ewell while Hill’s remaining division watched the upper Rapidan crossings.

Longstreet selected a southern route

Lee first placed First Corps behind Hill, but Longstreet obtained permission to use shorter back roads that avoided congestion.

Three Confederate columns moved east

By early afternoon, Ewell, Hill, and Longstreet were marching on parallel routes while Stuart’s cavalry sought information around the U.S. army.

The U.S. cavalry screened the movement

Sheridan kept Stuart from learning much about Grant’s dispositions, but made little effort to determine where Lee’s infantry was moving.

Lee considered three responses

He could defend Mine Run, fall back to the North Anna, or move east and strike Grant while the U.S. army remained in the Wilderness.

Lee chose the Wilderness

He expected the dense terrain and limited roads to reduce Grant’s numerical advantage and create a chance to attack.

Ewell and Hill received limits

Lee ordered them to make contact but avoid a general battle until Longstreet arrived.

Meade expected Lee to defend or retreat

Meade judged Mine Run and the North Anna to be Lee’s likely choices and gave little weight to an advance east of Mine Run.

Grant preferred offensive action

Grant’s experience made him more willing than Meade to attack and less comfortable waiting for Lee’s move.

Meade halted in the Wilderness

After about 15 hours of marching, the U.S. infantry stopped so the supply train could close up and the troops could rest.

The U.S. corps bivouacked in two groups

V and VI Corps stopped between Germanna Ford and Wilderness Tavern. II Corps camped east of Chancellorsville.

Ewell and Hill stopped within four miles

Ewell halted at Locust Grove and Hill at New Verdiersville. Longstreet remained about 20 miles away at Brock’s Bridge.

Lee continued east despite uncertain information

Stuart could not determine whether Grant would turn west to fight or east toward Fredericksburg, but Lee chose the more aggressive course.

Fitzhugh Lee’s scouts found the U.S. direction

Scouts reached Chancellorsville and reported that Federal infantry was turning west toward Orange Court House rather than east toward Fredericksburg.

Lee planned to hold Grant in the Wilderness

He intended Ewell and Hill to pin the U.S. army until Longstreet arrived for a larger attack.

Ewell advanced at 5 a.m. on May 5

Second Corps moved down Orange Turnpike and contacted U.S. pickets about three miles east.

Lee warned Ewell not to become trapped

With Hill behind schedule and Longstreet distant, Lee allowed reconnaissance but prohibited Ewell from committing so deeply that he could not withdraw.

Ewell occupied Saunders Field

Edward Johnson deployed on the western edge of the clearing, Robert Rodes extended south, and Jubal Early remained in reserve.

The Confederates built fieldworks

Ewell’s soldiers dug rifle pits and stacked logs into parapets, part of the increasing use of entrenchments during active campaigning.

Incomplete reports reached Meade

Dispatches suggested the Confederates were not present in strength. Meade canceled the scheduled march and prepared to attack.

Grant approved the attack

Both commanders believed they had an opportunity to strike an isolated portion of Lee’s army before the full Confederate force arrived.
