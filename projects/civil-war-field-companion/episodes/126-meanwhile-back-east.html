Episode 126 · July 26–October 10, 1861, with a November 20 army review and an August 1862 framing prologue · Washington, D.C.; the Potomac defenses in Maryland and Virginia; Fairfax Court House, Centreville, and Bailey’s Crossroads, Virginia

"MEANWHILE, BACK EAST..."

George B. McClellan took command of the disorganized United States forces around Washington after First Bull Run and turned them into the Army of the Potomac. He restored discipline, expanded the army, organized brigades and divisions, developed a large staff, and strengthened the capital’s fortifications. The work made Washington more secure and won McClellan intense loyalty from many soldiers, but his inflated estimates of Confederate strength and conflict with General-in-Chief Winfield Scott encouraged delay. By October, McClellan had proposed an army of 273,000 for a broad offensive and resisted pressure for an immediate advance. The episode’s opening looks ahead to the failed Peninsula Campaign and the August 3, 1862 order to withdraw his army from Harrison’s Landing

What happened

The episode opens at Harrison’s Landing

In early August 1862, the Army of the Potomac remained on the James River after the Seven Days Battles. This prologue shows where the story of McClellan’s rise will end.

Washington ordered the Peninsula evacuation

On August 3, 1862, McClellan received the order to withdraw the Army of the Potomac from the Peninsula and join the force being assembled in northern Virginia.

Johnston’s wounding opened the way for Lee

Joseph E. Johnston was seriously wounded at Fair Oaks on May 31, 1862. Robert E. Lee then took command of the Confederate army defending Richmond.

The narrative returns to July 1861

The main story begins five days after First Bull Run, when the United States needed to rebuild its field force and secure Washington.

McClellan arrived in Washington

McClellan reached the capital on July 26 and assumed command of the troops in and around Washington on July 27.

Lincoln gave McClellan his assignment

McClellan was to defend the capital, impose order on the defeated regiments, and create an army capable of operating against Richmond.

Reorganization and training began

McClellan concentrated on administration, discipline, supply, drill, and the formation of a field army rather than an immediate return to combat.

Marcy became chief of staff

McClellan used a large headquarters staff and appointed his father-in-law, Randolph B. Marcy, to coordinate it as chief of staff.

Washington’s fortification accelerated

McClellan endorsed and expanded the construction program that placed forts, batteries, rifle pits, and military roads around the capital.

McClellan exaggerated the threat

He repeatedly estimated that Johnston greatly outnumbered him even as the United States force around Washington expanded faster than the Confederate army.

The Army of the Potomac took shape

Three-month units were replaced by longer-service volunteers, and the growing force received the name by which it would fight through the rest of the war.

Regiments became brigades and divisions

Training advanced from company and regimental drill to coordinated brigade and division maneuvers, supported by cavalry and artillery.

Officer quality remained uneven

Many volunteer officers owed their commissions to state politics or election by their soldiers. Examination boards removed or induced the resignation of officers judged unfit.

McClellan cultivated the rank and file

Long inspections placed the commanding general before the soldiers and helped create the personal loyalty associated with “Little Mac.”

The grand reviews displayed the new army

The episode culminates this theme with the mass review at Bailey’s Crossroads, where disciplined formations demonstrated the army’s growth to soldiers, officials, and spectators.

Scott remained McClellan’s superior

General-in-Chief Winfield Scott disputed McClellan’s threat estimates and objected to the younger general’s efforts to bypass the established chain of command.

Pinkerton reinforced the inflated estimates

Allan Pinkerton’s intelligence organization gathered useful information but produced Confederate strength estimates far above the force actually available in northern Virginia.

McClellan proposed a war-winning concentration

His August strategic memorandum asked for a main army of 273,000 men, supported by naval operations and subsidiary forces, to overwhelm organized Confederate resistance.

McClellan resisted pressure to advance

On October 10 he asked Lincoln not to hurry him. The exchange closed the episode with the tension between thorough preparation and the administration’s need for action.
