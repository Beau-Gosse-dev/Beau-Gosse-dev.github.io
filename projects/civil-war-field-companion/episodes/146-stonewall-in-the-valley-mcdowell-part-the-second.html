Episode 146 · May 7–13, 1862 · The Staunton–Parkersburg Turnpike from Shenandoah Mountain through McDowell to Franklin, with the main battle on Sitlington Hill above the Bull Pasture River in Highland County, Virginia

STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: McDOWELL (Part the Second)

Confederate strategic victory after a tactically costly and disputed engagement. On May 8, Milroy and Schenck sent about 2,400–2,500 infantry across the Bull Pasture River in a spoiling attack against Confederate troops assembling on Sitlington Hill. The United States regiments withdrew after dark, then evacuated McDowell for Franklin. Jackson pursued but did not attack the strong Franklin position and turned back on May 13. The operation checked Frémont’s advance toward Staunton and freed Jackson to move against Banks, although Confederate casualties were roughly twice the United States total
Episode scopeThe approach, Battle of McDowell, and pursuit to Franklin, May 7–13, 1862The narrative begins with the strategic reason for Jackson’s westward move, reconstructs the May 8 fighting, and ends when Jackson breaks off the pursuit and turns back toward the Valley.
Operational purposeDefeat Frémont’s eastern vanguard before turning against BanksJackson intended to protect Staunton, prevent a Federal concentration in the upper Valley, and keep Banks from reinforcing McDowell at Fredericksburg.
Confederate concentrationJackson’s Valley Army joined Edward “Allegheny” Johnson west of StauntonThe episode estimates about 10,000 combined. The National Park Service gives approximately 11,600 on the May 7 march; strength totals differ by date and by whether present, effective, or engaged troops are counted.
United States forces at McDowellMilroy’s brigade reinforced by Schenck on the morning of May 8The episode estimates about 5,000 after Schenck arrived. The National Park Service gives perhaps 4,500, while broader battle summaries count larger forces present in the area. Only about 2,400–2,500 infantry made the spoiling attack.
May 7 first contactMilroy’s outposts withdrew across Shenandoah Mountain toward McDowellJohnson’s vanguard drove back the 32nd Ohio near Rogers Tollgate. United States artillery on Shaw’s Ridge then checked the Confederate advance before Milroy withdrew into McDowell.
Night positionsJohnson about six miles east of McDowell; Jackson near Buffalo Gap about ten miles behind the townThese episode distances are approximate camp locations, not surveyed unit positions.
TerrainMcDowell sat immediately west of the north–south Bull Pasture River under surrounding high groundSitlington Hill, a spur of Bull Pasture Mountain south of the turnpike, rose roughly 500–600 feet above the river. Hall’s Hill and Cedar Knob dominated the north side of the road.
Place-name variantsSitlington Hill is also recorded as Sitlington’s, Sittlington’s, Setlington’s, and Bull Pasture MountainThis guide uses “Sitlington Hill,” the spelling used by the National Park Service. The transcript generally says “Sittlingtons Hill,” while Hotchkiss’s manuscript map uses a historical variant.
Schenck’s reinforcement marchApproximately 34 miles in 23 hours from the Franklin roadSchenck reached McDowell about 10 a.m. on May 8 with exhausted infantry, artillery, and cavalry. As senior officer, he assumed overall United States command on the field.
United States planA limited spoiling attack followed by withdrawal from McDowellMilroy wanted to attack; Schenck rejected an all-out battle. They agreed to strike the assembling Confederates, disrupt an expected attack, and leave under cover of darkness.
Immediate triggerAn erroneous report that Confederates were placing artillery on Sitlington HillJackson had no guns on the crest. The slope was too difficult for artillery, and guns there could not have depressed sufficiently against troops directly below.
Assault force25th, 32nd, 75th, and 82nd Ohio Infantry and 3rd West Virginia InfantryColonel Nathaniel C. McLean of the 75th Ohio directed the main movement. The attack began about 4:30 p.m.; the hardest fighting developed after 5 p.m.
Confederate initial lineJohnson deployed the 12th Georgia and the 44th, 52nd, and 58th Virginia on Sitlington HillThe 31st Virginia supplied skirmishers near the turnpike. Regiments from Taliaferro’s and Campbell’s brigades arrived during the battle, while the Stonewall Brigade remained in reserve and did not enter the fighting.
12th Georgia salientA forward spur exposed the regiment to fire from three directionsThe regiment fought the 75th Ohio at close range until ammunition and losses forced it back. The 48th Virginia relieved it near sunset.
Terrain advantageThe crest protected Confederate soldiers while they reloadedThe steep open approach forced United States soldiers to climb under fire and limited their view of the defenders.
Terrain disadvantagesSkyline exposure, setting sun, overshooting, and no Confederate artillery on the crestConfederates stepped above the crest to fire and were silhouetted against the eastern sky. They faced west into the setting sun, and many shots passed over troops below. The National Park Service also notes that many Confederate infantrymen carried smoothbores while most attackers had rifled muskets.
Turnpike fight3rd West Virginia met detachments of the 31st and 52nd VirginiaSeveral companies on both sides had been recruited around Clarksburg. At close range, former neighbors recognized and called to one another across the firing line.
Confederate reinforcementTaliaferro’s and Campbell’s regiments extended and relieved the lineThe 10th Virginia formed behind the left; the 23rd and 37th Virginia relieved the 25th Virginia; and the 48th Virginia replaced the depleted 12th Georgia. Exact arrival order and unit frontage varied during the confused evening action.
Johnson woundedA bullet shattered Edward Johnson’s ankle at about 8 p.m.William B. Taliaferro then became the senior Confederate commander on Sitlington Hill. Johnson’s wound removed him from field service for about a year.
End of fightingFiring diminished around 9 p.m.; United States regiments recrossed by about 10 p.m.Darkness, exhaustion, ammunition limits, and the increasing number of Confederate reinforcements ended the attack.
United States casualties259 in the episode: 26 killed, 230 wounded, and 3 missingPublished totals conflict. The National Park Service gives 256 without the same category breakdown; Encyclopedia Virginia gives 259 as 34 killed, 220 wounded, and 5 captured. The episode’s exact breakdown is preserved here but should not be treated as uniquely settled.
Confederate casualties532: 146 killed or mortally wounded, 382 wounded, and 4 capturedThe National Park Service also gives 532. Some official battlefield summaries round the Confederate total to about 500.
12th Georgia losses175 casualties among about 540 engagedThe episode gives 52 killed and 123 wounded. The regiment accounted for roughly one-third of all Confederate casualties at McDowell.
Senior leader killedCol. Simeon B. Gibbons, 10th Virginia InfantryGibbons was the senior Confederate regimental commander killed at McDowell. Cols. George H. Smith of the 25th Virginia and Michael G. Harman of the 52nd Virginia were wounded but survived.
Battle assessmentUnited States tactical performance; Confederate operational and strategic successMilroy and Schenck achieved their limited purpose of delivering a damaging blow and withdrawing. They did not take the hill and abandoned McDowell, while Jackson checked Frémont’s advance and retained freedom to turn against Banks. Sources variously call the battlefield result a Confederate victory, a draw, or a United States tactical success.
Retreat to FranklinThe United States column left after midnight on May 9 and reached Franklin on May 11Milroy’s rear guard destroyed provisions and discarded excess ammunition. The retreating troops later burned timber beside the road, and the fires and smoke delayed Confederate pursuit.
End of pursuitJackson declined to attack the strong Franklin position and turned back on May 13With the immediate western threat checked, he returned toward McDowell, Staunton, and his planned concentration with Ewell against Banks.

What happened

A United States soldier describes the climb

The opening reading follows Lt. Alfred E. Lee of the 82nd Ohio down to the turnpike and up the steep exposed slope under Confederate fire, then records the exhaustion and dead left after darkness ended the attack.

A Confederate soldier describes the crest

George W. Spenagle of the 25th Virginia explains that downhill fire tended to pass over the attackers while Confederate soldiers were exposed against the skyline whenever they rose to shoot.

The Battle of McDowell is dated

The hosts place the engagement on Thursday, May 8, 1862, and connect it to operations around Fredericksburg and Richmond.

Lee links Valley action to Richmond

Robert E. Lee wanted Jackson to keep United States forces occupied in the Valley so they could not reinforce Irvin McDowell’s force on the direct route toward Richmond.

Jackson identifies the two western threats

Banks was advancing up the Valley while Frémont’s forces approached through the Alleghenies. Jackson feared they might unite at Staunton.

Jackson sets a two-stage plan

He intended first to strike Frémont’s advance guard under Milroy, then return east, join Ewell, and attack Banks.

Ewell replaces Jackson near Swift Run Gap

Ewell’s division held the position opposite Banks while Jackson moved to Staunton and joined Edward Johnson for the western operation.

Milroy is identified as Frémont’s vanguard

The episode estimates Milroy’s advance force at about 3,000 and Jackson’s combined force with Johnson at about 10,000.

Jackson meets Johnson at West View

On May 6 Jackson went northwest from Staunton to coordinate with Johnson. Their combined column moved west at daybreak on May 7.

Johnson leads the advance

Jackson placed Johnson in front because of his experience in the mountain region. Taliaferro and Campbell followed, with Winder and the VMI cadets at the rear.

The advance reaches Federal pickets

Near the eastern foot of Shenandoah Mountain, Johnson’s column encountered cavalry outposts that warned the 32nd Ohio near Rogers Tollgate.

Artillery checks Johnson at Shaw’s Ridge

The 75th Ohio and United States guns fired from the ridge, causing the Confederates to fall back. Milroy then withdrew the outpost force into McDowell before dark.

The ground around McDowell is reconstructed

The episode traces the Bull Pasture River, the surrounding mountains, Sitlington Hill south of the turnpike, and Hall’s Hill and Cedar Knob north of it.

Milroy prepares before daylight

Expecting attack, he sent patrols toward North River Gap and posted pickets and cavalry east of the river while waiting for Schenck.

Schenck arrives after a forced march

At about 10 a.m. Schenck reached McDowell after covering roughly 34 miles in 23 hours. As the senior officer, he took overall command.

Schenck and Milroy agree on a spoiling attack

They rejected both passive defense and an all-out assault. A limited evening blow would disrupt the Confederates and cover a planned withdrawal after dark.

Federal reconnaissance climbs Sitlington Hill

Pickets from the 2nd West Virginia and a company of the 73rd Ohio probed east of the river while the 82nd Ohio and artillery covered the turnpike from Cedar Knob.

Jackson and Johnson appear on the crest

Around noon the two generals, their staffs, and a small 52nd Virginia escort reconnoitered from Sitlington Hill and drew increased United States artillery fire.

Jackson does not expect a battle that day

He asked Hotchkiss and VMI engineer Thomas H. Williamson to look for an artillery route around the Federal right, then rode back toward headquarters for supper.

Johnson deploys the first Confederate regiments

The 12th Georgia held the exposed center, the 52nd and 58th Virginia formed toward the left, and the 44th Virginia occupied the right near a ravine.

Milroy launches five regiments

Just under 2,400 men moved forward in the episode’s count. A false warning about Confederate guns on the hill helped trigger the attack.

The attacking columns divide

The 25th and 75th Ohio attacked the center; the 32nd and 82nd Ohio moved against the Confederate right and rear; the 3rd West Virginia advanced along the turnpike.

The hill helps and hurts the defenders

The crest gave cover for reloading, but defenders were silhouetted when firing, faced the low western sun, frequently overshot, and had no artillery on the height.

The 12th Georgia is caught in a salient

Its forward spur allowed the 75th Ohio to press around both sides and expose the Georgians to fire from three directions.

Fighting extends to the turnpike

The 3rd West Virginia encountered troops from the 31st and 52nd Virginia. Men recruited around Clarksburg recognized former neighbors in the opposing line.

Jackson sends in reinforcements

The 10th Virginia formed in reserve, and the 23rd and 37th Virginia relieved the 25th Virginia as additional Valley Army regiments reached the hill.

The 48th Virginia relieves the 12th Georgia

Near sunset the depleted Georgians withdrew without ammunition. Campbell’s 48th Virginia took their place and met a renewed attack by the 32nd Ohio.

The last United States assaults fail

The 3rd West Virginia joined the 82nd Ohio against the right, while the 32nd Ohio struck again elsewhere on the line. Both efforts broke down as Confederate strength increased.

Edward Johnson is severely wounded

A bullet shattered his ankle at about 8 p.m. Taliaferro took command on the hill, and Jackson sent Hotchkiss forward with orders to hold until the Stonewall Brigade could arrive.

The attackers recross the river

After firing ended around 9 p.m., Milroy withdrew the regiments from the east bank by about 10 p.m. A council of officers agreed that McDowell could not be held.

The episode gives the casualty totals

It reports 259 United States casualties and 532 Confederate casualties, including 175 in the 12th Georgia. Published United States category totals vary among modern references.

The result is separated into tactical and strategic effects

The hosts judge the spoiling attack a United States tactical success but the operation a Confederate victory because the Federal brigades retreated and Frémont’s immediate threat was removed.

The retreat begins after midnight

The United States column left McDowell toward Franklin. Milroy’s rear guard burned provisions and threw excess ammunition into the Bull Pasture River.

Jackson begins a delayed pursuit

The Confederates started on the afternoon of May 9, after the main Federal body had gained about 13 miles. Jackson declined an attack when Schenck halted on commanding ground.

Schenck and Milroy reach Franklin

The exhausted brigades arrived on May 11. Frémont was not there, and the poor condition of Blenker’s newly arrived division delayed his concentration.

Forest fires obstruct the pursuit

Retreating troops set heavy roadside timber alight. Smoke and fire slowed the Confederate approach to Franklin more effectively than a conventional rear guard.

Jackson turns back

On May 13 he ended the pursuit rather than attack the strong Franklin position and returned toward McDowell to begin the next operation against Banks.

The episode recommends Tanner’s campaign history

The book recommendation is Robert G. Tanner’s “Stonewall in the Valley,” first published in 1976.
