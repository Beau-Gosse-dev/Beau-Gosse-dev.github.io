SHILOH (Part the Second)

Grant’s army established dispersed camps at Pittsburg Landing and Crump’s Landing while waiting for Buell. The United States position was organized for an advance on Corinth, not for defense. Johnston ordered about 42,000 Confederates north from Corinth, but delays disrupted the timetable. The written deployment order stacked four corps one behind another instead of assigning them separate sectors, creating a command problem before the battle began

What happened

Johnston faced two approaching armies

About 42,000 Confederates at Corinth could fight Grant on roughly equal terms, but Grant and Buell together would have a large numerical advantage.

Johnston chose to defeat the armies separately

He planned to attack Grant before Buell completed the march from Nashville.

Halleck directed Grant and Buell

Halleck remained in St. Louis and intended to take field command after the two armies united.

C.F. Smith led the initial river movement

While Grant was sidelined, Smith took the army up the Tennessee River and Buell began marching from Nashville.

The first troops reached Savannah

The 46th Ohio arrived March 11. About eighty steamboats brought the rest of Smith’s army over the following days.

Smith assigned railroad raids

Sherman was to cut the Memphis & Charleston Railroad, and Lew Wallace was to damage the Mobile & Ohio Railroad near Purdy.

C.F. Smith injured his leg

Smith scraped his shin while boarding a launch. The wound became infected and eventually caused his death.

Lew Wallace completed his raid

His division marched from Crump’s Landing and destroyed a half-mile trestle north of Purdy, then occupied positions from Adamsville to the river.

Sherman moved upriver

Nineteen transports carried his division toward Eastport to reach the Memphis & Charleston Railroad.

Confederates blocked the Eastport landing

Sherman shifted to Yellow Creek, but heavy rain, mud, and an unfordable creek ended the attempt.

Hurlbut reached Pittsburg Landing

Sherman encountered Hurlbut’s division unloading at the river bluff about eight miles above Savannah.

Smith selected Pittsburg Landing as the camp

He ordered Sherman to camp several miles inland, leaving room for later divisions between Sherman and the river.

Sherman occupied Shiloh Church

His four brigades camped about two and a half miles from the river, with headquarters at the log Methodist meetinghouse.

The main army assembled

W.H.L. Wallace’s and McClernand’s divisions joined Hurlbut behind Sherman. Lew Wallace remained several miles away at Crump’s Landing.

Grant resumed command

Grant arrived at Savannah on March 17 after Halleck restored him to duty.

Smith’s condition worsened

The infected leg wound left him unable to command. He died at Savannah on April 25.

Halleck prohibited an advance

Grant wanted to move against Corinth, but orders required him to wait for Buell and avoid bringing on a battle.

The camps were not arranged for defense

The army expected to advance from Pittsburg Landing and did not anticipate a Confederate attack from Corinth.

Buell crossed the Duck River

On April 2, his army cleared a major obstacle but still had about eighty miles to reach Grant.

A cavalry probe alarmed Beauregard

Routine United States activity near Crump’s Landing was mistaken for the start of an offensive.

Johnston ordered the army to march

The April 2 order called for departure at 6 a.m. on April 3, arrival near Pittsburg Landing that day, and an attack at dawn April 4.

The march began hours late

Inexperienced units, inadequate staff work, and unclear orders kept most troops in camp. Hardee finally left Corinth at 3 p.m.

The attack was postponed

Johnston, Beauregard, and Bragg rescheduled the assault from Friday to Saturday morning.

Thomas Jordan wrote the detailed order

Beauregard’s chief of staff prepared a complex march and deployment plan for four unequal corps.

The corps were stacked in successive lines

Hardee formed the first line, followed by Bragg and Polk, with Breckinridge in reserve. Each line stretched across the battlefield.

The order differed from Johnston’s stated plan

Johnston had described Polk on the left, Hardee in the center, and Bragg on the right driving the United States left away from the river.

The deployment would break corps control

Successive formations would feed into the same contested ground and intermingle, reducing the corps to collections of brigades and regiments.
