Episode 197 · September 17, 1862 · Two sectors of the Antietam battlefield near Sharpsburg, Maryland: the Lower Bridge and Antietam Creek on the Federal left, and the Sunken Road from the Hagerstown Turnpike east toward the Roulette farm lane in the Confederate center

ANTIETAM (Part the Twelfth)

Between about 10:00 and 11:00 a.m., the Ninth Corps made two uncoordinated attacks against the Lower Bridge. The 11th Connecticut was repulsed with roughly one-third of the regiment hit, Crook’s brigade stopped upstream, Rodman’s flanking column lost time searching for a usable ford, and the 2nd Maryland was repulsed in Nagle’s attack. Near the Sunken Road, Richardson’s division renewed the Federal offensive. Meagher’s Irish Brigade suffered severe losses, Caldwell extended the line, and Barlow’s enfilading fire helped make the Confederate position untenable. Posey’s failed counterattack, disrupted Confederate command, and a misunderstood order in Rodes’s brigade accelerated the collapse. Federal troops seized the road and entered the Piper cornfield, opening a temporary breach in Lee’s center
Episode scopeAbout 10:00 a.m. to the Federal advance beyond the Sunken RoadThe episode alternates between the first two attacks on the Lower Bridge and Richardson’s attack on the Confederate center, ending before the later Federal withdrawal from the Piper farm sector.
Two simultaneous frontsLower Bridge and Sunken RoadThe Ninth Corps acted on the southern end of the battlefield while the II Corps attacked Lee’s center roughly two miles to the north.
Ninth Corps strengthAbout 12,500 in the episode; close to 12,000 in NPS interpretationTerrain prevented more than a small part of the corps from approaching the narrow bridge at once.
Lower Bridge dimensionsThree stone arches, about 125 feet long and 12 feet wideThe Rohrbach Bridge carried the road from Rohrersville toward Sharpsburg. It became known as Burnside Bridge after the battle.
Confederate terrain advantageSteep west bank, quarry cuts, woods, stone walls, and flanking fireThe east-bank approach ran nearly parallel to Confederate positions before turning onto the bridge, exposing Federal troops at short range.
Bridge defendersAbout 400 in the episode; fewer than 500 in NPS interpretationThe 2nd and 20th Georgia of Robert Toombs’s brigade held the immediate crossing under Col. Henry L. Benning. The episode emphasizes Benning; the NPS identifies Toombs as the brigade commander.
Confederate support at the bridgeFive batteries in the episodeConfederate guns occupied higher ground behind the infantry. NPS interpretation describes a layered defense with artillery and the rest of David R. Jones’s division farther west.
Federal bridge planFrontal pressure plus a downstream flank crossingThe 11th Connecticut and Crook’s brigade were to press the bridge while Rodman’s division and Ewing’s brigade moved downstream. Two other divisions would exploit a successful crossing.
First bridge attack11th Connecticut and Crook’s brigade at about 10:00 a.m.The 11th Connecticut advanced along the creek. Crook misunderstood his role and reached a position roughly 300 to 350 yards upstream, where his brigade was pinned down.
11th Connecticut lossAbout one-third of the regiment killed or woundedCapt. John D. Griswold was killed while attempting to cross the creek, and Col. Henry W. Kingsbury was hit four times and mortally wounded.
Rodman’s assigned forceAbout 3,200 menRodman’s division and Ewing’s brigade found the engineers’ proposed crossing impracticable, then had to march farther downstream after Snavely’s Ford was located.
Reconnaissance failureThe usable downstream ford was not located in advanceLocal civilians had reported Snavely’s Ford, but the Ninth Corps did not establish its location before the attack. The resulting delay prevented the flanking column from supporting the first two bridge assaults.
Second bridge attackNagle’s brigade at about 11:00 a.m.Nagle sent the 2nd Maryland and 6th New Hampshire along the Rohrersville Road while the 48th Pennsylvania and 9th New Hampshire supplied covering fire from higher ground.
2nd Maryland repulse45 percent casualties in about ninety seconds in the episodeThe regiment advanced roughly 250 yards along the exposed road under infantry and artillery fire, then broke off the charge before reaching the bridge.
Richardson’s divisionAbout 4,000 II Corps veterans in the episodeRichardson arrived after French’s three brigades had been stopped and formed on French’s left rather than moving toward Greene’s earlier foothold near the Dunker Church.
Confederate reserve committedAbout 3,400 men in Richard H. Anderson’s division in the episodeAnderson’s division was Lee’s last available battlefield reserve apart from A. P. Hill’s division, which was still marching from Harpers Ferry.
Irish Brigade63rd, 69th, and 88th New York; 29th MassachusettsBrig. Gen. Thomas Francis Meagher led Richardson’s first attack. The automated transcript repeatedly renders his surname as “Marr.”
Irish Brigade lossesMore than 500 of roughly 1,200 killed or wounded in NPS interpretationThe episode describes more than half the brigade falling in minutes. NPS figures give the 69th New York 196 killed or wounded out of 317 and the 63rd New York a 59 percent loss.
Father William CorbyGave absolution as the Irish Brigade prepared to attackCorby was chaplain of the 88th New York. The episode places him moving along the brigade front before Meagher ordered the advance.
Caldwell’s brigadeThe fresh Federal line that replaced Meagher and extended eastRichardson followed the Irish Brigade with Col. John C. Caldwell’s brigade, increasing pressure on the eastern section of the Sunken Road.
Barlow’s enfilade61st and 64th New York fired west along the roadCol. Francis C. Barlow moved his two understrength regiments to a knoll that exposed the shallow bend and the right of Rodes’s line to fire from the flank.
Richard H. Anderson woundedDivision command passed to Brig. Gen. Roger A. PryorAnderson was badly wounded soon after reaching the sector. Pryor did not know Anderson’s orders, and the arriving brigades lacked effective division-level direction.
George B. Anderson mortally woundedAn ankle wound led to his death on October 16, 1862The North Carolina brigade commander was carried from the field, later underwent amputation, and died after infection developed.
Charles C. Tew2nd North Carolina colonel killed in the roadThe automated transcript renders his surname as “II.” He succeeded George B. Anderson in brigade command and was killed during the collapse.
Posey’s counterattackRepulsed north of the Sunken RoadCol. Carnot Posey advanced his arriving Mississippi brigade into the crowded sector. Its retreat spread confusion through the right side of the Confederate position.
Rodes’s mistaken orderA flank adjustment became a brigade retreatRodes ordered Lt. Col. James N. Lightfoot to refuse the 6th Alabama’s exposed right. Lightfoot instead ordered the regiment to about-face and march to the rear, and the movement spread to the rest of the brigade.
John B. GordonWounded five times and survivedThe 6th Alabama colonel fell before the order error. His wounds removed an experienced regimental commander at the point under Barlow’s enfilading fire.
Confederate center breachedFederal troops crossed the road into the Piper cornfieldThe 2nd and 14th North Carolina made a final stand but were hit from two directions. The defenders withdrew toward the Piper farm, leaving the center temporarily open.
Cook’s counterstroke675 men in the episode; more than half lostCol. John R. Cook brought the 27th North Carolina and 3rd Arkansas southeast from the West Woods area against the Federal right. Brooke shifted his brigade west and stopped the attack.
Recommended book“The Gleam of Bayonets” by James V. MurfinThe full subtitle is “The Battle of Antietam and Robert E. Lee’s Maryland Campaign, September 1862.” The author’s surname is spelled Murfin, not “Murphin.”
Transcript normalizationsMeagher, Rodes, Tew, Nagle, Griswold, Pry House, and enfilading fireThe automated transcript produces “Marr,” “Rhodes,” “Charles II,” “Nagel,” “Griswald,” “pry house,” and “inflating fire.” This guide uses the historical forms.

Important leaders killed or wounded

United States

Col. Henry W. Kingsbury

Commander of the 11th Connecticut. Hit in the heel, leg, shoulder, and abdomen while leading the first attack near the Lower Bridge. He was carried to the Rohrbach farm and died on September 18, 1862.

United States

Capt. John D. Griswold

Company D, 11th Connecticut. Killed while attempting to lead his company across Antietam Creek during the first bridge attack.

Confederacy

Maj. Gen. Richard H. Anderson

Commander of Anderson’s division. Badly wounded soon after reaching the Sunken Road sector. He survived, but his removal left Roger A. Pryor in command during the collapse.

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. George B. Anderson

Commander of Anderson’s brigade, Hill’s division. Mortally wounded in the ankle. Infection followed an amputation, and he died in Raleigh, North Carolina, on October 16, 1862.

Confederacy

Col. Charles C. Tew

Commander of the 2nd North Carolina and successor to George B. Anderson. Killed in the Sunken Road during the final collapse. The automated transcript renders his name as “Charles II.”

Confederacy

Col. John B. Gordon

Commander of the 6th Alabama. Received his fifth wound of the day at the Sunken Road. He survived and returned to military service in 1863.
