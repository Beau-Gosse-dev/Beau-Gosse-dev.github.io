Episode 149 · May 23–25, 1862 · Northern Shenandoah Valley, Virginia: Strasburg, Cedarville, Middletown, Newtown (present-day Stephens City), Kernstown, and Winchester

STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: A DAY OF MISSED OPPORTUNITIES

Confederate forces pierced Nathaniel Banks’s retreating column at Middletown and captured men, horses, wagons, and stores, but Thomas J. “Stonewall” Jackson failed to destroy Banks’s main force. Delayed intelligence, divided columns, a two-hour turn south against a small rear guard, looting, darkness, and exhausted troops allowed most of Banks’s command to reach Winchester. Jackson and Richard Ewell closed on the town overnight, setting up the First Battle of Winchester on May 25

What happened

The episode opens on May 24

The hosts place the narrative between Jackson’s victory at Front Royal on May 23 and the First Battle of Winchester on May 25.

Front Royal creates the opportunity

The previous day’s victory turned Banks’s flank, captured most of the Front Royal garrison, and opened routes toward Winchester.

The operational triangle is introduced

Winchester, Strasburg, and Front Royal formed a triangular road network that controlled the race between the two armies.

The two main turnpikes are measured

The episode gives about 18 miles from Strasburg to Winchester on the Valley Turnpike and about 19 miles from Front Royal to Winchester on the eastern turnpike.

The base route links Front Royal and Strasburg

A winding dirt road and the Manassas Gap Railroad crossed the roughly 13-mile southern side of the triangle.

Jackson considers Banks’s escape routes

He judged a retreat toward Winchester most likely but also considered whether Banks might remain at Strasburg, move west, or slip east through Front Royal.

Chapel Road becomes the interception route

Jackson planned to cross from Cedarville to Middletown and strike Banks’s column on the Valley Turnpike once the direction of retreat was confirmed.

Scouts cover the possible routes

Turner Ashby watched the Strasburg–Front Royal road while George H. Steuart’s mounted regiments moved toward Newtown to observe the Valley Turnpike.

Jackson’s infantry waits at Cedarville

The men halted after marching about 80 miles in four days and waited for definite news of Banks.

A courier brings the decisive report

At about 11 a.m., Jackson learned that Steuart had found Federal wagons filling the Valley Turnpike at Newtown and moving toward Winchester.

Banks’s night decision is reconstructed

The episode describes Banks sorting incomplete and conflicting reports after the Front Royal disaster and preparing to withdraw from Strasburg.

The trains start north

Supply wagons began leaving about 10 p.m. on May 23. Regiments south of Strasburg packed their trains and drew closer to town.

Front Royal’s loss is confirmed

Reliable confirmation reached Banks’s headquarters around midnight, when regimental trains were also beginning to roll out.

Half the wagons remain at daybreak

The vehicles already on the road extended for about seven miles, but only roughly half the train had cleared Strasburg.

The depot burdens Banks with 500 wagons

Strasburg’s accumulated stores made the train unusually large for Banks’s field force.

The sick and wounded join the movement

Twenty ambulances carried 78 men while about 450 more sick and wounded attempted to keep pace on foot.

Black civilians flee with the army

Drivers and families escaping enslavement crowded the retreat among wagons, ambulances, horses, and cattle.

The wagon column reaches 15 miles

By mid-morning its head approached Winchester while vehicles still stretched far back along the Valley Turnpike.

Banks’s infantry leaves Strasburg

The soldiers began marching north at about 9:15 a.m., behind the trains and refugees.

Steuart reports from Newtown

His courier told Jackson that the mounted detachment had reached Newtown around 10 a.m. and attacked the Federal train.

The first opportunity is missed

Steuart’s men captured helpless patients and attendants instead of immediately blocking the road by burning abandoned wagons.

Daniel Derrickson is mortally shot

The episode identifies the 46-year-old private of the 66th Ohio as the only man shot during the brief hospital-train attack. He died after writing his name and regiment for his family.

Teamsters abandon wagons

The attack caused panic, especially among Black drivers who feared capture and re-enslavement.

Federal troops drive Steuart away

Cavalry and double-timing infantry reached Newtown before the road was blocked and forced Steuart’s remaining men into the woods before noon.

Jackson splits the Confederate force

Ewell remained on the eastern road with Trimble and Elzey while Jackson took Taylor’s brigade across Chapel Road toward Middletown.

Federal cavalry slows the Chapel Road advance

Maine and Vermont troopers delayed Hotchkiss, Ashby, and the Confederate vanguard for about two hours.

John Hatch brings up the rear guard

Banks’s cavalry commander reached Middletown after the trains, infantry, Banks, and Williams had already passed north.

The Confederate vanguard reaches the turnpike

Hotchkiss and Ashby brought forward about 100 men of the 8th Louisiana, Chew’s battery, and two of Poague’s rifled guns.

The 1st Maine charges

Hatch ordered Federal cavalry down the pike. Louisiana infantry behind a stone wall and Confederate artillery opened at short range and inflicted heavy losses.

Confederate guns rake the wagon train

Surviving horsemen encountered the rear of the baggage train while Chew and Poague fired along a bend in the crowded road.

Jackson cannot locate the Federal infantry

At about 4 p.m. he did not know what portion of Banks’s column had passed north or whether he had cut its head, middle, or tail.

The first order tells Ewell to advance

Jackson directed Ewell to move promptly toward Winchester, but the episode says there is no evidence that this courier reached him.

Jackson turns south and reverses Ewell’s order

Firing south of Middletown led Jackson to send Taylor toward Strasburg and order Ewell to halt and send Elzey’s brigade back.

A small rear guard absorbs Jackson’s attention

The episode identifies the force south of town as only five guns, about 50 artillerymen, 75 infantry, and scattered cavalry companies.

Ewell waits near Double Tollgate

He had halted for about nine hours roughly nine miles from Winchester and had received no useful update from Jackson.

The second order reaches Ewell

At about 5:15 p.m. Ewell received the instruction to remain in place and send Elzey to Middletown, reducing his available force to about 2,000 men in the episode’s accounting.

Jackson resumes the northward pursuit

At about 5:45 p.m. he turned back toward Winchester and sent a third message directing Ewell to advance with his entire force.

Ewell advances before the courier arrives

His patience exhausted, Ewell moved without fresh orders. Jackson’s confirming message reached him about 15 minutes later.

Ewell reaches Winchester’s outskirts

Around 10 p.m. the 21st North Carolina met Federal pickets southeast of town. Firing continued until about midnight.

Banks forms a night rear guard

Near Winchester he sent George H. Gordon back with the 2nd Massachusetts, 28th New York, and artillery. Silas Colgrove’s 27th Indiana was already resisting farther south.

Friendly fire strikes the 27th Indiana

The 2nd Massachusetts mistakenly fired toward the Indiana regiment before the rear-guard line was sorted out.

A volley disrupts the Confederate advance

Fire from the 2nd Massachusetts sent Ashby’s cavalry back through Poague’s guns and into Jackson’s and Winder’s staffs, causing confusion in the 33rd Virginia.

Gordon reaches Winchester

After a stubborn withdrawal from wall to wall and fence to fence, the Federal rear guard entered the town around 2 a.m.

Jackson reaches north of Kernstown

At about 3 a.m., after the night pursuit, the Confederate infantry was collapsing from exhaustion.

Fulkerson forces a halt

He warned Jackson that further marching would leave only a skirmish line by dawn. Jackson allowed the men about two hours of sleep.

Banks wins the race to Winchester

Jackson’s use of Ewell and his southward turn at Middletown cost the time needed to destroy Banks during the retreat.

The First Battle of Winchester is set

Banks prepared inside Winchester while Jackson rested southwest of town and Ewell lay southeast of it.

The book recommendation

The hosts recommend David G. Martin’s “Jackson’s Valley Campaign: November 1861–June 1862.”
