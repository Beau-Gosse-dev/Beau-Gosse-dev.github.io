Episode 474 · April 16–May 3, 1864 · Camden, Poison Spring, Marks’ Mills, Jenkins’ Ferry, and Little Rock, Arkansas

RED RIVER CAMPAIGN (Part the Eighth)

Steele occupied Camden but could not supply his 15,000-man force. Confederate troops destroyed or captured Union wagon trains at Poison Spring and Marks’ Mills, and Confederate soldiers murdered many Black Union troops at Poison Spring. Steele evacuated Camden on April 26. At Jenkins’ Ferry on April 30, his infantry used flooded ground, abatis, and breastworks to defeat repeated Confederate attacks while the army crossed the Saline River. Steele reached Little Rock on May 3. The Arkansas column never joined Banks, and the Camden Expedition failed

What happened

Both parts of the Red River plan were failing

Banks had retreated after Mansfield and Pleasant Hill. Steele had turned toward Camden because supply shortages prevented him from continuing toward Shreveport.

Kirby Smith moved infantry into Arkansas

He took most of Richard Taylor’s infantry north to join Sterling Price and John Marmaduke against Steele.

Steele reached Camden

He entered the fortified town on April 16 with about 15,000 men but urgently needed food and forage.

A foraging train left Camden

On April 17, Steele sent 198 wagons, two cannon, and 200 men of the 1st Kansas Colored Cavalry into the countryside.

The escort grew to about 1,000 men

Additional infantry, cavalry, and two mountain howitzers joined the column on April 18.

Marmaduke prepared an ambush

About 3,100 Confederate cavalrymen positioned themselves at Poison Spring.

The Confederate attack split the escort

James Williams arranged his troops around the long wagon train. Confederate forces struck its front and right flank.

The wagon train was lost

Confederates captured all 198 wagons and four cannon. The episode gives 301 U.S. casualties, including 182 from the 1st Kansas Colored, and 115 Confederate casualties.

Black Union soldiers were murdered

Confederate accounts confirm that many soldiers refused surrender from Black troops and killed wounded or captured men after the fighting.

Steele questioned Banks’s reports

Banks claimed victories in Louisiana while also reporting a retreat. Steele concluded that Confederate concentration made a march south impossible.

Kirby Smith took field command

He reached the Camden area on April 19 and ordered Fagan and Shelby between Steele and Little Rock while other divisions threatened Camden.

Fagan moved against the supply column

Learning that 240 wagons were approaching from Pine Bluff, he made a forced march to Marks’ Mills.

The Union column camped without pickets

Francis Drake’s escort included about 1,440 soldiers and at least 300 civilians and freedom seekers.

Marks’ Mills began on April 25

Fagan attacked from the front and flank. Shelby’s arriving brigade helped overwhelm the Union infantry.

The Union force surrendered

Drake was wounded. The episode reports about 1,300 Union casualties, most of them captured, and the loss of the supply train.

Steele decided to abandon Camden

Without the food carried in the captured wagons, he could not sustain 15,000 men and 9,000 horses and mules.

The evacuation began after dark

Steele crossed the Ouachita River on the night of April 26 and removed the pontoon bridge. Confederates entered Camden the next morning.

Steele marched toward Jenkins’ Ferry

He chose that route to avoid the Moro Bottoms. Soldiers discarded equipment and personal property to move faster.

High water blocked the Saline River

Steele reached Jenkins’ Ferry on April 29 and built a pontoon bridge. Wagons and artillery began crossing late that afternoon.

Marmaduke engaged the rear guard

Confederate cavalry began skirmishing early on April 30 while infantry came up behind it.

Terrain restricted the battlefield

Bluffs, a bayou, woods, flooded fields, and standing water left little room to deploy. U.S. troops built breastworks and abatis near the ferry road.

Price ordered frontal attacks

Churchill’s and Marmaduke’s troops advanced in separate, poorly coordinated assaults across about a quarter mile of flooded open ground.

Union fire defeated the first attacks

Most of Steele’s infantry was still west of the river, not merely a small rear guard. Musketry and artillery stopped Churchill and Parsons.

Walker’s division attacked

Kirby Smith committed another division after the earlier assaults failed. All three of Walker’s brigade commanders were wounded; Horace Randal and William Scurry were mortally wounded.

Steele completed the crossing

After the final Confederate attack withdrew, the Union infantry crossed the Saline and removed the pontoon bridge.

The retreating army ran out of food

Hunger and exhaustion continued until the head of the column met supply wagons at Benton on May 2.

Steele reached Little Rock

The army returned safely on May 3. Kirby Smith could not cross the Saline without a pontoon bridge and ended the pursuit.

The campaign had heavy losses

The episode reports 800–1,000 Confederate casualties at Jenkins’ Ferry, roughly 2,300 Confederate and 2,750 U.S. losses in the Arkansas campaign, and more than 600 Union wagons lost.

Randal and Scurry died

Both brigade commanders died from their Jenkins’ Ferry wounds on April 30 and were buried at Tulip.
