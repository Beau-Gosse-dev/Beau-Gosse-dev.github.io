Episode 192 · September 17, 1862 · The northern Antietam battlefield near Sharpsburg, Maryland, from the Joseph Poffenberger farm and North Woods south along the Hagerstown Turnpike through Miller’s Cornfield to the West Woods and Dunker Church plateau

ANTIETAM (Part the Seventh)

Abner Doubleday’s United States division advanced astride the Hagerstown Turnpike, where John Gibbon’s brigade and Battery B fought at close range against John R. Jones’s division. After Jones was disabled and William E. Starke was mortally wounded leading a counterattack, Andrew J. Grigsby withdrew the shattered Confederates into the West Woods. At about 7 a.m., John Bell Hood committed his two brigades, drove Hooker’s front line back through Miller’s Cornfield, and wrecked the remnants of James B. Ricketts’s division. Federal infantry and Battery B stopped Hood’s advance near the north edge of the Cornfield. By about 7:30 a.m. Hooker’s I Corps and Jackson’s original force were both spent, and neither side held a decisive advantage; Hooker then depended on Mansfield’s arriving XII Corps

Forces and losses

Reported figures retain the scope and estimates of the existing guide. They may cover different units or periods; missing breakdowns are not inferred.

Figures reported in this guide
ScopeReported figure
Hood called forwardAbout 2,300 men in two brigadesHood’s men had been pulled into reserve to cook their first hot meal in days. They abandoned half-baked flour cakes and moved out of the West Woods when Jackson requested help.
Ricketts’s divisionAlmost 40 percent casualties; perhaps 300 still with the colorsRicketts estimated that only about 300 of the 3,150 men who began the attack remained with their colors after Hood’s advance, with additional losses to straggling and flight.
1st Texas loss186 casualties among 226 engaged, more than 82 percentThe regiment surged ahead of the rest of Wofford’s brigade and reached the north edge of the Cornfield. National Park Service interpretation identifies its loss as 82 percent; nine color bearers fell and the Lone Star flag was lost.
I Corps lossAbout 2,600 casualties, roughly 30 percentThe episode says more than three-quarters of these losses fell on the divisions of Ricketts and Doubleday. I Corps was effectively finished as an attacking force for the day.
Jackson’s opening-force lossAbout 3,000 casualties in the episode’s estimateThe episode estimates that Lawton’s division lost nearly half its strength, John R. Jones’s division about 40 percent, and Hood’s division about 60 percent. These figures summarize commands engaged in the opening phase, not the entire Confederate army.

Important leaders killed or wounded

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. William E. Starke

Commander of Jackson’s division after John R. Jones was disabled. Hit by three bullets and mortally wounded while leading the division’s counterattack from the West Woods; Col. Andrew J. Grigsby assumed command.

Confederacy

Brig. Gen. Alexander R. Lawton

Commander of Ewell’s division. Seriously wounded during the opening Cornfield battle before Hood’s division relieved the surviving front line.

United States

Lt. Col. Alois O. Bachman

Commander of the 19th Indiana, Gibbon’s brigade. Mortally wounded during the close fight west of the Hagerstown Turnpike; Gibbon’s report praised his gallantry.

United States

Lt. Col. Edward S. Bragg

Commander of the 6th Wisconsin, Gibbon’s brigade. Wounded during the advance through the western Cornfield; Maj. Rufus R. Dawes succeeded him in command.

United States

Capt. Joseph B. Campbell

Commander of Battery B, 4th U.S. Artillery. Shot through the shoulder while serving his guns; Lt. James Stewart and a depleted crew continued the battery’s defense.

Confederacy

Maj. Matt Dale

Commander of the right wing of the 1st Texas. Killed while reporting the destruction of his wing to Lt. Col. Philip A. Work during the regiment’s advance.

Confederacy

Maj. J. H. Dingle

Field officer of the Hampton Legion. Killed at the Hagerstown Turnpike fence while carrying the regiment’s colors after three earlier color bearers had fallen.
