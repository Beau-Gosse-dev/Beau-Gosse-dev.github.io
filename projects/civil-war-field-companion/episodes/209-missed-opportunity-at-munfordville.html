Episode 209 · August 28–September 25, 1862; fighting and siege at Munfordville, September 14–17 · The Confederate line of advance from Chattanooga through Sparta, Glasgow, and Cave City to Munfordville and Woodsonville at the Green River crossing of the Louisville & Nashville Railroad, Kentucky

MISSED OPPORTUNITY AT MUNFORDVILLE

Confederate tactical victory but an unrealized strategic opportunity. James R. Chalmers’s unsupported September 14 assault failed with heavy losses; Braxton Bragg then surrounded the reinforced United States garrison and accepted John T. Wilder’s surrender on September 17. Short of food and forage, Bragg abandoned the commanding Green River position on September 20 and opened Buell’s road to Louisville
Episode scopeBragg’s advance, the capture of Munfordville, and the decision to leaveThe episode follows the Army of the Mississippi from Chattanooga to the Green River, describes the September 14 assault and September 16–17 surrender, and closes with Bragg’s withdrawal toward Bardstown and Buell’s arrival at Louisville.
CampaignConfederate Heartland OffensiveBragg moved north from Tennessee while Edmund Kirby Smith operated independently in eastern and central Kentucky.
Strategic objectiveThe Green River bridge and Buell’s supply lineThe Louisville & Nashville Railroad and turnpike crossed the Green River at Munfordville. Holding the crossing placed Bragg between the Army of the Ohio and its principal supply base at Louisville.
Bragg’s armyAbout 30,000 men when reorganized at ChattanoogaThe episode describes two wings under Leonidas Polk and William J. Hardee, each with two infantry divisions, plus roughly 6,000 cavalry divided between John C. Wharton and Joseph Wheeler.
Command burdenBragg acted as both army commander and chief of staffHis chief of staff left because of illness before the campaign. Bragg also divided his cavalry between wing commanders, leaving no mounted reserve directly under army headquarters.
Buell’s problemA slow advance on a vulnerable railroad supply lineConfederate cavalry raids, railroad repair, hunger, and Buell’s restrictions on foraging delayed the Army of the Ohio across northern Mississippi, Alabama, and Tennessee.
Bragg leaves ChattanoogaAugust 28, 1862He moved two days before Kirby Smith’s victory at Richmond, Kentucky, crossed the Cumberland Plateau, and reached Sparta on September 6.
Glasgow concentrationSeptember 13, 1862Bragg’s army reached the Glasgow area, about twelve miles south of Cave City and roughly thirty-five miles east of Bowling Green.
Fort CraigRedoubts, rifle pits, trenches, and a south-bank stockadeUnited States troops had fortified the bridge approaches. The works south of the Green River protected both the railroad span and nearby Munfordville.
Initial garrison2,122 men for duty in Wilder’s official reportThe command included the 67th and 89th Indiana, detachments of the 17th and 74th Indiana, one company of the 18th U.S. Infantry, Kentucky troops, cavalry, and four artillery pieces.
Faulty reconnaissanceJohn S. Scott reported 1,200–1,800 raw troops in weak worksScott also claimed that reinforcements could not arrive. The railroad and telegraph still connected the garrison with the north, and the fortifications were stronger than his description suggested.
First surrender demandSeptember 13, about 8 p.m.Scott demanded unconditional surrender from the north side of the river. Wilder refused.
Chalmers acts on his ownAbout 2,500 Mississippians marched from Cave City at 10 p.m.Chalmers did not wait for Bragg’s main army. He planned a converging attack before dawn on September 14.
Assault plan10th and 44th Mississippi west; 7th, 9th, and 29th Mississippi eastRichards’s sharpshooters opened against the pickets, four guns initially occupied Lewis’s Hill, and the 44th Mississippi began as the brigade reserve.
Plan breaks downThe attack began before every Confederate unit was in positionUnited States pickets withdrew into the works, the first Confederate gun position lacked range, and burning buildings were mistaken for a signal to attack.
Western attackThe 10th Mississippi was pinned before the worksThe regiment advanced across exposed and obstructed ground; the 44th Mississippi went forward in support. Both lost senior officers under concentrated infantry and artillery fire.
Eastern attackThe 7th, 9th, and 29th Mississippi failed before Fort CraigUnited States fire checked the charge, and artillery sent by Scott mistakenly fired into Chalmers’s troops, increasing the confusion.
September 14 losses285 Confederate casualties; 37 United States casualtiesThese are the National Park Service battle-detail totals for the failed assault. Wilder’s contemporary report also placed his killed and wounded at 37.
Dunham arrivesSix companies of the 50th Indiana reached the works during the battleTheir train stopped about six miles away. Roughly 400 men marched by a circuitous route, avoided Scott’s cavalry, and entered the defenses; Cyrus L. Dunham then took command as senior officer.
A stronger garrisonAbout 4,000 defenders by September 16Reinforcements arrived while Dunham and Wilder strengthened the works during September 15 and 16.
Bragg concentratesMore than 20,000 Confederates and dozens of gunsBragg’s September 16 message claimed a force exceeding 20,000. Wilder later reported counting forty-five cannon and being surrounded by more than 25,000 men.
EncirclementPolk north of the Green River; Hardee south of itPolk’s right wing occupied commanding river hills on the north bank while Hardee’s left wing took the heights south of the fortifications.
A command crisisGilbert relieved Dunham and restored WilderAfter Dunham telegraphed Louisville about the garrison’s doubtful prospects, acting Maj. Gen. Charles C. Gilbert ordered Wilder back into command and told Dunham to place himself under arrest.
Council of warThe officers would resist unless Bragg proved overwhelming strengthWith limited small-arms ammunition, exhausted men, and no relief force near enough to help, Wilder asked for evidence that further defense would be a useless loss of life.
Buckner’s roleA former surrendering commander guided Wilder through the Confederate linesSimon B. Buckner, a Munfordville native who had surrendered Fort Donelson in February, showed the blindfolded Wilder enough of Bragg’s position to establish the disparity in force.
SurrenderAgreed about 2 a.m.; formal march-out at 6 a.m., September 17Approximately 4,000 United States troops stacked arms. Bragg reported taking an equal number of small arms, ten artillery pieces, and large quantities of munitions; the prisoners were paroled.
Battlefield total4,433 estimated casualties in the NPS tableThe National Park Service counts 4,148 United States losses—overwhelmingly surrendered—and 285 Confederate losses. Other summaries combine or estimate categories differently.
The apparent opportunityBragg stood astride Buell’s road to LouisvilleThe Green River offered strong defensive ground, and Bragg initially expected Buell to attack. Buell’s army was then moving north from Bowling Green.
The logistical limitThe army could remain only a few days without starvingDrought had exhausted men and animals, and the long-standing garrison had already stripped the nearby country of food and forage. Bragg could neither sustain the position nor spare enough strength to attack Buell safely.
Bragg abandons MunfordvilleSeptember 20, 1862The army moved northeast toward Bardstown in search of supplies and a junction with Kirby Smith. The Confederates destroyed the railroad bridge as they left.
Buell reaches LouisvilleSeptember 25, 1862Once Bragg left the Green River line, the Army of the Ohio had a clear route to Louisville, where it could refit and absorb reinforcements.
