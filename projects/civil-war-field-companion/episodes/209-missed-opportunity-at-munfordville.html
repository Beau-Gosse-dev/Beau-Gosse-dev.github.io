Episode 209 · August 28–September 25, 1862; fighting and siege at Munfordville, September 14–17 · The Confederate line of advance from Chattanooga through Sparta, Glasgow, and Cave City to Munfordville and Woodsonville at the Green River crossing of the Louisville & Nashville Railroad, Kentucky

MISSED OPPORTUNITY AT MUNFORDVILLE

Confederate tactical victory but an unrealized strategic opportunity. James R. Chalmers’s unsupported September 14 assault failed with heavy losses; Braxton Bragg then surrounded the reinforced United States garrison and accepted John T. Wilder’s surrender on September 17. Short of food and forage, Bragg abandoned the commanding Green River position on September 20 and opened Buell’s road to Louisville
Episode scopeBragg’s advance, the capture of Munfordville, and the decision to leaveThe episode follows the Army of the Mississippi from Chattanooga to the Green River, describes the September 14 assault and September 16–17 surrender, and closes with Bragg’s withdrawal toward Bardstown and Buell’s arrival at Louisville.
CampaignConfederate Heartland OffensiveBragg moved north from Tennessee while Edmund Kirby Smith operated independently in eastern and central Kentucky.
Strategic objectiveThe Green River bridge and Buell’s supply lineThe Louisville & Nashville Railroad and turnpike crossed the Green River at Munfordville. Holding the crossing placed Bragg between the Army of the Ohio and its principal supply base at Louisville.
Bragg’s armyAbout 30,000 men when reorganized at ChattanoogaThe episode describes two wings under Leonidas Polk and William J. Hardee, each with two infantry divisions, plus roughly 6,000 cavalry divided between John C. Wharton and Joseph Wheeler.
Command burdenBragg acted as both army commander and chief of staffHis chief of staff left because of illness before the campaign. Bragg also divided his cavalry between wing commanders, leaving no mounted reserve directly under army headquarters.
Buell’s problemA slow advance on a vulnerable railroad supply lineConfederate cavalry raids, railroad repair, hunger, and Buell’s restrictions on foraging delayed the Army of the Ohio across northern Mississippi, Alabama, and Tennessee.
Bragg leaves ChattanoogaAugust 28, 1862He moved two days before Kirby Smith’s victory at Richmond, Kentucky, crossed the Cumberland Plateau, and reached Sparta on September 6.
Glasgow concentrationSeptember 13, 1862Bragg’s army reached the Glasgow area, about twelve miles south of Cave City and roughly thirty-five miles east of Bowling Green.
Fort CraigRedoubts, rifle pits, trenches, and a south-bank stockadeUnited States troops had fortified the bridge approaches. The works south of the Green River protected both the railroad span and nearby Munfordville.
Initial garrison2,122 men for duty in Wilder’s official reportThe command included the 67th and 89th Indiana, detachments of the 17th and 74th Indiana, one company of the 18th U.S. Infantry, Kentucky troops, cavalry, and four artillery pieces.
Faulty reconnaissanceJohn S. Scott reported 1,200–1,800 raw troops in weak worksScott also claimed that reinforcements could not arrive. The railroad and telegraph still connected the garrison with the north, and the fortifications were stronger than his description suggested.
First surrender demandSeptember 13, about 8 p.m.Scott demanded unconditional surrender from the north side of the river. Wilder refused.
Chalmers acts on his ownAbout 2,500 Mississippians marched from Cave City at 10 p.m.Chalmers did not wait for Bragg’s main army. He planned a converging attack before dawn on September 14.
Assault plan10th and 44th Mississippi west; 7th, 9th, and 29th Mississippi eastRichards’s sharpshooters opened against the pickets, four guns initially occupied Lewis’s Hill, and the 44th Mississippi began as the brigade reserve.
Plan breaks downThe attack began before every Confederate unit was in positionUnited States pickets withdrew into the works, the first Confederate gun position lacked range, and burning buildings were mistaken for a signal to attack.
Western attackThe 10th Mississippi was pinned before the worksThe regiment advanced across exposed and obstructed ground; the 44th Mississippi went forward in support. Both lost senior officers under concentrated infantry and artillery fire.
Eastern attackThe 7th, 9th, and 29th Mississippi failed before Fort CraigUnited States fire checked the charge, and artillery sent by Scott mistakenly fired into Chalmers’s troops, increasing the confusion.
September 14 losses285 Confederate casualties; 37 United States casualtiesThese are the National Park Service battle-detail totals for the failed assault. Wilder’s contemporary report also placed his killed and wounded at 37.
Dunham arrivesSix companies of the 50th Indiana reached the works during the battleTheir train stopped about six miles away. Roughly 400 men marched by a circuitous route, avoided Scott’s cavalry, and entered the defenses; Cyrus L. Dunham then took command as senior officer.
A stronger garrisonAbout 4,000 defenders by September 16Reinforcements arrived while Dunham and Wilder strengthened the works during September 15 and 16.
Bragg concentratesMore than 20,000 Confederates and dozens of gunsBragg’s September 16 message claimed a force exceeding 20,000. Wilder later reported counting forty-five cannon and being surrounded by more than 25,000 men.
EncirclementPolk north of the Green River; Hardee south of itPolk’s right wing occupied commanding river hills on the north bank while Hardee’s left wing took the heights south of the fortifications.
A command crisisGilbert relieved Dunham and restored WilderAfter Dunham telegraphed Louisville about the garrison’s doubtful prospects, acting Maj. Gen. Charles C. Gilbert ordered Wilder back into command and told Dunham to place himself under arrest.
Council of warThe officers would resist unless Bragg proved overwhelming strengthWith limited small-arms ammunition, exhausted men, and no relief force near enough to help, Wilder asked for evidence that further defense would be a useless loss of life.
Buckner’s roleA former surrendering commander guided Wilder through the Confederate linesSimon B. Buckner, a Munfordville native who had surrendered Fort Donelson in February, showed the blindfolded Wilder enough of Bragg’s position to establish the disparity in force.
SurrenderAgreed about 2 a.m.; formal march-out at 6 a.m., September 17Approximately 4,000 United States troops stacked arms. Bragg reported taking an equal number of small arms, ten artillery pieces, and large quantities of munitions; the prisoners were paroled.
Battlefield total4,433 estimated casualties in the NPS tableThe National Park Service counts 4,148 United States losses—overwhelmingly surrendered—and 285 Confederate losses. Other summaries combine or estimate categories differently.
The apparent opportunityBragg stood astride Buell’s road to LouisvilleThe Green River offered strong defensive ground, and Bragg initially expected Buell to attack. Buell’s army was then moving north from Bowling Green.
The logistical limitThe army could remain only a few days without starvingDrought had exhausted men and animals, and the long-standing garrison had already stripped the nearby country of food and forage. Bragg could neither sustain the position nor spare enough strength to attack Buell safely.
Bragg abandons MunfordvilleSeptember 20, 1862The army moved northeast toward Bardstown in search of supplies and a junction with Kirby Smith. The Confederates destroyed the railroad bridge as they left.
Buell reaches LouisvilleSeptember 25, 1862Once Bragg left the Green River line, the Army of the Ohio had a clear route to Louisville, where it could refit and absorb reinforcements.

What happened

The campaign resumes after Richmond

Kirby Smith’s victory opened Lexington, which his Army of Kentucky entered on September 2 before sending detachments toward Louisville and Cincinnati.

Smith occupies the Bluegrass

The episode describes the welcome at Lexington and the September 4 capture of Frankfort, the only loyal-state capital occupied by Confederate troops during the war.

Bragg prepares to follow

Troop movements from Mississippi delayed Bragg at Chattanooga until late August, leaving Smith to begin the Kentucky advance first.

Braxton Bragg introduced

The episode traces the army commander’s West Point education, Regular Army career, Mexican War service, plantation years, and rapid Confederate promotion.

The Chattanooga plan comes apart

Bragg and Kirby Smith had agreed to cooperate in Middle Tennessee, but Smith redirected his column toward Kentucky and Bragg chose to follow the new strategy.

Bragg reorganizes the army

The Army of the Mississippi formed a right wing under Leonidas Polk and a left wing under William J. Hardee, each containing two divisions.

Cavalry and staff arrangements weaken headquarters

Bragg divided the cavalry between wing commanders and assumed the duties of his ailing chief of staff in addition to army command.

Buell’s difficult summer

The Army of the Ohio struggled toward Chattanooga while Confederate cavalry raids, damaged railroads, and shortages slowed its advance.

Railroad repair governs the advance

Henry Halleck had ordered Buell to rebuild the Memphis & Charleston Railroad as his army moved east from Corinth.

Buell begins to concentrate

Reports of Kirby Smith’s movement and Bragg’s intentions led Buell to draw his scattered army toward Decherd, Tennessee, on August 19.

Bragg leaves Chattanooga

The Army of the Mississippi began its march on August 28 and crossed the Cumberland Plateau toward Sparta.

The Confederate column enters Kentucky

Bragg moved through Tompkinsville and reached the Glasgow area by September 13.

Buell’s supply line is exposed

The Louisville & Nashville Railroad tied Buell’s forward army to its principal depot at Louisville.

Munfordville controls the Green River crossing

The turnpike and railroad crossed at this fortified point twelve miles north of Cave City.

Wilder commands Fort Craig

About 2,000 United States infantry and several guns defended the bridge approaches behind redoubts and connecting trenches.

Scott demands surrender

On the evening of September 13, Confederate cavalry commander John S. Scott called on the garrison to capitulate; Wilder refused.

Chalmers chooses an immediate attack

Acting on Scott’s inaccurate description of the defenses and garrison, Chalmers decided to strike without waiting for Bragg’s main army.

The Mississippi brigade marches overnight

About 2,500 men left Cave City at 10 p.m. and deployed before Fort Craig ahead of dawn on September 14.

The first shots

Richards’s sharpshooters engaged Wilder’s pickets as the Confederate assault began around 5 a.m.

Confederate guns lack range

Artillery on Lewis’s Hill had to move closer, where United States cannon could answer effectively.

A mistaken signal starts the western attack

Col. Robert A. Smith interpreted burning buildings near the defenses as the signal and sent the 10th Mississippi forward.

The 10th Mississippi is pinned down

Concentrated fire stopped the regiment before the works. Smith was mortally wounded, and Lt. Col. James G. Bullard was killed.

The 44th Mississippi joins the costly attack

Blythe’s regiment advanced in support. Its acting commander, Lt. Col. James Moore, was mortally wounded.

Chalmers sends in the eastern regiments

The 9th and 29th Mississippi advanced, and the 7th moved forward to support them against Fort Craig.

Friendly artillery fire breaks the assault

Fire from Scott’s supporting guns landed among Chalmers’s own infantry and helped destroy the coordination of the attack.

The failed attack ends

By about 9:30 a.m., after more than four hours of fighting, Chalmers withdrew from the immediate defenses.

The casualty disparity is clear

The episode gives Chalmers 285 killed, wounded, and missing against 37 casualties in Wilder’s command.

Wilder refuses again

When Chalmers renewed his surrender demand, Wilder answered that he would defend the position until overpowered.

Dunham reaches the fort

Cyrus L. Dunham arrived with about 400 men of the 50th Indiana and assumed command as the senior officer.

The garrison doubles

Reinforcements raised the defending force to about 4,000 by the morning of September 16.

Buckner supplies local knowledge

The Munfordville native helped Bragg plan the investment of the fortified river crossing.

Bragg brings up the army

The Army of the Mississippi moved from Glasgow late on September 15 and reached the Munfordville defenses the next day.

Polk and Hardee close the ring

Hardee’s troops occupied the south-bank approaches while Polk crossed upstream and placed guns on the north-bank heights.

The disparity becomes overwhelming

By late afternoon, roughly 25,000 Confederates and a ring of artillery surrounded the 4,000-man garrison.

Bragg demands surrender

Dunham initially refused, saying that he would defend the works to the utmost despite the consequences of an assault.

A truce pauses the crisis

The opposing commanders agreed to stop firing until 9 p.m. while the garrison reconsidered its position.

Dunham telegraphs Louisville

The line remained open long enough for Dunham to report his doubts to acting Maj. Gen. Charles C. Gilbert.

Wilder returns to command

Gilbert relieved Dunham, restored Wilder, and ordered Dunham under arrest; Dunham offered to fight in the trenches as a private soldier.

The officers set a condition for resistance

They agreed to continue only unless Bragg could demonstrate that his superiority made defense a useless sacrifice.

Buckner takes charge of the negotiations

Having surrendered Fort Donelson himself, Buckner understood Wilder’s dilemma and offered to show him the Confederate position.

Wilder crosses the lines

Around 2 a.m., the blindfolded United States commander entered Buckner’s tent and asked what he ought to do.

Wilder decides to surrender

After seeing the Confederate positions and considering whether resistance would help Buell elsewhere, Wilder chose capitulation.

The garrison capitulates

In the early hours of September 17, Wilder surrendered approximately 4,000 troops without a renewed assault.

One of the war’s larger surrenders

At 6 a.m., the garrison marched out and stacked arms; the prisoners were paroled and sent toward United States lines.

Bragg wins his first victory in army command

He reported about 4,000 prisoners, an equal number of small arms, ten cannon, and large quantities of military stores.

A powerful tactical position

Bragg now stood between Buell and Louisville with a defensible river barrier across the Army of the Ohio’s route.

Buell closes from Bowling Green

The Army of the Ohio was already north of Nashville and concentrating around Bowling Green as Munfordville fell.

The armies probe toward each other

Buell approached cautiously because the Green River was a serious obstacle and his estimates exaggerated Bragg’s strength.

Logistics undo the position

The drought-stricken Confederate army found little food or forage around a post whose garrison had already consumed local supplies.

The missed opportunity debated

Later criticism focused on Bragg’s decision to give up the Green River line, but the episode argues that his supply condition left few practical choices.

Bragg marches for Bardstown

On September 20 the Confederates abandoned Munfordville, seeking food and a junction with Kirby Smith farther northeast.

Buell reaches Louisville

With the route open, the Army of the Ohio arrived at Kentucky’s largest city on September 25.

The episode’s reading recommendation

Gerald J. Prokopowicz’s All for the Regiment follows the Army of the Ohio through the campaigns that culminated at Perryville.
