STONEWALL JACKSON (Part the First)

This biographical episode follows Thomas J. Jackson from childhood through West Point, the Mexican War, peacetime U.S. Army service, and ten years at Virginia Military Institute. It ends when Jackson takes the VMI cadet corps from Lexington toward Richmond on April 21, 1861. No Civil War battle is narrated. The experiences emphasized in the episode—academic persistence, aggressive service under fire, rigid adherence to duty, conflict with superiors, religious discipline, and demanding physical standards—later shaped his Confederate command style
Episode scopeJackson’s life before active Civil War field commandThe opening discusses his later reputation and the 1862 Valley Campaign, but the chronological biography stops on April 21, 1861.
BirthJanuary 21, 1824, Clarksburg, VirginiaClarksburg became part of West Virginia in 1863. Jackson’s father died when he was two and his mother when he was seven; relatives, especially his uncle Cummins Jackson, raised him.
West PointCadet, July 1, 1842–July 1, 1846; graduated 17th of 59Jackson entered with weak formal preparation and improved his standing through sustained study.
U.S. Army branch1st U.S. ArtilleryHe graduated as a brevet second lieutenant and served in Winfield Scott’s campaign in Mexico.
Mexican War serviceVeracruz, Cerro Gordo, La Hoya, Ocalaca, Contreras, Churubusco, Molino del Rey, Chapultepec, and Mexico CityThis is the engagement list in Cullum’s register. The episode names the principal actions but omits several smaller or intermediate engagements.
Mexican War promotionsSecond lieutenant on August 20, 1847; brevet captain on August 20; brevet major on September 13Cullum’s register dates the captain’s brevet to gallantry at Contreras and Churubusco and the major’s brevet to Chapultepec. The episode incorrectly connects promotion to first lieutenant with the siege of Veracruz.
Peacetime stationsFort Columbus, Fort Hamilton, and Fort MeadeThe official register places Jackson at Fort Columbus in 1848, Fort Hamilton in 1849–1851, and in Florida operations against Seminoles in 1851.
Fort Meade disputeA command conflict with Maj. William H. FrenchThe episode traces the dispute from control of a construction worker through competing accusations. Department commander David E. Twiggs transferred French; Jackson remained but soon left for VMI.
Army resignation chronologyVMI service began in 1851; resignation was formally recorded February 29, 1852The episode says Jackson resigned in August 1851 and then joined VMI. Cullum’s register records the later formal resignation date, so his VMI appointment and separation from the Army overlapped administratively.
VMI positionProfessor of Natural and Experimental Philosophy and instructor of artillery, 1851–1861Francis H. Smith was VMI superintendent. D. H. Hill, then teaching at nearby Washington College, helped secure Jackson’s appointment.
First marriageElinor “Ellie” Junkin, married August 4, 1853Ellie and the couple’s stillborn son died from complications of childbirth on October 22, 1854.
Second marriageMary Anna Morrison, married July 16, 1857The episode covers their courtship and structured household routine. Their first daughter, Mary Graham Jackson, died in infancy in 1858, a fact not mentioned in the episode.
Religion, education, and slaveryJackson helped operate a Sabbath school for free and enslaved Black people while enslaving people in his own householdVMI’s Jackson House Museum identifies seven individuals whom Jackson enslaved during his Lexington years: Albert, Amy, Hetty, Cyrus, George, Ann, and Emma. This context is necessary alongside the episode’s account of his religious teaching.
The lemon storyA disputed element of Jackson’s postwar imageThe episode treats habitual lemon-eating as a postwar legend associated with Henry Kyd Douglas. The evidence does not justify turning either the habit or its complete invention into a firm biographical fact.
Departure for RichmondApril 21, 1861The episode gives 1:00 p.m. as the departure time. A VMI publication says Jackson ordered the corps forward at 12:30 p.m.; the date and purpose are not in dispute, but the exact time is.
Combat and senior-leader lossesNone within the chronological episode narrativeMexican War actions are summarized retrospectively, but the episode does not identify a senior leader killed or mortally wounded in those passages.
Book recommendationMighty Stonewall by Frank E. VandiverThe hosts also refer listeners to James I. Robertson Jr.’s longer Stonewall Jackson: The Man, the Soldier, the Legend, recommended in episode 57.
