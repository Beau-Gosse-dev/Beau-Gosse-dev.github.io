What happened

The episode returns to the Chattahoochee mills

When Johnston abandoned the Shoup Line, U.S. troops gained an industrial district along the river. Cavalrymen removed workers and burned mills that produced cloth for Confederate uniforms.

Johnston formed a line south of Peachtree Creek

The Army of Tennessee occupied ground about three miles north of central Atlanta. Wheeler covered the front and William H. Jackson held the Confederate left.

Alexander P. Stewart took command of Polk’s former corps

Stewart assumed corps command on July 7. William W. Loring returned to his division, and Jefferson Davis selected Henry D. Clayton to replace Stewart as a division commander.

Sherman planned to isolate Atlanta by rail

Sherman intended to avoid a direct attack on the city defenses and make Atlanta unusable by cutting the railroads that connected it to the rest of the Confederacy.

Two cavalry raids targeted railroads west of Atlanta

Lovell Rousseau led a long-range raid from Tennessee into Alabama while George Stoneman crossed the Chattahoochee southwest of Atlanta.

Rousseau damaged the railroad in Alabama

His 2,500 cavalrymen destroyed supplies, bridges, rolling stock, and about 30 miles of track. The damage interrupted Confederate communications for weeks.

Stoneman withdrew after crossing the river

Stoneman captured a damaged bridge, crossed on July 14, then retreated when Confederate troops opposed him. Sherman ended that part of the operation.

Sherman began the movement around Atlanta

McPherson moved from Roswell toward the Georgia Railroad. Schofield advanced in the center and Thomas served as the western hinge of the movement.

U.S. armies advanced to Nancy Creek

On July 17, the three armies pushed back Wheeler’s cavalry and moved about halfway from the Chattahoochee to Peachtree Creek.

The episode shifts to the wider war

Jubal Early crossed the Potomac on July 5, defeated a U.S. force at Monocacy on July 9, and reached the outskirts of Washington two days later.

Grant remained outside Richmond and Petersburg

After the Overland Campaign and the first attacks on Petersburg, the Army of the Potomac was exhausted and unable to force an immediate breakthrough.

The 1864 election affected military strategy

Republican divisions, Democratic opposition to the war and emancipation, and rising war weariness made a clear U.S. military success politically important.

Atlanta became a major political objective

With Grant stopped outside Petersburg and Early threatening Washington, the outcome of Sherman’s campaign carried greater importance for both governments.
