STONEWALL IN THE VALLEY: FRONT ROYAL & WINCHESTER (Part the First)

Operational prelude; no battle is fought in this episode. Shields left the Shenandoah Valley to reinforce Irvin McDowell, reducing Nathaniel Banks to a divided command at Strasburg and Front Royal. After conflicting orders nearly pulled Richard Ewell east toward Richmond, Ewell and Jackson agreed on May 18 to concentrate against Banks. Both Confederate commands marched on May 19 and were united in the Luray Valley by May 22, setting up the Battle of Front Royal on May 23

What happened

The Valley Campaign resumes

The hosts return to the spring 1862 crisis, when McClellan’s army was approaching Richmond and Confederate leaders wanted Jackson to keep United States troops occupied in the Shenandoah Valley.

Jackson’s strategic purpose is restated

His operations were intended to prevent the Federal forces opposite him from reinforcing the advance on Richmond.

Kernstown’s mixed result

The March 23 battle was a tactical defeat, but the alarm it created altered Federal deployments and kept additional troops away from McClellan.

Ewell enters the Valley equation

Robert E. Lee arranged for Richard S. Ewell’s approximately 8,500-man division east of the Blue Ridge to support Jackson.

Ewell replaces Jackson near Swift Run Gap

Ewell moved into the Elk Run Valley position near Conrad’s Store to watch Banks while Jackson went to Staunton and joined Edward Johnson.

Jackson attacks the western threat first

Jackson combined with Johnson to prevent Banks and Frémont from joining. Their operation produced the Battle of McDowell on May 8.

McDowell becomes a strategic success

Although Jackson suffered the heavier tactical loss, the battle and pursuit pushed Frémont away from the Valley and removed the immediate western threat.

Jackson returns from the mountains

By May 17 the Valley force was back near the western edge of the Shenandoah. Six regiments from Edward Johnson’s force were absorbed into Jackson’s command.

Banks’s April confidence is recalled

Banks had told Washington on April 30 that the Valley mission was finished because he believed Jackson had left or was leaving for Richmond.

Washington redistributes Banks’s army

Lincoln and Stanton directed James Shields’s division east to reinforce Irvin McDowell for the planned movement from Fredericksburg toward Richmond.

Shields leaves the Valley

The episode dates the division’s departure to May 15. Banks retained Williams’s roughly 4,500-man division and about 1,500 cavalry.

Banks’s remaining infantry lacks battle experience

The men had served for months and had drilled, marched, and skirmished, but the episode describes the command as untested in a pitched battle.

Banks expects an attack by May 22

Now at Strasburg and aware that Jackson had returned from McDowell, Banks warned Washington that Jackson probably intended to strike him.

Williams’s reduced division is described

The division had two brigades and eight infantry regiments after its third brigade was sent east at the end of March.

Banks must defend two points

His May 22 message identified Strasburg and Front Royal as positions that were both exposed to Confederate attack.

Strasburg and Front Royal are linked

The villages lay about 12 miles apart and were connected by the Manassas Gap Railroad and a country road.

Massanutten divides the theater

The long mountain separated the main Shenandoah Valley on the west from the narrower Luray Valley on the east, with New Market Gap providing the practical military crossing.

Roads make both posts important

The Valley Turnpike ran through Strasburg, while another improved north–south route served Front Royal and the Luray Valley.

The Valley Turnpike forms Banks’s main corridor

The road connected Staunton, Harrisonburg, New Market, Strasburg, and Winchester and supported rapid movement through the main Valley.

Front Royal receives a small garrison

Banks placed about 900 infantry and two guns at Front Royal but no cavalry, while keeping most of his available force near Strasburg.

Banks asks Washington for help

He tried to create urgency with his May 22 dispatch and sent his adjutant to make the case for reinforcements in person.

Stanton discounts the danger

The Secretary of War rejected the urgency of Banks’s request and did not consider Jackson a serious immediate threat.

Banks misreads the Confederate locations

He expected Jackson to move north on the Valley Turnpike and believed Ewell remained nearly 60 miles from Front Royal at Swift Run Gap.

Jackson and Ewell are already much closer

By May 22 the two Confederate commands had united in the Luray Valley, inside the geography Banks thought separated them.

The narrative returns to Ewell

Ewell had occupied Jackson’s old camps near Swift Run Gap since the end of April and was frustrated by Jackson’s secrecy and repeated orders to remain in place.

Jackson leaves Ewell without the full plan

Ewell understood that he must watch Banks and discourage a Federal departure, but he did not know whom Jackson had gone to attack.

Ewell questions Jackson’s judgment

In an exchange with Colonel James A. Walker, Ewell vented his fear that an unexplained order to remain might expose the division to destruction.

Walker’s history with Jackson is recalled

The hosts note that Walker had been dismissed from Virginia Military Institute after a dispute with Professor Thomas J. Jackson and a challenge to duel.

Munford brings news of Shields

On the night of May 11, cavalry officer Thomas Munford told Ewell that Shields’s division was leaving the Valley to reinforce McDowell.

Ewell studies the map in frustration

Munford later described Ewell springing from bed and kneeling over a map as he tried to determine Jackson’s location and the opportunity presented by Shields’s movement.

Ewell records two miserable weeks

In a May 13 letter to a niece, Ewell described headaches, indigestion, and deep frustration with the confused direction he was receiving.

Twenty-seven messages deepen the confusion

By May 16, according to the episode, Ewell had received 27 messages from Jackson, Lee, and Johnston rather than a single coherent set of instructions.

Jackson orders Ewell to follow Banks

Jackson’s May 13 direction required Ewell to pursue if Banks withdrew down the Valley toward Strasburg and the railroad.

Johnston’s order points east

Ewell also understood Johnston to require his division to leave the Valley and reinforce Richmond if Banks moved east to join McDowell.

Ewell rides to Mount Solon

Unable to resolve the contradiction by message, Ewell rode about 30 miles through the night to Jackson’s headquarters.

The generals meet on May 18

At daybreak they conferred in a mill and agreed that the divided Federal forces created an opportunity to attack Banks.

Ewell says Johnston’s order still binds him

Despite sharing Jackson’s objective, Ewell initially concluded that he had to follow Shields east and leave the Valley.

Lee’s May 16 message supports an attack

Lee urged action that would prevent Banks from reinforcing either McDowell or McClellan and advised Jackson to move quickly if he attacked.

Jackson has appealed to Johnston

Jackson had written the previous evening for revised orders, but a reply might take three days because Johnston used couriers rather than the telegraph.

Ewell agrees to remain temporarily

He would stay and fight under Jackson until Johnston answered, provided Jackson accepted responsibility for the decision.

The command decision is put in writing

The episode says the generals constructed an exchange designed to document Ewell’s dilemma and protect him from later charges of disobedience.

Jackson’s May 18 order is read

Jackson stated that Ewell belonged to his command while in the Valley District and directed him to camp between New Market and Mount Jackson on May 21 unless later superior orders intervened.

Jackson outlines the Strasburg plan

The two commands would concentrate between New Market and Mount Jackson, then move north on the Valley Turnpike and attack Banks at Strasburg.

Both Confederate commands begin marching

Jackson put the Valley force on the road at 5 a.m. on May 19, while Ewell’s division left Swift Run Gap at daybreak.

The episode stops before the fighting

The concentration began the ten days of movement and combat that would carry Jackson toward the Potomac. No leader death or mortal wound occurs within this installment.
