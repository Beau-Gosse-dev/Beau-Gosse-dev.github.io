Episode 168 · July 1–August 3, 1862 · Malvern Hill and Harrison’s Landing on the James River, Virginia; Washington, D.C.; and the planned transfer route through Fort Monroe to Aquia Creek, Virginia

"IT IS DETERMINED TO WITHDRAW YOUR ARMY FROM THE PENINSULA"

The Army of the Potomac won the defensive battle at Malvern Hill but continued its withdrawal to Harrison’s Landing rather than counterattacking. McClellan then asked for major reinforcements to renew operations against Richmond. After consulting him, General-in-Chief Henry W. Halleck concluded that Washington could not supply the requested force and ordered the army transferred from the Peninsula to Aquia Creek to unite with John Pope’s Army of Virginia
Episode scopeThe aftermath of Malvern Hill, the Harrison’s Landing encampment, and the decision to end the Peninsula CampaignThe opening section reviews responsibility for the July 1 battle before the narrative follows McClellan, Lincoln, and Halleck through August 3.
Malvern Hill resultUnited States victory on July 1, 1862Confederate attacks were poorly coordinated and failed to break the Federal position. The National Park Service estimates about 3,000 United States and 5,355 Confederate casualties.
Confederate attack patternSeparate brigades entered the fight without a single coordinated assaultThe episode accepts D. H. Hill’s criticism that the attacks were piecemeal but rejects his claim that Magruder’s command did little fighting.
Magruder’s participationThree of four brigades made substantial attacksThe episode states that Magruder’s men reached closer to the Federal line near the Crew house than other Confederate formations.
Episode’s responsibility assessmentRobert E. Lee held final command responsibility for the failed attacksThis is the hosts’ interpretation. They emphasize Lee’s approval of the artillery plan, the ambiguous attack order, his failure to cancel it, and his later orders to continue the assault.
Withdrawal after Malvern HillAbout eight miles to Harrison’s Landing on July 2McClellan declined proposals for a counterattack and moved the army down the James River to an entrenched position protected by United States gunboats.
Army at Harrison’s LandingAbout 100,000 soldiers in the U.S. Army campaign summaryThe army occupied a strong bridgehead on the James. Disease, heat, logistics, and the separation from Pope’s army shaped the administration’s later decision.
McClellan’s enemy estimateNearly 180,000 Confederates in the episode’s accountThe episode presents this as a serious overestimate that reinforced McClellan’s belief that withdrawal was necessary.
Field command criticismThe episode faults McClellan for remaining away from key fightingThe hosts distinguish their explanation—protection of his reputation—from either personal cowardice or treason. That explanation is interpretation, not an established documentary fact.
Lincoln at Harrison’s LandingJuly 7, 1862Lincoln traveled to inspect the army and met McClellan. The Library of Congress dates McClellan’s Harrison’s Landing letter to the president to the same day.
Harrison’s Landing letterMilitary strategy and a conciliatory war policyMcClellan argued that the war should preserve the Union without becoming a campaign of subjugation, confiscation, or forcible abolition. The episode describes the letter as unsolicited advice extending beyond field command.
Second Confiscation ActApproved July 17, 1862The legislation expanded Federal action against rebel property and addressed the freedom of enslaved people held by participants in the rebellion.
First emancipation draft presentedJuly 22, 1862Lincoln read his preliminary draft to the cabinet as a military measure. He delayed public issuance until after a favorable military event; the preliminary proclamation followed Antietam in September.
New general-in-chiefHenry W. Halleck, appointed July 11, 1862The post had been vacant since Lincoln removed McClellan from the general-in-chief role in March. McClellan remained commander of the Army of the Potomac.
Halleck’s consultationJuly 24–27, 1862Halleck’s official report says that he left Washington on July 24, consulted McClellan at Harrison’s Landing, and returned on July 27. The episode describes him as arriving on July 24.
Reinforcement sequence50,000 requested; 20,000 offered; 35,000 later stated as necessaryHalleck reported that McClellan initially wanted at least 50,000 additional troops, agreed the next morning to attempt an advance with 20,000, and then raised the requirement to 35,000 after Halleck returned to Washington.
Withdrawal orderAugust 3, 1862, at 7:45 p.m.Halleck ordered McClellan to begin immediate measures to transfer the army from the Peninsula to Aquia Creek while covering the movement.
Strategic purposeUnite McClellan’s army with Pope’s and cover WashingtonHalleck argued that the administration could not reinforce both separated armies and that Aquia Creek offered the feasible route to concentrate them on the Rappahannock line.
Recommended book“Abraham Lincoln and the Second American Revolution” by James M. McPhersonThe podcast’s official book list identifies this collection of essays as the recommendation for episode 168.
Transcript normalizationsBenjamin Huger, Philip Kearny, Lafayette McLaws, William H. C. Whiting, George Brinton McClellan, and Gideon WellesThe automated transcript produces “U.J.,” “Kearney,” “McClaws,” “Whiting” without initials, “George Britton McClellan,” and “Wells.” “U.J.” refers to Huger.
