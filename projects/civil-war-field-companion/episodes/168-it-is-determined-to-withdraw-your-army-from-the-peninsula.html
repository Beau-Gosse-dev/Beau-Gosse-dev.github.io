Episode 168 · July 1–August 3, 1862 · Malvern Hill and Harrison’s Landing on the James River, Virginia; Washington, D.C.; and the planned transfer route through Fort Monroe to Aquia Creek, Virginia

"IT IS DETERMINED TO WITHDRAW YOUR ARMY FROM THE PENINSULA"

The Army of the Potomac won the defensive battle at Malvern Hill but continued its withdrawal to Harrison’s Landing rather than counterattacking. McClellan then asked for major reinforcements to renew operations against Richmond. After consulting him, General-in-Chief Henry W. Halleck concluded that Washington could not supply the requested force and ordered the army transferred from the Peninsula to Aquia Creek to unite with John Pope’s Army of Virginia
Episode scopeThe aftermath of Malvern Hill, the Harrison’s Landing encampment, and the decision to end the Peninsula CampaignThe opening section reviews responsibility for the July 1 battle before the narrative follows McClellan, Lincoln, and Halleck through August 3.
Malvern Hill resultUnited States victory on July 1, 1862Confederate attacks were poorly coordinated and failed to break the Federal position. The National Park Service estimates about 3,000 United States and 5,355 Confederate casualties.
Confederate attack patternSeparate brigades entered the fight without a single coordinated assaultThe episode accepts D. H. Hill’s criticism that the attacks were piecemeal but rejects his claim that Magruder’s command did little fighting.
Magruder’s participationThree of four brigades made substantial attacksThe episode states that Magruder’s men reached closer to the Federal line near the Crew house than other Confederate formations.
Episode’s responsibility assessmentRobert E. Lee held final command responsibility for the failed attacksThis is the hosts’ interpretation. They emphasize Lee’s approval of the artillery plan, the ambiguous attack order, his failure to cancel it, and his later orders to continue the assault.
Withdrawal after Malvern HillAbout eight miles to Harrison’s Landing on July 2McClellan declined proposals for a counterattack and moved the army down the James River to an entrenched position protected by United States gunboats.
Army at Harrison’s LandingAbout 100,000 soldiers in the U.S. Army campaign summaryThe army occupied a strong bridgehead on the James. Disease, heat, logistics, and the separation from Pope’s army shaped the administration’s later decision.
McClellan’s enemy estimateNearly 180,000 Confederates in the episode’s accountThe episode presents this as a serious overestimate that reinforced McClellan’s belief that withdrawal was necessary.
Field command criticismThe episode faults McClellan for remaining away from key fightingThe hosts distinguish their explanation—protection of his reputation—from either personal cowardice or treason. That explanation is interpretation, not an established documentary fact.
Lincoln at Harrison’s LandingJuly 7, 1862Lincoln traveled to inspect the army and met McClellan. The Library of Congress dates McClellan’s Harrison’s Landing letter to the president to the same day.
Harrison’s Landing letterMilitary strategy and a conciliatory war policyMcClellan argued that the war should preserve the Union without becoming a campaign of subjugation, confiscation, or forcible abolition. The episode describes the letter as unsolicited advice extending beyond field command.
Second Confiscation ActApproved July 17, 1862The legislation expanded Federal action against rebel property and addressed the freedom of enslaved people held by participants in the rebellion.
First emancipation draft presentedJuly 22, 1862Lincoln read his preliminary draft to the cabinet as a military measure. He delayed public issuance until after a favorable military event; the preliminary proclamation followed Antietam in September.
New general-in-chiefHenry W. Halleck, appointed July 11, 1862The post had been vacant since Lincoln removed McClellan from the general-in-chief role in March. McClellan remained commander of the Army of the Potomac.
Halleck’s consultationJuly 24–27, 1862Halleck’s official report says that he left Washington on July 24, consulted McClellan at Harrison’s Landing, and returned on July 27. The episode describes him as arriving on July 24.
Reinforcement sequence50,000 requested; 20,000 offered; 35,000 later stated as necessaryHalleck reported that McClellan initially wanted at least 50,000 additional troops, agreed the next morning to attempt an advance with 20,000, and then raised the requirement to 35,000 after Halleck returned to Washington.
Withdrawal orderAugust 3, 1862, at 7:45 p.m.Halleck ordered McClellan to begin immediate measures to transfer the army from the Peninsula to Aquia Creek while covering the movement.
Strategic purposeUnite McClellan’s army with Pope’s and cover WashingtonHalleck argued that the administration could not reinforce both separated armies and that Aquia Creek offered the feasible route to concentrate them on the Rappahannock line.

What happened

The episode returns to Malvern Hill

The narrative begins by reviewing the final battle of the Seven Days and the Confederate defeat on July 1.

D. H. Hill identifies piecemeal attacks

The episode accepts Hill’s criticism that Confederate units entered separately but rejects his claim that Magruder’s command did little fighting.

Jackson’s role is reassessed

D. H. Hill’s division attacked under Jackson’s authority, and additional divisions were moving forward when darkness ended the battle.

Huger’s brigades enter the account

The transcript’s “U.J.” is Benjamin Huger. The episode states that three of his four brigades eventually joined the fighting despite friction with Magruder.

Longstreet and the artillery plan

The episode notes criticism of Longstreet for encouraging the belief that two large Confederate artillery groupings could suppress the Federal guns.

Magruder’s conduct is reviewed

The hosts separate Magruder’s delayed arrival from his later attacks, which they describe as compliance with Lee’s repeated orders.

Two attack orders reach Magruder

One order came through Lee’s chief of staff, Robert H. Chilton, and another came from Lee through Captain A. G. Dickinson.

The hosts assign final responsibility to Lee

Their interpretation places command responsibility for Malvern Hill’s Confederate defeat on the army commander.

The attack signal was unclear

Chilton’s untimed order made the advance of Lewis Armistead’s brigade the signal for a general assault, but the episode states that Armistead was not informed of that role.

Lee leaves the main front

After deciding that the artillery effort was failing, Lee moved away without first canceling the earlier assault instructions.

Reports prompt another attack order

Messages from Whiting and Magruder indicating Federal withdrawal and Armistead’s advance led Lee to order Magruder forward without personally confirming conditions at the front.

Lee continues the attacks

The episode states that Lee sent Lafayette McLaws’s division forward and redirected Magruder rather than stopping the assault.

Lee’s physical condition is discussed

The hosts describe him as fatigued and ill and note that Longstreet accompanied him in case he needed help directing the battle.

Confederates expect a counterattack

After the costly repulse, some Confederate officers expected McClellan to use Malvern Hill as the base for an attack on July 2.

Porter urges offensive action

Fitz John Porter and other Federal officers pressed McClellan to counterattack from the strong position.

McClellan orders the withdrawal to continue

The Army of the Potomac moved approximately eight miles down the James River to Harrison’s Landing.

McClellan estimates 180,000 Confederates

The episode presents the estimate as a large exaggeration that helped convince him continued retreat was necessary.

McClellan’s absence from battle is criticized

The narrative turns from the retreat decision to his repeated physical distance from the principal fighting during the Seven Days.

Corps commanders manage the retreat

The episode argues that McClellan left major battlefield decisions and the protection of the withdrawal to subordinate commanders.

Kearny condemns McClellan

Philip Kearny’s reported anger after the withdrawal is used to show the depth of criticism among some senior Federal officers.

The hosts offer a reputation-based explanation

They reject cowardice and treason and argue that McClellan distanced himself to protect his self-image and public reputation. This is their interpretation.

Army loyalty survives the retreat

The episode distinguishes dissatisfied officers from many soldiers who retained confidence in McClellan and blamed Washington for inadequate support.

Lincoln travels to Harrison’s Landing

On July 7, the president left Washington to inspect the Army of the Potomac and meet its commander.

McClellan gives Lincoln a policy letter

The Harrison’s Landing letter addressed national war policy as well as military operations.

McClellan advocates a limited war

He argued for conciliation and against confiscation and forcible abolition as instruments of the war.

Lincoln reads the letter without comment

The episode describes the president reading it in McClellan’s presence and pocketing it without giving the favorable response McClellan wanted.

Federal policy is moving beyond conciliation

Congress was completing the Second Confiscation Act while Lincoln considered emancipation as a military measure.

Lincoln consults Seward and Welles

The episode refers to Lincoln’s mid-July discussion with the secretaries of state and the navy about emancipation as a military necessity.

The cabinet hears the emancipation draft

Lincoln presented his preliminary draft on July 22 and stated that the basic decision was his, while inviting cabinet advice on implementation and timing.

Halleck becomes general-in-chief

Lincoln called Halleck from the Western Theater on July 11 to fill the post removed from McClellan in March.

McClellan resents Halleck’s appointment

The episode quotes McClellan’s private description of the promotion as a personal rebuke and shows his contempt for civilian and military superiors.

Halleck goes to Harrison’s Landing

He inspected the army and asked whether a practical renewed offensive against Richmond could begin from the James River base.

McClellan asks for 50,000 reinforcements

Halleck’s official report and the episode agree that this was McClellan’s initial minimum for renewed operations.

A movement against Petersburg is proposed

McClellan first suggested crossing the James and operating against Petersburg, south of Richmond.

The proposal shifts back toward Richmond

McClellan then discussed advancing on the north bank of the James and stated that he needed 30,000 reinforcements.

Halleck limits the offer to 20,000

Halleck said that Washington could not provide 50,000 and that he was authorized to offer no more than 20,000.

McClellan accepts the smaller reinforcement

After considering the choice overnight, he told Halleck that he would attempt an advance with 20,000 additional soldiers.

The stated requirement rises to 35,000

After Halleck returned to Washington, McClellan telegraphed that 35,000 reinforcements would be necessary.

Halleck orders the army off the Peninsula

The August 3 telegram directed McClellan to begin the transfer to Aquia Creek immediately and cover the movement.

The episode recommends James M. McPherson

The selection is “Abraham Lincoln and the Second American Revolution,” a collection of essays on Lincoln and the transformation produced by the war.
