CLASH OF THE IRONCLADS (Part the Fourth)

Monitor and Virginia fought for about four hours on March 9, but neither penetrated the other’s armor or disabled the opposing ship. The immediate engagement was a tactical draw. Monitor protected the grounded Minnesota and the United States blockade remained in place, giving the United States the strategic advantage. Virginia withdrew around 12:30 p.m.; Monitor did not pursue because its orders centered on Minnesota

What happened

Virginia prepared to return

Before dawn on March 9, its crew inspected damage from the previous day. The smokestack, fittings, two gun muzzles, and the bow were damaged, but 98 hits on the armor had not penetrated.

Washington learned of the March 8 defeat

Delayed reports reached Gideon Welles and the White House on Sunday morning. Lincoln called an immediate cabinet meeting.

The cabinet expected further attacks

Edwin Stanton feared Virginia might attack Washington or northern ports. Welles explained that its deep draft kept it from ascending the Potomac and that Monitor was already headed to Hampton Roads.

Stanton prepared to obstruct the Potomac

The War Department planned to sink stone-filled barges below Washington. Lincoln delayed the action because it would also block United States shipping.

Monitor waited beside Minnesota

The crew had slept little during the dangerous trip from New York, but Worden prepared the ship for battle at first light.

Jones waited for tide and visibility

Fog covered Hampton Roads. Catesby ap Roger Jones delayed Virginia’s movement until the tide rose and the fog cleared.

Virginia moved toward Minnesota

Around 8 a.m., Virginia left Sewell’s Point with Patrick Henry, Jamestown, and Teaser and steamed toward the grounded frigate.

The Confederates saw Monitor

Virginia’s crew had not expected the unfamiliar low-profile ship beside Minnesota. They identified it as the new United States ironclad.

Worden promised to defend Minnesota

Minnesota’s captain, Gershom Van Brunt, doubted Monitor could help. Worden said he would stay with the frigate as long as possible.

Virginia fired toward Minnesota

The Confederate ironclad opened from long range. A shell passed over Monitor and struck Minnesota.

Monitor intercepted Virginia

Just before 8:30 a.m., Worden got underway and moved away from Minnesota so the fight would occur farther from the grounded ship.

Greene commanded the turret

Samuel D. Greene supervised 16 gun crewmen loading and firing the two Dahlgrens. The speaking tube to Worden’s pilot house did not work, so messengers carried orders.

Monitor fired at close range

Worden turned to clear the pilot house from the guns’ line of fire. The ships passed within about 100 yards and then moved on roughly opposite courses.

Virginia answered with a broadside

Shots struck Monitor’s turret and deck but did not penetrate. The turret continued operating.

Monitor’s maneuverability mattered

Its shallow draft and tighter turning radius let Worden choose positions outside the firing arcs of Virginia’s fixed broadside guns.

Monitor used reduced powder charges

The Navy limited the 11-inch Dahlgrens to two-thirds charges because John Dahlgren feared repeated full charges might burst them. Later tests showed the guns could safely use more powder.

The ships fought in tightening circles

They closed to point-blank range. Projectiles struck and dented armor but generally glanced away without penetrating.

Failed communications slowed Monitor

Worden could not see the turret’s target and Greene could not see where the pilot house was steering. Paymaster William Keeler and clerk Daniel Toffey carried messages between them.

Both commanders changed tactics

After about two hours, Jones and Worden recognized that gunfire alone was unlikely to destroy the opposing ironclad.

Monitor tried to strike Virginia’s stern

Worden attempted to damage the rudder or propeller but missed the stern by about two feet.

Virginia grounded near Minnesota

Jones steered toward the frigate and ran onto a shoal. Monitor moved into a blind spot and concentrated fire against one section of the casemate.

Virginia forced itself off the shoal

H. Ashton Ramsay’s engineers raised boiler pressure beyond normal limits and used full reverse power until the ship pulled free.

Virginia tried to ram Monitor

The slow ship needed much of an hour to line up the attack. Monitor turned at the last moment, and Virginia delivered only a glancing blow that dented Monitor’s hull.

Jones considered boarding

Reuben Thom organized marines to cover the pilot-house slits and jam the turret, but Monitor moved away before they could attempt it.

Virginia again threatened Minnesota

Jones fired at the grounded frigate. Monitor closed to protect it and made another unsuccessful attempt to reach Virginia’s stern.

A shell struck Monitor’s pilot house

The blast drove fragments and powder into Worden’s face as he looked through a vision slit. He was temporarily blinded but survived and later recovered his sight.

Greene assumed command

Greene left the turret, reached the damaged pilot house, and took control while Monitor moved into water too shallow for Virginia.

Virginia withdrew

Jones first turned toward Minnesota, but pilots warned that the falling tide threatened to trap the deep-draft ship. Virginia left around 12:30 p.m.

The engagement ended in a tactical draw

The episode states that Monitor was hit 23 times and Virginia 20 times. Neither was seriously damaged. Worden was the only casualty aboard either ironclad that day.

Monitor accomplished its defensive mission

Virginia did not destroy Minnesota or break the blockade. United States warships retained control of Hampton Roads.

The Navy ordered more monitor-type ships

Twenty-one seagoing and seven river monitors entered service during the war. Confederate shipbuilders continued to favor casemate ironclads based on Virginia.

Virginia affected the Peninsula Campaign

Its presence restricted United States naval access to the James River and complicated McClellan’s operations even after the March battle.

Virginia tried to draw Monitor into another fight

Josiah Tattnall brought Virginia out on April 11, but Monitor remained near Fort Monroe under orders not to take unnecessary risks.

Virginia was scuttled after Norfolk fell

Confederates evacuated Norfolk in May. Virginia remained too deep to go up the James even after being lightened, so its crew destroyed it near Craney Island on May 11.

United States ships were stopped at Drewry’s Bluff

Monitor and Galena led a James River expedition on May 15. Confederate batteries eight miles below Richmond forced the squadron to withdraw.

Monitor sank off Cape Hatteras

While under tow toward Wilmington, it foundered in a storm shortly after midnight on December 31. Four officers and 12 enlisted men died.

The wreck was found in 1973

A Duke University team located Monitor with sonar. The site became the first United States national marine sanctuary in 1975.

The turret was recovered in 2002

A conservation team raised the 120-ton turret on August 5. It was transferred to the Mariners’ Museum in Newport News for treatment.
