What happened

The Red River campaign disrupted Grant’s plan

Nathaniel Banks retained veteran XVI and XVII Corps troops after his defeat at Mansfield and the difficult retreat down the Red River.

Sherman learned the borrowed troops would not return

On April 22, Grant confirmed that the Army of the Tennessee would begin the Georgia campaign without the divisions in Louisiana.

McPherson’s expected strength fell to seven divisions

Sherman estimated about 35,000 men and considered the force too small for an independent movement far from the main armies.

Sherman canceled the direct march on Rome

On April 23, he ordered McPherson toward Lebanon and Summerville, leaving open either a later move on Rome or a turn toward Snake Creek Gap.

Two furloughed XVII Corps divisions were also delayed

Administrative problems kept them from reaching Cairo, Illinois, in time for the campaign.

McPherson’s force fell to five divisions

By April 27, only about 24,000 men were available for the opening movement.

Sherman adopted a third plan

McPherson would move through Chattanooga, LaFayette, Villanow, and Snake Creek Gap to cut the railroad near Resaca.

Taylor’s Ridge screened the flanking column

The ridge hid McPherson from Confederate observers but had enough gaps to maintain contact with Thomas west of Rocky Face Ridge.

The revised plan followed Thomas’s earlier proposal

George H. Thomas had suggested using Snake Creek Gap after U.S. scouts found it unobstructed and unfortified.

Sherman assigned the smaller army to the gap

He kept Thomas’s larger Army of the Cumberland as the main force and used McPherson as the army group’s right wing.

The railroad was the operational objective

If McPherson cut the line at Resaca, Thomas and Schofield could press Johnston south from Dalton toward a blocked retreat route.

Johnston used intelligence to prepare a defense

Outnumbered, he sought to identify Sherman’s main movement early enough to concentrate at the threatened point.

Confederate intelligence was generally accurate

Scouts, cavalry patrols, and spies tracked U.S. formations in Tennessee and Alabama, especially Thomas’s army near Chattanooga.

Johnston expected the main attack near Dalton

He anticipated pressure at Mill Creek Gap, northern Rocky Face Ridge, and Crow Valley.

Johnston expected McPherson to move toward Rome or Alabama

This assumption caused Confederate headquarters to discount the possibility of a move through Snake Creek Gap toward Resaca.

Johnston prepared his long left flank

He shifted cavalry near Rome and Resaca, accelerated work on Rome’s defenses, and posted James Cantey’s infantry there.

The U.S. armies reached their opening positions

By May 7, XX Corps stood west of Dug Gap, IV Corps faced northern Rocky Face Ridge, and XXIII Corps faced Crow Valley.

Johnston built a strong Dalton line

Hood held Rocky Face Ridge and Crow Valley with additional divisions from Hardee; Hardee kept two divisions in reserve near Dalton.

The Confederate left lacked reconnaissance

Wheeler concentrated cavalry near Dalton instead of maintaining strong scouting coverage southwest toward Snake Creek Gap.

Richmond ordered major reinforcements to Georgia

On May 4, Davis and Bragg ordered Leonidas Polk to send William W. Loring’s division and join Johnston with as many troops as possible.

Polk began concentrating more than 20,000 men

Three infantry divisions and one cavalry division started moving toward Blue Mountain, Alabama, before later receiving orders for Rome.
