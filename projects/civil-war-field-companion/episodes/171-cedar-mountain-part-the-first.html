CEDAR MOUNTAIN (Part the First)

Operational movement without a battle in this episode. Robert E. Lee sent Thomas J. “Stonewall” Jackson to protect Gordonsville, reinforced him with A. P. Hill’s Light Division, and authorized a limited offensive. On August 7 Jackson began moving toward Nathaniel Banks’s isolated II Corps near Culpeper
Episode scopeLee’s post–Seven Days reorganization through Jackson’s August 7 advanceThe episode establishes the strategic and command situation before the August 9 Battle of Cedar Mountain. It does not describe the battle itself.
Strategic problemLee had to watch both McClellan at Harrison’s Landing and Pope in northern VirginiaA junction between the Army of the Potomac and the new Army of Virginia would place a much larger Federal force between Richmond and Washington.
Harrison’s LandingLee judged McClellan’s position too strong for a direct attackThe Army of the Potomac was protected by fieldworks and United States Navy gunfire from the James River. Lee began drawing most of his army back toward Richmond on July 8.
Army repairCaptured Federal stores helped refit the Army of Northern VirginiaAround Richmond, Lee also reviewed the performance of officers and addressed command failures exposed during the Seven Days.
Command reformLee grouped divisions into large commands under Longstreet and JacksonThe Confederate formations were called wings or commands because corps had not yet been formally authorized. Formal corps organization followed in November 1862.
Longstreet’s wingFive divisions under Richard H. Anderson, David R. Jones, Cadmus M. Wilcox, James L. Kemper, and John Bell HoodThe episode identifies James Longstreet as commander of the larger of Lee’s two new infantry groupings.
Jackson’s wingCharles S. Winder’s and Richard S. Ewell’s divisionsWinder took over Jackson’s former division. Lee initially kept A. P. Hill’s Light Division under his own direction, then sent it to Jackson in late July.
Confederate cavalryJ. E. B. Stuart with brigades under Wade Hampton, Fitzhugh Lee, and Beverly H. RobertsonThe cavalry remained an army-level organization rather than part of either infantry wing.
Leadership changesJohn B. Magruder, Benjamin Huger, and Theophilus H. Holmes were removed from prominent field rolesLee’s reorganization included reassignment or removal of senior officers whose Seven Days performance he judged unsatisfactory.
Longstreet–Hill disputeA newspaper controversy led to Hill’s arrest and preparations for a duelLongstreet objected to a Richmond Examiner account praising Hill’s role at Glendale. Hill then refused communication with Longstreet; Lee intervened before a duel occurred.
Pope’s occupation policyFederal troops would requisition supplies and civilians could be held responsible for partisan attacksThe episode summarizes Pope’s July orders governing subsistence, guerrilla activity, and civilians. Lee protested the policy to General in Chief Henry W. Halleck.
Federal objectiveAdvance toward Gordonsville through Culpeper Court HouseSecretary of War Edwin M. Stanton had assigned Pope’s Army of Virginia the task of moving toward Gordonsville. Pope ordered Banks forward on July 12.
GordonsvilleJunction of the Orange & Alexandria and Virginia Central railroadsThe Virginia Central connected Richmond with the Shenandoah Valley. A Federal seizure or destruction of the junction would damage an important Confederate transport link.
Jackson dispatchedWinder’s and Ewell’s divisions moved toward Gordonsville under July 13 ordersThe episode estimates the two divisions at about 14,000 men. National Park Service summaries likewise identify Lee’s mid-July movement of Jackson to protect the junction.
Hatch’s raidJohn P. Hatch failed to execute Pope’s intended rapid cavalry strikeHatch took infantry, artillery, baggage, and three days of preparation. The column turned back after finding Confederates in strength near Gordonsville.
Cavalry command changeJohn Buford replaced Hatch and became a brigadier general of volunteersPope removed Hatch from the cavalry assignment after the failed raid and selected a staff officer who would soon become one of the war’s best-known cavalry commanders.
Jackson at GordonsvilleHis force reached the railroad junction by July 19The Confederate arrival blocked the immediate Federal plan to damage the Virginia Central Railroad.
Hill reinforces JacksonThe Light Division began arriving at Gordonsville on July 27The episode gives Hill about 12,000 men and says the division nearly doubled Jackson’s strength. Official summaries place Jackson’s eventual force in the area at about 24,000.
Lee’s command warningLee urged Jackson to consult his division commandersLee specifically pointed to Hill as an officer with whom Jackson could confer. The advice reflected concern about Jackson’s secrecy and Hill’s forceful temperament.
Mosby’s intelligenceJohn S. Mosby reported Federal troops moving north from the Virginia PeninsulaAfter his prisoner exchange, Mosby described transport activity at Fort Monroe. Lee concluded that Ambrose E. Burnside’s men were moving to reinforce Pope.
Lee authorizes actionJackson could attack an isolated part of Pope’s army before Federal reinforcements arrivedLee left the timing and location to Jackson’s judgment because the Confederate force was not large enough to attack Pope’s entire army safely.
Pope takes the fieldPope joined the Army of Virginia at the end of JulyAfter inspecting his command, he prepared to move against Jackson and predicted on August 3 that he would take Gordonsville within ten days unless Jackson received major reinforcements.
Federal concentrationOn August 6 Pope ordered his army toward Culpeper between the Rappahannock and RapidanFederal cavalry covered river crossings while Pope waited for Burnside and units of the Army of the Potomac.
McClellan’s withdrawal orderHalleck ordered the Army of the Potomac off the Peninsula on August 3The order directed movement by water toward Aquia Creek and a junction with Pope. The episode gives August 4, the date on which the order was sent to McClellan; official Army history dates Halleck’s directive August 3.
Halleck’s restrictionPope was warned not to risk a disaster before reinforcements arrivedThe warning conflicted with Pope’s public emphasis on aggressive action and left Banks forward near Culpeper while the rest of the army was still concentrating.
August 7 decisionJackson moved after learning that Banks’s II Corps was isolated near CulpeperThe episode estimates Banks at about 11,000 men. Jackson planned to march through Orange Court House, cross the Rapidan, and attack before other Federal formations joined Banks.
Senior command casualtiesNone in the action covered by this episodeNo battle occurs within the episode’s timeline. The August 9 fighting and its command casualties belong to the next episode’s scope.
Recommended book“Second Manassas 1862: Robert E. Lee’s Greatest Victory” by John LangellierThe transcript renders the surname approximately as “Lengelier.” The publisher and official podcast listing give John Langellier.
Transcript normalizationscorps, Ewell, Huger, Gordonsville, Charles S. Winder, and John LangellierThe automated transcript includes “core,” “Yule,” “U.J.,” “Gordon’sville,” “Winders,” and “Lengelier.” “Johnson decided to strike” at 19:39 refers to Jackson.
