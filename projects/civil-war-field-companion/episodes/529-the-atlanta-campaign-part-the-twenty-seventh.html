What happened

The episode returns to Kennesaw Mountain

Johnston ordered a withdrawal after Sherman moved south around the Confederate left. The retreat began on July 2 and U.S. troops found the works empty early on July 3.

U.S. troops occupied the Confederate positions

George H. Thomas reported the retreat before 3 a.m. John A. Logan’s XV Corps confirmed that Little Kennesaw was vacant.

Soldiers saw the graves left by the failed assaults

Lyman Whitney of the 34th Illinois questioned why a flanking movement had not been used before the June 27 attacks.

The episode evaluates the battle

Kennesaw was a Confederate tactical victory within a campaign that U.S. forces ultimately won. The Confederate position was abandoned less than a week after the assaults.

Confederate accounts emphasized the victory

Officers described high morale, continued confidence in Johnston, and the heavy losses inflicted on the attacking U.S. troops.

U.S. soldiers described the attacks as unnecessary

Veterans criticized the order to attack fortified high ground and continued to question the decision decades later.

The episode asks why Sherman attacked

Sherman usually preferred maneuver, but he believed circumstances sometimes required assaults against strong positions.

Chickasaw Bayou provides an earlier comparison

In December 1862, Sherman attacked fortified heights north of Vicksburg while separated from Grant and lost almost 1,800 men.

Tunnel Hill provides a second comparison

At Chattanooga in November 1863, Sherman attacked Patrick Cleburne’s division on difficult ground and lost just over 1,700 men.

The Kennesaw phase had stalled the campaign

Rain, woods, and expanding Confederate works slowed U.S. flanking movements and kept Sherman’s armies in place.

Sherman wanted to prevent reinforcements from leaving Georgia

Grant had directed Sherman to keep Johnston occupied so Confederate troops could not be sent to reinforce Robert E. Lee in Virginia.

Concern about detached Confederate troops influenced the decision

Historian Earl J. Hess argues that Sherman feared Johnston could hold Kennesaw with fewer men and send the rest east.

The episode separates strategy from tactics

Sherman understood large-scale strategic movement, but the planning and coordination of the June 27 attacks were poor.

The later turning movement succeeded

The movement begun July 1 forced Johnston out without another direct attack, but it temporarily separated much of Sherman’s force from the railroad supply line.

The historical judgment is that the attacks were a mistake

The episode concludes that Sherman recovered from the failure by resuming maneuver and continuing toward Atlanta.
