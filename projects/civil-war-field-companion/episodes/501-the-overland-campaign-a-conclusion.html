THE OVERLAND CAMPAIGN: A Conclusion

Grant did not destroy Lee’s army in the open, but he retained the initiative, prevented major reinforcements from leaving Virginia, crossed the James River, and shifted the campaign to Petersburg

What happened

The Overland campaign ended at Cold Harbor

The truce after the June 3 assault marked the end of Grant’s effort to destroy Lee’s army north of Richmond.

Grant chose a new strategy

He planned to cross the James River and cut the railroads that supplied Richmond and the Army of Northern Virginia through Petersburg.

The armies remained in place during preparations

The Army of the Potomac held the Cold Harbor trenches to keep Lee occupied while U.S. forces attacked rail connections north and west of Richmond.

Sheridan and Hunter received railroad objectives

Sheridan was to damage the Virginia Central Railroad and meet David Hunter near Charlottesville; Hunter was expected to threaten Lynchburg and the James River Canal.

The supporting operations failed

Wade Hampton stopped Sheridan at Trevilian Station. Hunter remained west of the Blue Ridge, did not capture Lynchburg, and was forced into West Virginia by Jubal Early.

Grant crossed the James River

The Army of the Potomac used a long pontoon bridge to move south of the river before Lee fully understood the operation.

The first attack did not take Petersburg

U.S. forces reached the city before most of Lee’s army but did not capture it. Both sides constructed larger defensive systems and began a siege lasting nearly ten months.

U.S. casualties reached about 55,000

From May 4 through June 12, the Army of the Potomac and IX Corps lost about 55,000 killed, wounded, or missing. Supporting offensives added roughly 10,000 more.

Later victories changed public interpretation

Mobile Bay and the capture of Atlanta restored Northern confidence, supported Lincoln’s reelection, and caused many people to view the Virginia campaign as part of an eventual U.S. victory.

The defense of Grant’s campaign is summarized

Supporters argued that Grant retained the initiative, imposed irreplaceable losses on Lee, held the Army of Northern Virginia in place, protected Washington, and supported Sherman’s campaign.

Critics rejected the casualty cost

Grant’s opponents accepted that he had restricted Lee’s options but argued that his methods were unimaginative and wasted soldiers’ lives.

Lee’s campaign is reassessed

Lee repeatedly sought a major counterattack but never regained the initiative. Delayed troop concentration and accumulated losses limited his later opportunities.

Confederate casualties approached 33,000

The episode gives 11,125 casualties in the Wilderness, 12,450 at Spotsylvania, more than 1,500 at the North Anna, and nearly 33,000 for the whole campaign.

Subordinate commanders affected the outcome

The episode shifts from Grant and Lee to the commanders responsible for the supporting offensives and the army corps.

Western Virginia operations achieved little

William Averell failed to damage the salt and lead works substantially, while George Crook damaged the railroad at Dublin but withdrew.

Sigel and Hunter failed in the Shenandoah Valley

Franz Sigel lost at New Market. Hunter advanced successfully at first but did not complete the railroad and Lynchburg objectives.

Butler’s assignment is reconsidered

Benjamin Butler failed to break through at Bermuda Hundred, but he initially held Confederate reinforcements away from Lee and continued to threaten the Richmond–Petersburg railroad.

Grant and Meade divided army-level command

Grant set the larger strategy while Meade managed the Army of the Potomac. Their working relationship was effective, but their command styles differed.

Grant adapted after the Wilderness

Instead of retreating after an inconclusive and costly battle, Grant moved south and continued the campaign toward Spotsylvania.

Warren’s caution cost opportunities

Gouverneur K. Warren repeatedly questioned orders and failed to attack exposed Confederate movements on June 1, frustrating Grant.

Sheridan changed the use of cavalry

After serious failures in the Wilderness, Sheridan emphasized independent raids and helped turn U.S. cavalry into a more powerful offensive force.

Breckinridge and Beauregard stopped supporting offensives

John C. Breckinridge defeated Sigel at New Market and P. G. T. Beauregard contained Butler, allowing reinforcements to reach Lee.

Confederate cavalry lost Stuart

J. E. B. Stuart screened Lee’s movements but was mortally wounded while fighting Sheridan at Yellow Tavern.

Longstreet and Early had limited time in corps command

Longstreet was seriously wounded by Confederate fire in the Wilderness. Early took command after Ewell but inherited a depleted corps.

U.S. control of waterways sustained the campaign

Water transport made the Bermuda Hundred operation and the supply bases at Belle Plain, Port Royal, and White House Landing possible.

Ferrero’s Black division was kept from combat

About two-thirds of Edward Ferrero’s IX Corps division were Black soldiers. The division guarded wagons while about 48,000 other replacements went to the front.

The Lost Cause interpretation is defined

The episode identifies it as a postwar account that minimized slavery, glorified the Confederacy, and explained defeat mainly through U.S. numerical and industrial superiority.

The Grant–Lee duel does not explain the entire campaign

A complete account must include the Shenandoah Valley, southwestern Virginia, Bermuda Hundred, cavalry operations, logistics, naval power, and the Atlanta campaign.

The campaign formed part of a coordinated national strategy

Grant continued pressure after each battle, kept Lee from helping other Confederate armies, and crossed the James when the direct route to Richmond failed.
