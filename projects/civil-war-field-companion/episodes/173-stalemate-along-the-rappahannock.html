Episode 173 · August 18–24, 1862 · The Rapidan and Rappahannock river corridors from Culpeper and Gordonsville to Freeman’s Ford, White Sulphur Springs, Rappahannock Station, Warrenton, and Catlett Station in central and northern Virginia

STALEMATE ALONG THE RAPPAHANNOCK

Indecisive along the Rappahannock. Pope escaped Lee’s planned turning movement at the Rapidan and held the north and east bank of the Rappahannock. Confederate probes did not establish a permanent crossing, but Stuart’s raid captured intelligence that confirmed Pope’s dispositions and the approaching Federal reinforcements, helping Lee plan the next phase of the Northern Virginia Campaign
Episode scopeLee’s advance from the Rapidan, the August 21–23 actions along the Rappahannock, and Stuart’s Catlett Station raidThe episode follows the armies from the immediate aftermath of Cedar Mountain through the recovery of Ewell’s flood-isolated bridgehead before dawn on August 24.
Strategic deadlineDefeat Pope before the Army of the Potomac could reinforce himMcClellan’s withdrawal from the Peninsula freed Lee to concentrate against Pope, but the same movement meant that Federal reinforcements would soon arrive in northern Virginia.
Confederate concentrationLongstreet joined Jackson near Gordonsville; Lee arrived August 15Lee left a smaller force under Gustavus W. Smith to protect Richmond and took direct command of the field army assembled south of Pope.
Lee’s first planTurn Pope’s left and get between the Army of Virginia and WashingtonLee selected an eastward movement across the Rapidan rather than Longstreet’s proposed movement around Pope’s right. The intended attack date slipped from August 18 to August 20.
Captured Confederate ordersFederal cavalry obtained Lee’s advance orders on August 18A Federal patrol surprised Stuart’s headquarters. Stuart escaped, but his adjutant, Maj. Norman R. Fitzhugh, and a copy of Lee’s orders were captured.
Pope’s responseWithdraw behind the RappahannockPope informed Halleck shortly after noon on August 18, kept campfires burning that night, and began a concealed movement northeast before midnight.
Withdrawal completedThe Army of Virginia was across by noon on August 20The night movement was confused and unpopular in the ranks, but Lee did not discover it until Federal columns and wagon dust were observed from Clark’s Mountain on August 19.
Rappahannock barrierKelly’s Ford, Rappahannock Station, Beverly Ford, Freeman’s Ford, and White Sulphur Springs were the principal contested crossingsPope strongly guarded the accessible lower and central crossings while maintaining communications toward Manassas Junction and Fredericksburg.
Opposing movements on August 20Longstreet moved toward Kelly’s Ford; Jackson moved toward Rappahannock StationHeat and dust slowed Jackson, and the full Confederate army did not reach the river line until August 21.
Freeman’s Ford probeBohlen’s three-regiment Federal force crossed after Confederate guns fell silentSigel sent Brig. Gen. Henry Bohlen across to investigate. Isaac R. Trimble’s concealed brigade attacked, and John Bell Hood’s arriving brigade joined the action.
Freeman’s Ford resultThe Federal force was routed back across the riverBohlen was killed. Men were shot while retreating or drowned while trying to recross the river. Jackson had already begun shifting his main force farther upriver.
White Sulphur Springs crossingEwell established a bridgehead late on August 22The 13th Georgia crossed the ford under artillery cover at about 4 p.m.; Jubal Early’s brigade crossed an old dam about a mile downstream.
Flood-isolated bridgeheadA severe rainstorm made the Rappahannock impassableThe sudden rise stranded Early and the 13th Georgia on Pope’s side of the river and also prevented Pope from crossing elsewhere to attack Jackson’s rear.
Pope’s countermoveMarch north against the isolated ConfederatesOn August 23 Pope sent his army toward White Sulphur Springs, trusting the flood to prevent Longstreet from crossing against his rear. Rain and mud delayed Milroy’s brigade and Buford’s cavalry until late in the day.
Bridgehead recoveredEarly and Lawton withdrew before dawn on August 24Jackson’s engineers rebuilt a bridge on August 23. Jackson initially reinforced the north-bank position, but Ewell inspected it and removed the exhausted troops during the night.
Rappahannock Station bridgeheadHartsuff’s brigade held the south-bank approaches to the railroad bridgePope had kept the bridge intact as a possible route back across the river. Its defense required a Federal position on the Confederate side.
Artillery duelNearly 50 Confederate guns opened on August 23Federal batteries answered from the north bank. The episode describes the exchange as one of the largest artillery duels fought to that point in the war.
Railroad bridge destroyedThe burning span collapsed around noon on August 23Hartsuff withdrew under Pope’s orders. Brig. Gen. Zealous B. Tower’s rear guard first tried artillery fire, then set the bridge ablaze as Confederate infantry approached.
Stuart’s raiding forceAbout 1,500 selected cavalrymenLee approved Stuart’s proposal on August 22. The 1st and 11th Virginia Cavalry moved against Catlett Station while the 9th Virginia sought Pope’s headquarters baggage.
Catlett Station objectiveBurn the Orange and Alexandria Railroad bridge over Cedar RunDestroying the bridge could have cut Pope’s principal supply line and forced him to leave the Rappahannock. Rain-soaked timbers would not burn.
Catlett Station intelligencePope’s dispatch book was capturedThe papers included marching orders, formation strengths, unit locations, and information about the approach of Army of the Potomac reinforcements.
Operational effect of the raidConfirmation that Lee’s opportunity was closingThe captured papers largely confirmed what Confederate commanders already suspected: Pope’s right was vulnerable, but Federal reinforcements would soon alter the balance.
Campaign result through August 24Neither army forced a decisive crossingPope blocked the principal crossings, and Jackson’s temporary bridgehead was withdrawn. Lee next chose a wider turning movement around Pope’s right.
Recommended book“The Second Bull Run Campaign: July–August 1862” by David G. MartinThe hosts recommend the volume as an introduction to the campaign and note its extended sidebars on people, places, events, and units.

United States

Brig. Gen. Henry Bohlen
