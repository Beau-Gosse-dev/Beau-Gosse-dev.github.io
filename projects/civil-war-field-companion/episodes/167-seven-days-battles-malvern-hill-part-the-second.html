SEVEN DAYS' BATTLES: MALVERN HILL (Part the Second)

United States victory at Malvern Hill. Federal artillery and infantry repulsed a series of disjointed Confederate attacks on July 1. George B. McClellan nevertheless withdrew to Harrison’s Landing, where naval guns and field fortifications protected the army. Robert E. Lee ended the Seven Days campaign and returned most of his army toward Richmond
Episode scopeThe fighting at Malvern Hill and the close of the Seven Days campaignThe episode covers the failed Confederate artillery plan, the infantry assaults late on July 1, the Federal withdrawal on July 2, and Lee’s decision on July 4 to end the campaign.
Battlefield advantageOpen approaches exposed attackers to concentrated Federal fireMalvern Hill was a modest elevation about two and one-half miles north of the James River. Its military strength came from long, gently sloping fields of fire and a defensible plateau rather than steep height.
Confederate artillery planTwo massed batteries were intended to create a crossfireLee assigned James Longstreet to the Confederate right and Thomas J. Jackson to the left. Robert H. Chilton drafted the related infantry order, but the guns were not concentrated as planned.
Largest Confederate concentrationSixteen guns in William H. C. Whiting’s sectorThe episode identifies Whiting’s three batteries in the Poindexter wheat field as the largest Confederate concentration of the day. Thirty-one Federal guns about 800 yards away answered them.
Artillery deployment failureBatteries entered separately and were defeated in detailJackson’s batteries began before Longstreet’s guns were ready. West of Willis Church Road, no more than eight Confederate guns were active together in the episode’s account, while 35 nearby batteries never entered the fight.
Confederate artillery casualtiesNearly 100 in the episode’s accountThe artillery exchange produced little effect on the Federal position and also exposed supporting Confederate infantry and horses to concentrated fire.
Lee changes courseBy about 3 p.m., the original artillery plan had failedLee and Longstreet examined the Federal right for a possible July 2 attack, but Lee did not cancel Chilton’s earlier untimed order for an assault on July 1.
Conflicting battlefield reportsRoutine Federal battery relief was mistaken for withdrawalFederal batteries that had expended much of their ammunition moved back to resupply while replacements came forward. Whiting reported that Confederate fire had driven them away.
Magruder’s reportArmistead had driven off Federal sharpshootersJohn B. Magruder sent Capt. A. G. Dickinson to report his arrival and Lewis A. Armistead’s limited advance. The report did not establish that the main Federal line was retreating.
Lee’s definite attack orderReached Magruder at about 5 p.m.Lee directed Magruder to advance rapidly, press forward his whole line, and follow up Armistead’s reported success. Chilton’s earlier order reached Magruder near the same time.
Magruder’s available forceAbout 15,000 men by his estimateNot all of these troops were aligned or ready when the orders arrived. Brigades entered the fight as they reached their assigned sectors.
Opening infantry attacksAmbrose R. Wright and William Mahone advanced at about 5:30 p.m.Their brigades moved toward the Crew house sector, crossed open ground under artillery fire, and stopped short of the Federal line.
Armistead and CobbBoth assaults were repulsed near the Federal gun lineArmistead’s men reached a forward position near Charles Griffin’s brigade before a counterattack drove them back. Howell Cobb’s supporting attack forced some infantry behind the guns but broke under point-blank canister.
Ames’s battery expenditure1,392 rounds of shell and canisterThe episode gives this total for the six guns of Battery A, 5th U.S. Artillery, commanded by Lt. Adelbert Ames west of Willis Church Road.
D. H. Hill’s attackFive brigades entered piecemeal through broken wooded terrainHill heard the attack near 5:30 p.m. and treated it as the signal for a general advance. Woods disrupted alignment, and the brigades did not emerge as one coordinated force.
D. H. Hill’s division lossesNearly 2,000 casualties from about 8,000 engaged in the episode’s accountThe 3rd Alabama lost six color bearers during the advance. Hill later condemned the attack because his division had been sent against a strong position without coordinated support.
Barksdale’s brigadeAbout one-third of 1,200 men became casualtiesThe Mississippians advanced toward the cabins and barn north of the Crew house but did not break the main Federal defense.
McLaws’s late attackOnly parts of Semmes’s and Kershaw’s brigades entered before darknessThe episode states that Paul J. Semmes’s troops reached the cabin area about 50 yards from the Federal line before a counterattack drove them back.
Closest Confederate advanceThe exact relationship to the Federal line is disputedThe episode states that no Confederate troops penetrated the Union line. The Historic American Landscapes Survey narrative describes a brief penetration late in the battle. The schematic map shows only generalized approach sectors.
Senior commander lossesNo principal general officer was killed or mortally woundedConfederate brigadier generals George B. Anderson and John R. Jones were wounded. The episode does not identify the death or mortal wounding of a senior commander as part of its narrative.
Battle casualtiesAbout 5,355 Confederate and 3,000 United States casualtiesThese are the rounded National Park Service totals. The episode states more than 5,000 Confederate casualties and fewer than 3,000 Federal casualties. Published totals vary by source and classification.
Tactical resultUnited States victoryNo Confederate assault secured the Malvern Hill position on July 1, and the attacking force suffered the larger loss.
Night withdrawalMcClellan ordered the army to Harrison’s LandingFitz John Porter and other commanders favored a counterattack, but McClellan continued the movement to the James. The retreat covered roughly eight to nine miles, depending on the starting point used.
Harrison’s LandingAbout 90,000 troops occupied a compact protected enclaveThe episode describes the remaining army, its animals, and its transport crowded into about four square miles behind field defenses and the guns of the United States Navy.
Campaign conclusionLee ended operations on July 4After observing that the Harrison’s Landing defenses and gunboats prevented a practical assault, Lee ordered most of the Army of Northern Virginia back toward Richmond.
Seven Days lossesMore than 20,000 Confederate and about 16,000 United States casualtiesThese are the episode’s approximate campaign totals. Lee had removed the immediate threat to Richmond, while McClellan preserved his army at the James River.
Recommended book“The Seven Days’ Battles: The War Begins Anew” by Judkin BrowningThe official episode page identifies this title and author. The automated transcript renders the author’s first name as “Jedkin.”

What happened

The Confederate artillery plan is restated

Lee and Longstreet intend to mass guns on both sides of Willis Church Road and create a crossfire before an infantry assault.

Chilton’s order remains imprecise

Lee’s aide drafts the key instruction without a fixed time and without a later cancellation when the artillery plan fails.

Jackson sends batteries forward separately

Guns enter the Poindexter wheat field as they arrive instead of waiting for a larger concentration.

Whiting receives the order to open fire

Whiting objects to placing sixteen guns in the exposed field but carries out Jackson’s order.

Federal guns engage Whiting’s batteries

Thirty-one guns about 800 yards away strike the Confederate pieces, crews, and horses.

The planned crossfire does not develop

Jackson’s guns begin before Longstreet’s batteries are ready, and the Confederate artillery remains divided.

Unused Confederate batteries are counted

The episode states that 35 nearby batteries never enter the battle.

Lee recognizes that the artillery plan has failed

At about 3 p.m., Lee and Longstreet begin examining the Federal right for a possible attack on July 2.

The order sequence remains disputed

The episode distinguishes disagreements about when Chilton wrote the order from the established fact that Lee never cancelled it.

Magruder reaches the field

Magruder assesses Armistead’s forward position, the Federal line, and the effect of Federal artillery.

Dickinson carries Magruder’s report

The message states that Magruder has arrived and that Armistead has driven back Federal sharpshooters.

Federal batteries rotate for ammunition

Confederate observers mistake battery relief and resupply for evidence that the Federal artillery is withdrawing.

Lee orders Magruder to advance

Lee directs Magruder to press forward his whole line based on reports that the enemy is leaving and Armistead has succeeded.

Magruder receives Chilton’s earlier order

The untimed order arrives near Lee’s definite instruction and reinforces Magruder’s conclusion that he must attack immediately.

Wright’s brigade begins the infantry assault

At about 5:30 p.m., Wright advances toward the Crew house under heavy Federal fire, followed by Mahone.

Armistead attacks near Griffin’s line

Armistead reaches a forward position, exchanges fire, and is driven back by concentrated fire and a Federal counterattack.

Cobb’s supporting charge fails

Cobb’s men push Federal infantry behind the gun line but break under point-blank canister.

Ames’s battery expenditure is recorded

Battery A, 5th U.S. Artillery, fires 1,392 rounds of shell and canister during the battle.

D. H. Hill commits his division

Hill hears the assault and orders his brigades forward, although the terrain prevents a coordinated advance.

The episode rejects a Confederate penetration

The narrative states that Hill’s men did not enter the Federal line and came no closer than about 200 yards.

Hill’s losses are summarized

The episode gives nearly 2,000 casualties among roughly 8,000 men engaged in Hill’s division.

Barksdale attacks near the Crew house

The Mississippi brigade advances toward the cabins and barn and loses about one-third of its strength.

McLaws’s division enters near darkness

Lee sends portions of Semmes’s and Kershaw’s brigades farther right, but fading light limits the attack.

Battle casualties are assessed

The episode reports more than 5,000 Confederate casualties and fewer than 3,000 United States casualties.

Lee questions Magruder

Magruder states that he attacked in response to Lee’s repeated orders.

Averell sees the battlefield on July 2

The 3rd Pennsylvania Cavalry commander describes lines of dead and wounded on the slopes as the rear guard leaves.

McClellan declines a counterattack

Despite advice from Porter and other commanders, McClellan orders the army to continue to Harrison’s Landing.

Rain obstructs the withdrawal

The river road becomes mud, and troops abandon equipment and wagons during the movement.

Lee examines Harrison’s Landing

On July 4, Lee determines that field defenses and naval gunfire make a successful assault impractical.

The Seven Days campaign ends

Lee orders most of his army back toward Richmond after McClellan establishes a protected base on the James.

The episode book is identified

The recommendation is Judkin Browning’s “The Seven Days’ Battles: The War Begins Anew.”
