Episode 206 · June–mid-August 1862, with Kentucky political context from May 1861–April 1862 · Northern Mississippi, northern Alabama, Chattanooga and Knoxville in Tennessee, Cumberland Gap, and Kentucky

BLUEGRASS STATE OR BUST!

Braxton Bragg shifted most of the Confederate Army of Mississippi from Tupelo to Chattanooga by a long, indirect rail-and-water route, ending the immediate threat to that city and helping the Confederacy regain the initiative in the western theater. Edmund Kirby Smith then pressed for an independent advance from East Tennessee into Kentucky rather than a preliminary reduction of Cumberland Gap and a prompt junction with Bragg. Bragg accepted a divided offensive despite warning that Don Carlos Buell could defeat the separated armies. Confederate expectations of a mass uprising in Kentucky rested on exaggerated reports and on a provisional government that never displaced the Union-aligned state government

What happened

The western theater story resumes after Corinth

The episode recalls that Halleck’s capture of the strategic rail junction capped the United States victories from Fort Henry and Donelson through Shiloh.

Halleck disperses the victorious army group

Grant received responsibility around Corinth and Memphis, while Buell took roughly 30,000 men east toward Chattanooga along the Memphis and Charleston Railroad.

Halleck is called to Washington

Lincoln selected him as general in chief in July, ending the period in which Grant and Buell had served under one western field commander.

Chattanooga anchors the strategic problem

The small Tennessee River city stood below Lookout Mountain at the junction of lines leading west, northeast through Knoxville, and southeast toward Atlanta.

The railroad hub has consequences far beyond the town

Its capture would assist East Tennessee Unionists, disrupt Confederate communications between east and west, and open a route toward Georgia.

Kirby Smith defends East Tennessee

From an independent command based at Knoxville, Smith had to consider both Buell’s approach from northern Alabama and George W. Morgan’s presence north of Knoxville.

George W. Morgan’s West Point connection is noted

Morgan and Kirby Smith entered West Point together in 1841; Morgan left the academy before Smith graduated with the class of 1845.

United States troops occupy Cumberland Gap

Morgan entered the pass on June 18 after the Confederate withdrawal, creating a potential approach toward Knoxville but also assuming a difficult supply burden.

Buell’s advance loses momentum

His reliance on a railroad under repeated attack by Confederate cavalry and guerrillas slowed his movement and gave the Confederates time to react.

The Confederate field army waits near Tupelo

The Army of Mississippi had remained south of Corinth after Beauregard’s successful withdrawal at the end of May.

Beauregard takes unauthorized medical leave

He transferred command to Bragg and departed for Bladon Springs without obtaining advance approval from Richmond.

Davis makes Bragg the permanent commander

The leave ended Beauregard’s western command and elevated Bragg, already the army’s senior subordinate, to leadership of the main Confederate field army.

Kirby Smith appeals for help

Richmond had no spare reinforcement for Chattanooga, and Bragg initially appeared tied to northern Mississippi by the United States forces near Corinth.

Smith invites Bragg to come east

He promised cooperation and offered to serve under Bragg if the two independent department commanders brought their armies together.

Bragg sees an opportunity to seize the initiative

The dispersed, inactive United States garrisons in Mississippi made a rapid concentration at Chattanooga appear more useful than remaining at Tupelo.

Price and Van Dorn remain behind

Bragg assigned Sterling Price to watch northern Mississippi and Earl Van Dorn to guard Vicksburg while the main infantry force shifted east.

Vicksburg becomes the remaining river barrier

Farragut’s fleet had taken New Orleans and Baton Rouge, while the federal river flotilla won at Memphis; neither force, however, had reduced Vicksburg.

Bragg begins the theater-changing movement

Cavalry, artillery, wagons, and supplies traveled east across northern Alabama while infantry used a much longer route over the Confederate rail network.

The infantry detours through Mobile and Atlanta

The roughly 770-mile trip avoided the direct railroad controlled by Buell and became the war’s first long-distance transfer of an entire field army.

Bragg initially looks toward Middle Tennessee

Chattanooga could serve as the base for recovering Nashville, forcing Buell onto the defensive, and eventually moving farther north.

A western Mississippi diversion is expected

Bragg anticipated that Price and Van Dorn would threaten Grant, preventing United States reinforcements from reaching Buell.

The first infantry reaches Chattanooga

Some units arrived July 29 and Bragg arrived July 30, but the separate movement of animals, guns, wagons, and supplies delayed full concentration.

Bragg and Kirby Smith confer

Their Chattanooga meeting began July 31 and continued into August 1, producing apparent agreement but not a unified chain of command.

Smith hesitates to surrender independence

Although he spoke cooperatively, his later choices showed reluctance to become Bragg’s subordinate in a campaign centered on Middle Tennessee.

Kentucky displaces Nashville in Smith’s planning

The episode interprets Smith’s conduct after the conference as a decision to preserve his separate army and strike north from Knoxville.

Davis expects combined success

The Confederate president anticipated that action against Buell, reinforced by Price and Van Dorn against Grant, could recover Tennessee and Kentucky.

Smith reshapes the plan from Knoxville

After the advertisement break, the narrative returns to Smith using the delay in Bragg’s concentration to pursue his own operational preference.

The original sequence targets Cumberland Gap first

Bragg would lend two divisions; Smith would reduce Morgan’s garrison and then join Bragg for the movement into Middle Tennessee.

Smith proposes bypassing the Gap

On August 9 he argued that the well-supplied United States position could resist a long investment even if smaller passes were used to sever its communications.

Bragg warns against divided operations

His August 10 response recognized Buell as the principal danger and stated that separation could imperil the campaign, yet Bragg did not compel a junction.

Both commanders turn toward Kentucky

Smith chose the Bluegrass State as his independent field of action, and Bragg accepted a broader northward campaign despite the command risk.

John Hunt Morgan magnifies Confederate hopes

After his July cavalry raid, Morgan reported that 25,000 or 30,000 Kentuckians would join an invasion—an extravagant prediction the campaign would disprove.

The summer offensive mood spreads

Western Confederates wanted to reverse the defeats of early 1862, while Lee’s Seven Days victories encouraged expectations of simultaneous gains.

Kentucky’s Confederate delegation presses Davis

Exiled politicians urged liberation and the use of Kentucky officers for recruiting, projecting tens of thousands of enlistments.

A Confederate star did not equal legal secession

Kentucky never left the United States; Confederate recognition rested on a provisional government created by a pro-secession minority.

Kentucky’s neutrality breaks down

Polk’s occupation of Columbus in September 1861 was followed by United States entry at Paducah and a legislative demand that Confederate forces withdraw.

The Russellville Convention creates a rival government

Pro-Confederate delegates repudiated the elected government at Frankfort and chose George W. Johnson as provisional governor.

The provisional government follows the army into exile

Recognized by the Confederacy on December 10, 1861, it retreated from Bowling Green after Forts Henry and Donelson fell.

George W. Johnson is mortally wounded at Shiloh

Serving with the 4th Kentucky Infantry, he was struck on April 7, received aid after capture, and died the next day.

Richard Hawes succeeds Johnson

The second provisional governor remained a refugee and advocated a Confederate campaign that could return his government to Kentucky.

Davis seeks theater-wide coordination

As commander in chief, he tried to align Bragg’s and Smith’s western movements with Lee’s offensive against United States forces in Virginia.

Lee approves but cannot wait

Lee supported the proposed Kentucky movement, yet the need to strike John Pope before McClellan could reinforce him prevented close synchronization.

The western strategic picture reverses

By mid-August the Confederates had regained initiative, but their Kentucky campaign began with divided authority, changing objectives, and persistent supply risks.

The episode recommends Earl J. Hess’s Bragg biography

“Braxton Bragg: The Most Hated Man of the Confederacy” is presented as a careful reassessment of a commander long treated with unusual hostility.
