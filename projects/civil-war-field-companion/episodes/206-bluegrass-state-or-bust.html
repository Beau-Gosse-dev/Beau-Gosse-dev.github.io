Episode 206 · June–mid-August 1862, with Kentucky political context from May 1861–April 1862 · Northern Mississippi, northern Alabama, Chattanooga and Knoxville in Tennessee, Cumberland Gap, and Kentucky

BLUEGRASS STATE OR BUST!

Braxton Bragg shifted most of the Confederate Army of Mississippi from Tupelo to Chattanooga by a long, indirect rail-and-water route, ending the immediate threat to that city and helping the Confederacy regain the initiative in the western theater. Edmund Kirby Smith then pressed for an independent advance from East Tennessee into Kentucky rather than a preliminary reduction of Cumberland Gap and a prompt junction with Bragg. Bragg accepted a divided offensive despite warning that Don Carlos Buell could defeat the separated armies. Confederate expectations of a mass uprising in Kentucky rested on exaggerated reports and on a provisional government that never displaced the Union-aligned state government
Episode scopeThe strategic prelude to the 1862 Confederate invasion of KentuckyThe episode follows the western armies from the aftermath of Corinth through Bragg’s concentration at Chattanooga and the mid-August decision to make Kentucky the principal objective.
United States position after CorinthA broad occupation, not a concentrated pursuitHenry Halleck dispersed his large force to hold northern Mississippi, western Tennessee, and northern Alabama rather than pursue the Confederate army south from Corinth.
Buell’s assignmentAdvance toward Chattanooga with roughly 30,000 menMaj. Gen. Don Carlos Buell moved east along the Memphis and Charleston Railroad, repairing and guarding the line as his army depended on it for supply.
Correct name: Ormsby MitchelNot “Ormsby Mitchell”The automated transcript adds a second l. Mitchel’s division already held important portions of the railroad across northern Alabama.
Halleck’s new officeGeneral in chief from July 23, 1862Lincoln summoned Halleck to Washington on July 11. The Army Center of Military History dates his tenure as commanding general from July 23.
End of unified western field commandGrant and Buell again operated independentlyHalleck’s departure removed the commander who had exercised authority over both armies during the Corinth campaign.
Chattanooga’s importanceTennessee River port, mountain gateway, and railroad junctionRail connections ran west toward Memphis, northeast through Knoxville toward Virginia, and southeast toward Atlanta. Control threatened Confederate east–west communications and opened routes into East Tennessee and Georgia.
Confederate East Tennessee commandMaj. Gen. Edmund Kirby SmithSmith commanded an independent department and the Army of East Tennessee from Knoxville; he was not automatically Bragg’s subordinate merely because Bragg outranked him.
Cumberland Gap occupationUnion forces entered June 18, 1862Brig. Gen. George W. Morgan occupied the pass after the Confederate garrison withdrew. The position threatened Knoxville but also required supply through difficult mountain country.
Buell’s central weaknessA long and repeatedly disrupted railroad supply lineConfederate cavalry and guerrillas damaged track behind the advance, while Buell devoted time and troops to repair and protection.
Beauregard’s leaveDeparted Tupelo in June without prior approval from RichmondP. G. T. Beauregard transferred temporary command to Bragg and went to Bladon Springs, Alabama, for his health. Jefferson Davis treated the unauthorized absence as grounds to replace him.
Bragg takes commandArmy of Mississippi and Department No. 2Davis made Bragg the permanent western commander in June 1862. The field army was still called the Army of Mississippi during the Kentucky campaign; it became the Army of Tennessee later that year.
Forces left in MississippiSterling Price near Tupelo; Earl Van Dorn responsible for VicksburgThese forces protected northern Mississippi and the remaining Confederate strongpoint on the Mississippi River while Bragg shifted the main field army east.
Vicksburg’s position in summer 1862The principal Confederate barrier remaining on the MississippiAfter the fall of New Orleans, Baton Rouge, Memphis, and other river positions, Union naval forces tried but failed to compel Vicksburg’s surrender.
Correct term: Pook TurtlesCity-class ironclad gunboatsThe transcript renders the nickname as “Pukes.” The vessels designed by Samuel M. Pook were commonly called Pook Turtles.
Bragg’s long transferAbout 30,000 men from Tupelo to Chattanooga by railBecause Buell blocked the direct line, the infantry traveled roughly 770 miles through Mobile, Montgomery, and Atlanta, including a crossing of Mobile Bay. The whole concentration took weeks; individual contingents could complete the journey in about six days.
Historical significance of the transferFirst long-distance transfer of an entire field army during the warThe U.S. Army Center of Military History uses this formulation. Cavalry, artillery, wagons, and supplies did not all follow the infantry’s rail route.
Chattanooga concentrationFirst infantry arrived July 29; Bragg arrived July 30The episode dates the command conference with Kirby Smith to July 31 and the early hours of August 1.
Bragg’s initial operational emphasisRecover Middle Tennessee and threaten NashvilleThe developing plan also contemplated a northward movement toward Kentucky and the Ohio River. Surviving interpretations differ on how fully the Kentucky axis was settled at the July 31 conference.
Independent command problemCooperation depended on consent, not a single theater commanderBragg commanded Department No. 2 while Smith commanded the separate Department of East Tennessee. Davis corresponded with both, and Bragg lacked normal authority to compel Smith’s compliance.
Original Cumberland Gap sequenceReinforce Smith, reduce the Gap, then join BraggBragg sent two divisions to Smith. Smith argued on August 9 that George Morgan’s well-stocked garrison could withstand a lengthy investment even if its communications were cut.
Smith’s alternativeBypass Cumberland Gap and invade KentuckySmith preferred a direct advance north from Knoxville, preserving his independent army and choosing Kentucky rather than Middle Tennessee as his theater of action.
Bragg’s warningBuell could defeat separated Confederate forcesOn August 10 Bragg urged concentration against the Army of the Ohio but ultimately accepted Smith’s separate axis and promised cooperation farther north.
John Hunt Morgan’s first Kentucky raidJuly 1862The cavalry raid alarmed Union authorities and encouraged Confederate hopes, but Morgan greatly overstated the recruiting response he had found.
Morgan’s recruiting prediction25,000 or 30,000 volunteersHis July 16 dispatch claimed the country could be secured and that tens of thousands would join. The prediction was not borne out during the campaign.
Kentucky’s legal statusA United States state that never secededKentucky was a slaveholding border state with divided loyalties. Its elected legislature rejected secession and eventually aligned the state government with the United States.
Correct name: Beriah MagoffinKentucky’s elected governorThe transcript renders his name phonetically. Magoffin favored neutrality and had Southern sympathies, but he did not have legislative support to take Kentucky out of the Union.
Neutrality collapsesSeptember 1861Confederate Maj. Gen. Leonidas Polk’s occupation of Columbus on September 3 was the first open entry by a belligerent field force. Union troops then occupied Paducah, and the legislature demanded that Confederate forces leave.
Russellville ConventionNovember 18–20, 1861Pro-Confederate delegates created a provisional government, elected George W. Johnson governor, and challenged the elected government at Frankfort without displacing it.
Confederate admission of KentuckyDecember 10, 1861The Confederacy recognized the provisional government and represented Kentucky with a star, although that government exercised power only where Confederate arms permitted.
George W. Johnson at ShilohMortally wounded April 7; died April 8, 1862The provisional governor served with the 4th Kentucky Infantry, was found and aided by Union Brig. Gen. Alexander McDowell McCook, and died aboard a hospital steamer.
Provisional successorRichard HawesHawes became the second Confederate provisional governor and lobbied Richmond for an invasion that could restore the government inside Kentucky.
Simultaneous Confederate offensivesKentucky in the west and northern Virginia/Maryland in the eastRobert E. Lee approved the western movement, but he had to strike John Pope before George B. McClellan could reinforce him. The campaigns were strategically complementary, not synchronized operations.
Strategic condition at episode’s endConfederate initiative restored, but command and supply remained dividedBragg and Smith entered the campaign with energetic troops and ambitious objectives, yet separate authority, changing plans, and uncertain Kentucky recruitment threatened the undertaking from the outset.
Recommended book“Braxton Bragg: The Most Hated Man of the Confederacy” by Earl J. HessThe episode recommends Hess’s 2016 biography for a more balanced assessment of Bragg than the general’s hostile historical reputation usually receives.
