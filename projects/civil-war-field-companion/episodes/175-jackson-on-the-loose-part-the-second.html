Episode 175 · August 26–28, 1862 · Bristoe Station, Manassas Junction, Bull Run Bridge, Kettle Run, and the First Manassas battlefield in Prince William County, Virginia

JACKSON ON THE LOOSE (Part the Second)

Confederate victory in the Manassas Station Operations. Jackson captured and destroyed the principal United States supply depot at Manassas Junction, defeated George W. Taylor near Bull Run Bridge, delayed Hooker at Kettle Run, and withdrew to Stony Ridge before Pope could concentrate against him
Episode scopeThe capture of Manassas Junction and Jackson’s move to Stony RidgeThe narrative resumes at Bristoe Station on the evening of August 26 and ends after Jackson’s three divisions left Manassas Junction during the night of August 27–28.
CampaignNorthern Virginia CampaignThe National Park Service groups the actions at Bristoe Station, Manassas Junction, Bull Run Bridge, Union Mills, and Kettle Run as the Manassas Station Operations.
Lee’s objectiveForce Pope off the Rappahannock line before more reinforcements arrivedJackson’s movement against Pope’s railroad communications was intended to make the Army of Virginia turn back toward Washington. Lee and Longstreet followed Jackson through Thoroughfare Gap.
Jackson’s position on August 26In Pope’s rear at Bristoe StationJackson struck the Orange and Alexandria Railroad after marching through Thoroughfare Gap. Wrecked trains blocked the line, but other trains escaped and carried warning of the attack.
Flank-march distanceApproximately 55 miles in two daysThe NPS campaign account gives about 55 miles for Jackson’s August 25–26 movement. The episode describes his troops as exhausted after more than 50 miles.
Night assault forceAbout 500 men from the 21st Georgia and 21st North CarolinaBrig. Gen. Isaac R. Trimble volunteered his two regiments. Jackson sent Maj. Gen. J. E. B. Stuart and cavalry to reinforce them and take command of the expedition.
Manassas garrisonA small mixed force under Capt. Samuel M. CraigThe episode identifies 115 men of the 105th Pennsylvania, eight guns of the 11th New York Battery, and inexperienced troopers of the 12th Pennsylvania Cavalry. Craig treated the first reports as a guerrilla or cavalry raid.
Capture of the junctionThe garrison was routed in less than five minutesThe attack came after midnight on August 26–27. The episode gives more than 300 United States prisoners and six captured guns, with two Confederates killed and two wounded.
Captured storesMore than 100 loaded boxcars and extensive depot supplies in the episode’s accountThe stores included food, clothing, equipment, ammunition, horses, ambulances, and sutler goods. Jackson’s hungry troops consumed or carried off what they could.
Morning deploymentHill and Taliaferro at Manassas; Ewell remained at BristoeJackson moved A. P. Hill’s and William B. Taliaferro’s divisions to the junction at first light on August 27. Richard S. Ewell’s three remaining brigades screened Jackson’s rear along the railroad.
Waagner’s probeThe 2nd New York Heavy Artillery approached from CentrevilleCol. Gustavus Waagner crossed Bull Run at Mitchell’s Ford with soldiers serving as infantry and joined surviving artillerymen from the depot garrison. He withdrew when Hill deployed a much larger force.
Hill’s display of forceAbout 9,000 men and 28 guns in the episode’s accountJackson ordered Hill’s division east of Manassas Junction after hearing the artillery exchange. Waagner did not continue his advance against that line.
Taylor’s mission and advanceGuard Bull Run Bridge and a railroad repair trainTaylor instead advanced the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd New Jersey toward Manassas Junction, believing that he faced a raiding party. He left the 4th New Jersey at the bridge to await the 11th and 12th Ohio.
Bull Run Bridge actionHill held fire until Taylor was within about 300 yardsArtillery and infantry fire stopped the New Jersey advance. Jackson approached under a white flag to request surrender, but Taylor continued the action and then ordered a withdrawal.
Taylor brigade casualtiesAbout 200 captured and at least 150 killed or wounded in the episode’s accountThe retreat became a rout at the railroad bridge. The 11th and 12th Ohio covered survivors. Taylor was mortally wounded; NPS describes the total loss as several hundred casualties.
Reinforcement decision at AlexandriaMcClellan held Franklin’s and Sumner’s corps near WashingtonAfter Taylor’s unsupported defeat, McClellan stated that corps should not advance without their artillery and cavalry. He countermanded Franklin’s movement and kept Sumner at Alexandria.
Pope’s responseConcentrate at Gainesville and destroy Jackson east of the Bull Run MountainsBy midnight on August 26, Pope understood that Jackson had turned his right. His August 27 orders sought to place his army between Jackson and Longstreet while Army of the Potomac troops approached from the east.
Kettle RunHooker attacked Ewell’s rear guard during the afternoon of August 27Hooker advanced about ten miles in the heat with limited ammunition and little artillery. Ewell burned the Kettle Run bridge, resisted for several hours, and retired toward Manassas after receiving Jackson’s permission.
Kettle Run lossesAbout 600 total casualties according to NPSThe episode assigns more than 300 losses to Hooker, including roughly half of the 72nd New York, and fewer than 200 to Ewell. Historical totals vary by the boundaries used for the action.
Disposition of the depotUseful stores were issued or removed; the remainder was burnedJackson directed surgeons to take ambulances and medical supplies, mounted troops to take horses, and infantry to carry four days’ rations. Liquor was destroyed to prevent drunkenness.
Decision to evacuateUnion pressure made Manassas Junction untenable before Longstreet arrivedWaagner and Taylor approached from the east while Hooker pressed Ewell from the southwest. Jackson began his withdrawal late on August 27 and ordered the remaining depot property destroyed.
Night movement and Stony RidgeThree divisions moved toward the old First Manassas battlefieldTaliaferro took the direct route. Hill and Ewell mistakenly moved toward Centreville before backtracking. Jackson selected wooded high ground north of Groveton, where an unfinished railroad grade provided cover.
Operational resultJackson drew Pope away from the Rappahannock and avoided encirclementThe depot was destroyed, Pope’s railroad communications were disrupted, and Jackson remained intact on the old battlefield. The move set the conditions for the opening fight at Brawner Farm on August 28.
Recommended book“A Self-Made Man: The Political Life of Abraham Lincoln, 1809–1849” by Sidney BlumenthalThe hosts identify the book as unrelated to Second Manassas but recommend its treatment of Lincoln’s early political career.
Transcript normalizationsTaliaferro, Ewell, Heintzelman, Waagner, Haupt, sutlers, and Rhine wineThe transcript produces “Tolliver,” “Yule,” “Heinselman,” “Wagner,” “Hopped,” “settlers,” and “rind wine.” It also says “Leon Longstreet” once; the intended name is James Longstreet.

What happened

The episode returns to Bristoe Station

Jackson is behind Pope’s army on the evening of August 26 after cutting the Orange and Alexandria Railroad.

Manassas Junction is the next objective

The depot is four or five miles away, but Jackson’s men have marched more than 50 miles in two days.

Trimble volunteers to take the depot

Isaac R. Trimble proposes an assault by the 21st Georgia and 21st North Carolina.

Stuart reinforces the night expedition

Jackson sends Stuart’s cavalry to support Trimble’s approximately 500 infantry and take command.

Craig’s garrison is described

Samuel M. Craig holds Manassas Junction with a small mixed force of infantry, artillery, and cavalry.

Stuart drives in the pickets

Confederate cavalry reaches the outer guard first, then pauses while Trimble’s infantry comes up.

Trimble attacks after the guard stands down

The Confederate infantry strikes from the darkness and routes the depot garrison in less than five minutes.

Prisoners and artillery are captured

The episode counts more than 300 prisoners and six guns, with four Confederate casualties.

The scale of the depot becomes clear

More than 100 boxcars and warehouses contain military stores, food, ammunition, equipment, and sutler goods.

Jackson divides the morning tasks

Hill and Taliaferro move to Manassas Junction while Ewell remains at Bristoe Station to watch Pope.

Waagner approaches from Centreville

The 2nd New York Heavy Artillery crosses Bull Run and joins surviving depot artillerymen.

Hill deploys east of the junction

Jackson sends the full division to support Stuart; the episode gives 9,000 men and 28 guns. Waagner withdraws.

Taylor’s brigade moves toward Bull Run Bridge

The railroad mission is intended to secure the bridge and protect a repair train, not attack Jackson’s main force.

Taylor advances beyond his assigned position

He leaves the 4th New Jersey at the bridge and leads the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd New Jersey toward Manassas Junction.

Hill waits for close range

Jackson orders the Confederate guns to hold fire until Taylor’s line is within about 300 yards.

Jackson requests Taylor’s surrender

Jackson rides forward under a white flag after artillery and musketry stop the attack. A United States shot ends the attempt.

The bridge constricts Taylor’s retreat

The New Jersey regiments withdraw in order at first, then crowd at the bridge and break under Confederate artillery fire.

Taylor’s losses are counted

The episode gives about 200 captured and at least 150 killed or wounded. Taylor is mortally wounded.

Franklin and Sumner remain near Washington

McClellan countermanded Franklin’s movement and kept Sumner at Alexandria, preventing either corps from reaching Pope in time.

Pope recognizes that his right has been turned

He correctly places Jackson near Manassas, Longstreet near Thoroughfare Gap, and the rest of Lee’s force farther west.

Pope orders a concentration at Gainesville

The Army of Virginia is directed to close on Jackson east of the Bull Run Mountains before Longstreet can join him.

Hooker advances toward Bristoe Station

The 72nd New York reconnoiters by train, then Hooker pushes the rest of his division toward Kettle Run.

Ewell forms across the railroad

Three Confederate brigades deploy to screen Jackson’s rear and protect the route back to Manassas Junction.

Hooker pushes Ewell across Kettle Run

At about 3 p.m., the United States advance takes the bridge area; Ewell’s men burn the bridge as they withdraw.

The Kettle Run fight remains balanced for about an hour

Hooker presses despite heat, fatigue, limited ammunition, and missing artillery. His numerical advantage then forces Ewell back.

Ewell receives permission to retire

Jackson’s order allows Ewell to fall back toward Manassas before his retreat is cut off.

Kettle Run casualties are assessed

The episode gives more than 300 United States losses and fewer than 200 Confederate losses; NPS summarizes about 600 total.

Jackson distributes captured property

Surgeons take medical supplies and ambulances, mounted troops take horses, and infantry receive food from the depot.

Jackson decides that the depot cannot be held

Pressure from the east and southwest is increasing while Longstreet remains too far away. Jackson begins removing useful stores.

The remaining stores are prepared for destruction

As the divisions leave late on August 27, boxcars, warehouses, and ammunition that cannot be moved are set on fire.

Jackson selects the old battlefield

The three divisions are ordered about seven miles north toward the First Manassas field to wait for Longstreet.

Separate routes obscure the withdrawal

Taliaferro moves directly, while Hill and Ewell take wrong turns toward Centreville before correcting their routes.

Jackson chooses Stony Ridge

The wooded ridge north of Groveton conceals his troops, and the unfinished railroad grade offers defensive cover.

The book recommendation begins

The hosts recommend Sidney Blumenthal’s “A Self-Made Man,” the first volume of his political biography of Abraham Lincoln.
