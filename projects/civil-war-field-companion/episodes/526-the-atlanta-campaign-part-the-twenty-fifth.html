What happened

The June 27 assaults had failed

XV, IV, and XIV Corps had not broken the Confederate lines. Schofield’s movement along the Sandtown Road provided the basis for a new plan.

Sherman decided to reinforce the flanking movement

He believed a strong move around the Confederate left would force Johnston to abandon Kennesaw or fight outside the works.

The armies paused while Sherman accumulated supplies

The movement would temporarily leave the railroad, so U.S. forces needed several days to prepare wagons and forage.

Medical teams treated the U.S. wounded

Field hospitals received wounded men throughout the day. Some injured soldiers remained between the lines or entered Confederate works for treatment.

John Baltzly surrendered at the Confederate parapet

The 52nd Ohio sergeant had been stranded near the works and could not move without being exposed to fire.

Confederate soldiers helped Lansing Dawdy

After five hours near the works, the wounded 86th Illinois adjutant signaled for help and was carried to a Confederate field hospital.

James Jennings was wounded while assisting Newton Bostwick

The 1st Tennessee sergeant was shot in the neck while helping the wounded 17-year-old U.S. soldier across the parapet. Both survived.

Major John Yager died between the lines

Confederate soldiers judged it too dangerous to reach the wounded 121st Ohio officer. He died during the night.

U.S. soldiers searched after dark

Small groups crawled among the dead to find survivors. Henry Scott was recovered but died the next day.

The episode gives the casualty totals

U.S. losses were about 3,000 killed, wounded, and missing. Confederate losses were about 700.

Sherman planned the next operation

Schofield would extend south, McPherson would leave the U.S. left and join him, and Thomas would cover the vacated sectors.

The three armies collected ten days of supplies

The order included forage for animals. Wounded men were moved to a new hospital at Rome, Georgia.

Sherman explained the plan to Washington

He expected to reach the railroad at Fulton and force Johnston south of the Chattahoochee. Grant told him to proceed as he thought best.

Schofield prepared to lead the movement

XXIII Corps was to move beyond Nickajack Creek and enlarge the U.S. position south of Kennesaw.

A signal officer saw Atlanta

On June 30, an officer climbed a tree south of Olley’s Creek and reported seeing the city and locomotive smoke.

Schofield considered a Confederate holdout at Kennesaw

Sherman replied that he could move below Johnston, cross the Chattahoochee, and cut the railroads without attacking the works.

Johnston could not spare cavalry for a major raid

Joseph Wheeler proposed attacking Sherman’s railroad, but Johnston needed cavalry on the army’s flanks and received no reinforcements from other theaters.

Sherman issued final orders

Schofield would secure the ground between Olley’s and Nickajack creeks, McPherson would shift south, and Thomas would hold the remaining line.

Speed depended on wagon supplies

Sherman directed all movements to be rapid because the armies could operate away from the railroad only as long as their carried supplies lasted.
