Episode 203 · April 16–September 22, 1862 · Washington, D.C.; Harrison’s Landing on the James River, Virginia; northern Virginia; and the Antietam battlefield near Sharpsburg, Maryland

EMANCIPATION PROCLAMATION (Part the Third)

Congress abolished slavery in the District of Columbia and enacted the Second Confiscation Act, which freed specified people enslaved by persons supporting the rebellion and strengthened federal non-return policy. Lincoln concluded that broader emancipation was a military necessity, read a three-sentence draft to his cabinet on July 22, and accepted William H. Seward’s advice to wait for military success before issuing it. After Lee’s Maryland campaign ended in retreat following Antietam, Lincoln issued the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation on September 22. It gave notice that people held as slaves in any state or designated part of a state still in rebellion on January 1, 1863, would then be free

United States civilian authority

Secretary of StateWilliam H. SewardAdvised delaying publication until a military success and countersigned the September 22 proclamation
Secretary of the TreasurySalmon P. ChaseSupported emancipation while preferring a more incremental military method; recorded the September 22 cabinet meeting in his diary
Secretary of WarEdwin M. StantonSupported immediate publication and transmitted Lincoln’s July military order to Army commanders
Secretary of the NavyGideon WellesHeard Lincoln’s first disclosure on July 13, remained silent in the July 22 cabinet discussion, and later recorded the decision sequence
Attorney GeneralEdward BatesSupported immediate release of the proclamation despite his conservative views on Black civil and political equality
Postmaster GeneralMontgomery BlairOpposed immediate publication because he feared Republican losses in the 1862 elections
Secretary of the InteriorCaleb B. SmithRemained silent during the July 22 discussion while holding private reservations about the policy

United States military chain
