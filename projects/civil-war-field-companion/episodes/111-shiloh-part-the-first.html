What happened

The episode introduces Shiloh

The battle took place on April 6 and 7 near Pittsburg Landing, a river landing beside scattered farms and Shiloh Church.

The battle produced more than 23,000 casualties

The episode compares the two-day total with the combined losses at First Manassas, Wilson’s Creek, Fort Donelson, and Pea Ridge.

The battlefield restricted movement

Forests, thick undergrowth, ridges, ravines, and swamps made it difficult to keep formations aligned. The Tennessee River and Owl and Snake creeks bounded the area.

Many troops had little training

Most soldiers in both armies had no battle experience, and some had received little instruction with their weapons.

Fort Henry and Fort Donelson changed the theater

Grant’s February victories broke the Confederate line in Kentucky and northern Tennessee and opened the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers.

Johnston ordered a general withdrawal

Confederate forces abandoned Bowling Green and Nashville to avoid being cut off and defeated separately.

Johnston’s column moved south

About 15,000 men withdrew from Bowling Green through Nashville and Murfreesboro toward northern Alabama while Buell followed.

Nashville fell

United States troops occupied the Tennessee capital and major supply center on February 25.

Polk abandoned Columbus

The Fort Henry and Donelson victories outflanked the Confederate position on the Mississippi River, forcing Polk to retreat south.

Pope advanced on New Madrid and Island No. 10

About 25,000 United States troops moved down the newly opened stretch of the Mississippi River.

Johnston continued into Alabama

His command left Murfreesboro on February 28 and reached Decatur on March 15 after abandoning most of western and central Tennessee.

Beauregard organized the western concentration

He directed Polk’s withdrawal, reinforced Island No. 10, and gathered troops from across the lower South at Corinth.

Corinth was the key rail junction

The Memphis & Charleston Railroad crossed the Mobile & Ohio at Corinth, connecting the lower Mississippi region with Chattanooga and Richmond.

Johnston reached Corinth

When his column arrived on March 24, troops under Polk, Ruggles, and Bragg were already assembling there.

The Army of the Mississippi reached about 40,000 men

Van Dorn’s force from Arkansas had not arrived, and the troops captured at Fort Donelson were unavailable.

Two United States armies moved toward Corinth

Grant’s army approached by the Tennessee River while Buell marched overland from Nashville.

Halleck received the unified western command

He became Buell’s superior and planned to take field command after Grant and Buell combined.

Halleck accused Grant of disobedience

Missing reports and an unauthorized visit to Nashville led Halleck to complain to Washington and imply that Grant had resumed drinking, without evidence.

Grant was removed temporarily

Halleck placed C.F. Smith in field command and ordered Grant to remain at Fort Henry.

Lincoln required evidence or Grant’s restoration

Halleck brought no charges and returned Grant to command on March 13.

Smith moved the army toward Savannah

The expedition traveled up the Tennessee River to establish a base, threaten the Memphis & Charleston Railroad, and meet Sherman’s new division.

Johnston chose to attack before Buell arrived

Grant’s and Johnston’s forces were then of similar size. The combined United States force would have a large numerical advantage.

The decision produced the Battle of Shiloh

Johnston planned to move north from Corinth and defeat Grant near Pittsburg Landing before the United States armies united.
