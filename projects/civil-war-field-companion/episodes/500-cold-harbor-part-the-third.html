What happened

Early’s June 2 attack had failed

Jubal Early did not turn the U.S. right at Bethesda Church, leaving Grant free to continue the planned assault at Cold Harbor.

Lee strengthened the Cold Harbor line

Two divisions from A. P. Hill’s corps and John C. Breckinridge’s division reinforced the sector.

Confederates secured Turkey Hill

Breckinridge and Cadmus Wilcox drove Hancock’s troops from the hill, protecting the Confederate right near the Chickahominy River.

Engineers laid out new earthworks

After dark, Confederate officers selected firing lines, marked them with rope, and directed infantry working parties to dig.

Evander Law shortened a weak sector

Law replaced an exposed right-angle position with a shorter line across its base and a better field of fire.

U.S. soldiers expected heavy losses

Across a gap of roughly 600 to 1,000 yards, soldiers watched the Confederate works improve and prepared identification slips for their bodies.

Rain and fog covered the battlefield

Overnight rain turned the ground to mud, and patchy fog remained in low areas before the attack.

The assault signal sounded

At 4:30 a.m. on June 3, a gun from the 10th Massachusetts Battery signaled the attack. U.S. artillery fired for about ten minutes before the infantry advanced.

Prepared fire stopped most of the advance

Confederate infantry and artillery opened from earthworks. The curved U.S. line caused the attacking corps to separate and exposed them to crossfire.

Hancock’s II Corps attacked first

Francis Barlow and John Gibbon led two divisions south of the Gaines Mill Road while David Birney remained in reserve.

Barlow broke into Breckinridge’s line

Nelson Miles and John R. Brooke struck a section whose defenders had left flooded trenches to sleep uphill. They captured about 425 men and eight guns.

The breach was not reinforced

Gibbon was delayed, one supporting brigade did not arrive, and Birney’s reserve remained back. Confederate counterattacks retook the guns and closed the opening.

II Corps lost nearly 3,000 men

Hancock’s two attacking divisions suffered nearly 3,000 casualties in less than an hour.

Wright’s VI Corps attack stopped quickly

David Russell delayed for flank support, James Ricketts advanced into heavy fire, and Horatio Wright ended the effort before losses grew further.

Smith’s XVIII Corps attacked without a reserve

William F. Smith sent George Martindale and William Brooks forward while a third division covered an extended front. Neither assault reached the main works.

Burnside and Warren advanced at 6 a.m.

Burnside drove in Confederate pickets on the U.S. right but stopped before the main line to bring up more artillery. Warren committed only two brigades.

Early reports gave Meade an incorrect picture

Barlow’s temporary breakthrough and incomplete messages led Meade to believe a renewed general attack might still succeed.

Grant left the decision with Meade

At 7 a.m. Grant told Meade to stop when failure was certain but to reinforce any point where an assault succeeded.

Meade ordered the three southern corps to press again

Hancock advised against another assault, Smith refused to repeat his attack, and Wright’s subordinates increased their fire without making another full advance.

Grant collected the corps commanders’ assessments

His staff found that none of the commanders expected a renewed attack to produce a useful breakthrough.

Grant suspended further attacks

At 12:30 p.m. Grant authorized Meade to stop the advance. Meade then ordered the army to hold and entrench its current positions.

The casualty total was about 7,000

The Army of the Potomac recorded losses for a broader period, but estimates place June 3 U.S. casualties near 7,000, most in the first hour.

Confederate losses were much lower

The Confederate army lost fewer than 1,500 men, with only about 500 killed or wounded opposite the main U.S. attacks.

Lee had no reserve for a counterattack

Lee told Confederate postmaster general John H. Reagan that shortening or weakening his line would allow Grant either to turn or break it.

Wounded soldiers remained between the lines

The armies did not agree on a truce until the evening of June 7. Most wounded men had been recovered at night or had died by then.
