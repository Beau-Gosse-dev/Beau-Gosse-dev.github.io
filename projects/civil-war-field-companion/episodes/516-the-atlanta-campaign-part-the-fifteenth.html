What happened

The episode compares the two 1864 campaigns

Grant’s Overland Campaign had produced more than 50,000 casualties in May. Sherman had generally used maneuver to limit similar losses in Georgia.

Sherman had advanced nearly 70 miles

By early June, the U.S. armies remained supplied by rail and were moving closer to Atlanta while preventing Johnston from reinforcing Lee.

Richmond pressured Johnston

Jefferson Davis and Braxton Bragg were concerned about the loss of ground in Georgia and wanted Johnston to produce a victory.

The U.S. armies returned to the railroad

After the Dallas operations, Sherman concentrated near Acworth and reconnected with the Western and Atlantic Railroad.

Sherman chose a closer movement toward Marietta

He repaired the railroad and planned to use nearby roads rather than repeat the wide movement away from his supply line.

Johnston occupied high ground west of Marietta

The Confederate army withdrew into the ridges and heights around Pine, Lost, and Kennesaw mountains.

Daily skirmishing defined the June fighting

Both armies used skirmish lines, sharpshooters, and rapidly built earthworks as they advanced short distances and maintained close contact.

U.S. forces advanced on June 10

Sherman moved from Acworth along Sandtown Road, staying near the railroad while pressing Johnston’s main line.

Thomas reached Pine Mountain

By June 12, the Army of the Cumberland pressured the Confederate center at the forward salient.

Johnston, Hardee, and Polk inspected Pine Mountain

On June 14, the three generals went to the summit to examine their own line and the U.S. positions.

Sherman ordered artillery to fire on the group

U.S. gunners fired after Sherman saw Confederate officers exposed on the summit.

Leonidas Polk was killed

An artillery shell struck Polk in the left side and killed him instantly. Johnston and Hardee survived.

The Confederates abandoned Pine Mountain

Johnston withdrew from the vulnerable salient, and William W. Loring assumed temporary command of Polk’s corps.

Short U.S. advances forced additional withdrawals

Sherman ordered pressure along the line after Pine Mountain was evacuated. The armies fought at Noonday Creek and Gilgal Church on June 15.

Johnston moved into the final Kennesaw line

On June 19, the Army of Tennessee occupied Big and Little Kennesaw and the surrounding ridges.

The Kennesaw position covered Marietta and the railroad

Loring held the mountain heights, Hood held the Confederate right near the railroad, and Hardee extended south.

Big Kennesaw dominated the surrounding ground

The peak rose about 700 feet above the plain. Little Kennesaw and Pigeon Hill extended the high ground to the south.

The mountain protected the approach to Atlanta

The position covered the Western and Atlantic Railroad and Marietta, with the Chattahoochee River and Atlanta farther south.

Confederate artillery used the heights

Guns on the mountain fired into U.S. camps and lines below. U.S. batteries responded with counterbattery fire.

Persistent rain slowed operations

Heavy rain turned roads into mud, complicated supply and movement, and left soldiers on both sides without dry shelter.

Sherman looked toward the Confederate left

He ruled out a northern movement near his railroad and preferred to extend Schofield and Thomas south of the mountain.

IV Corps fought for Bald Knob

On June 20 and 21, Howard’s troops seized high ground near the Confederate center, helping divert attention from Schofield’s movement farther south.

Johnston shifted Hood’s corps

To counter the U.S. extension, Hood moved from the Confederate right to the far left on the night of June 21.

Hood’s new assignment was defensive

Johnston wanted Hood to protect the southern flank. Hood’s aggressive response on June 22 led to the Battle of Kolb’s Farm.
