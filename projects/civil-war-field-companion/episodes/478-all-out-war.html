Episode 478 · March–May 1864 · United States and Confederate theaters of war, with main operations in Virginia and Georgia

ALL-OUT WAR

Grant replaced disconnected departmental campaigns with coordinated offensives against the Confederacy’s principal field armies and supply systems. Sherman was assigned to break up Johnston’s army and advance toward Atlanta. In Virginia, Meade would follow Lee’s army, Sigel would move through the Shenandoah Valley, and Butler would land at Bermuda Hundred against Richmond’s rail connections. The Mobile operation and Grant’s earlier North Carolina proposal were deferred. The strategy depended on applying pressure at the same time so the Confederacy could not shift reinforcements between fronts

What happened

Theodore Lyman assessed Grant

The Army of the Potomac staff officer recognized the new general-in-chief’s competence despite his plain appearance and earlier personal difficulties.

Grant entered the role with major victories

His record included the surrender of Confederate armies at Fort Donelson and Vicksburg and victories at Shiloh and Chattanooga.

Grant’s task was national strategy

As general-in-chief, he had to design and supervise operations that would defeat the rebellion and restore the Union.

The Anaconda Plan had achieved major goals

Vicksburg opened the Mississippi River and the naval blockade increasingly restricted Confederate imports.

Time favored Confederate endurance

After three bloody years, Northern war weariness created a possibility that the Confederacy could survive by outlasting public support.

The eastern stalemate carried political weight

Campaigns between Washington and Richmond received exceptional attention, while repeated U.S. failures strengthened public frustration.

Lincoln identified the interior-lines problem

He wanted superior U.S. forces to attack at several points simultaneously so Confederate troops could not move between threatened fronts.

Grant received his commission

Grant became lieutenant general on March 9, 1864, after months of considering operations for the next campaigning season.

Grant and Halleck exchanged broad proposals

Ideas included operations in Mississippi, Mobile, Atlanta, North Carolina, and up the James River against Richmond’s railroads.

Halleck became chief of staff

He transmitted orders and coordinated departments, while final strategic authority rested with Grant.

Grant consulted the principal army commanders

He visited Meade’s Army of the Potomac and then traveled to Nashville to meet Sherman and other western generals.

Sherman received the Atlanta assignment

Three U.S. armies would move from Chattanooga against Joseph E. Johnston’s Army of Tennessee along the railroad toward Atlanta.

Grant defined Sherman’s objective

Sherman was to break up Johnston’s army, penetrate Confederate territory, and damage resources supporting the war.

Mobile was planned as a supporting operation

Grant wanted an Army–Navy expedition against the Confederacy’s remaining major Gulf port.

The Red River campaign delayed Mobile

Lincoln and Halleck had already committed Nathaniel Banks to Louisiana, so Grant deferred the Gulf operation.

Grant had considered a North Carolina main effort

He proposed a 60,000-man movement through Raleigh and New Bern toward Wilmington and its blockade-running trade.

Washington required the main eastern attack against Lee

Halleck advised that Lincoln would not support a plan that left the Army of the Potomac and Lee’s army outside the principal operation.

Grant adopted a three-part Virginia plan

The Army of the Potomac would attack Lee while other forces threatened his supplies, reinforcements, railroads, and Richmond.

Sigel received the Shenandoah assignment

His troops would move south through the valley to restrict food shipments and prevent Confederate reinforcement of Lee or Richmond.

Butler received the James River assignment

The Army of the James would land at Bermuda Hundred and threaten the Richmond–Petersburg railroad and the Confederate capital.

The Army of the Potomac remained the main effort

Meade’s army would operate directly against Robert E. Lee and the Army of Northern Virginia.

Grant retained Meade

Meade offered to step aside, but Grant kept him in command of the Army of the Potomac.

Meade was ordered to follow Lee

Grant made Lee’s army the objective and directed Meade to go wherever Lee moved.

Destruction did not require one decisive battle

Grant expected maneuver, interrupted supplies, and threats to Richmond to make Lee’s position unsustainable even without a single annihilating engagement.

The offensives shared one plan

For the first time, major U.S. armies across the Confederacy were directed to apply coordinated pressure at the same time.

Field armies were the central objectives

Grant aimed to destroy Johnston’s and Lee’s armies, expecting the rebellion to collapse when its main organized forces could no longer fight.

The strategy depended on Northern endurance

Military pressure could succeed only if voters and soldiers accepted the casualties and costs needed to continue the war.

The election and battlefield results were linked

Progress in Virginia and Georgia would directly affect Lincoln’s prospects in the 1864 presidential election.
