Episode 185 · September 14, 1862 · Fox’s Gap, Turner’s Gap, and the high ground toward Frosttown Gap on South Mountain between Middletown and Boonsboro, Maryland

SOUTH MOUNTAIN (Part the Third)

United States tactical victory on the northern South Mountain front. IX Corps held Fox’s Gap, and I Corps drove back the Confederate left north of Turner’s Gap. Confederate troops still occupied Turner’s Gap at dark, but both flanks had been turned and Robert E. Lee ordered the mountain line abandoned during the night
Episode scopeThe afternoon fight at Fox’s Gap and the late attack north of Turner’s GapThe episode resumes after Jacob Cox’s morning victory at Fox’s Gap. It follows the failed Confederate counterattack, Jesse Reno’s death, and Joseph Hooker’s I Corps assault toward Frosttown Gap. The next episode covers Crampton’s Gap and the Confederate withdrawal.
Battle dateSunday, September 14, 1862The engagements at Fox’s, Turner’s, Frosttown, and Crampton’s Gaps formed the Battle of South Mountain during the Maryland Campaign.
Confederate purposeDelay McClellan while Jackson completed the capture of Harpers FerryLee rejected Longstreet’s proposal to concentrate immediately behind Antietam Creek. Holding the mountain passes protected the rear of the Confederate columns operating against Harpers Ferry and bought time for Lee’s divided army to reunite.
Longstreet’s marchFrom Hagerstown toward Boonsboro and South MountainLongstreet started about 8 a.m. The head of his column covered the roughly ten miles to Boonsboro in about four hours, then continued toward the summit in heat and choking dust. Later countermarching added distance for several brigades.
Lee’s positionNear the western foot of South Mountain with both hands splintedUnable to ride or direct the fight closely, Lee left immediate battlefield control to D. H. Hill and then Longstreet. He encouraged the passing troops as Confederate reinforcements climbed toward the gaps.
Hill’s intended counterattackFour brigades were to retake Fox’s GapRoswell Ripley had tactical responsibility for coordinating the brigades of Ripley, George B. Anderson, George T. Anderson, and Thomas Drayton. Broken ground, dense vegetation, separation between units, and the renewed Federal advance prevented the concerted attack Hill intended.
Federal renewal at Fox’s GapWillcox advanced at about 4 p.m.; Sturgis supported himOrlando B. Willcox’s division moved against the Confederate guns after a long midday lull. Samuel D. Sturgis’s division, with the brigades of James Nagle and Edward Ferrero, increased the pressure.
Drayton’s isolationOne Confederate brigade received the main Federal blowThe intended Confederate line never formed coherently. Drayton’s brigade, about 1,300 strong in the National Park Service account, was struck from more than one direction and lost about half its strength killed, wounded, or captured.
3rd South Carolina BattalionOnly 24 of 160 men emerged unhurtThe battalion held until the last moment during Drayton’s collapse. Its commander, Lt. Col. George S. James, was killed.
Hood restored for battleLee suspended John Bell Hood’s arrest without securing an apologyHood had been under arrest after a dispute with Nathan G. “Shanks” Evans over captured ambulances. When Hood’s soldiers called for him, Lee returned him to command for the emergency, and Hood led his two brigades toward Fox’s Gap.
Reno’s deathThe IX Corps commander was mortally wounded near Fox’s Gap at duskReno rode too near a Confederate position in the gathering darkness and was shot in the chest. He died while being carried down the mountain, near the ground where Confederate brigadier Samuel Garland had been mortally wounded earlier that day.
IX Corps losses157 killed, 691 wounded, and 41 missingThe episode gives 889 IX Corps casualties at Fox’s Gap. The U.S. Army campaign study likewise describes Union losses there as approaching 900; it places Confederate losses above 1,100.
Double envelopmentIX Corps attacked the Confederate right; I Corps attacked the leftBurnside’s right wing operated across a front approaching three miles. Reno’s corps fought at Fox’s Gap while Hooker’s corps moved north of the National Road toward the high ground around Frosttown Gap.
I Corps command changesGeorge Meade replaced John Reynolds; John Hatch replaced Rufus KingPennsylvania’s governor had obtained Reynolds for state militia duty during the invasion emergency. King’s continuing health problems led to Hatch assuming his division on September 14. When Hatch was wounded, Abner Doubleday took command.
Hooker’s planMeade on the right, Hatch on the left, Ricketts in reserveMeade’s Pennsylvania Reserves were to seize the northern ridge and push through the ravine to turn the Confederate left. Hatch attacked the parallel spur closer to the National Road. James B. Ricketts’s division was available to support either attack.
Attack beginsJust before 5 p.m.The late start left little daylight. Steep, wooded, rocky ground disrupted alignment and coordination, but also concealed parts of the Federal advance.
Rodes’s forceAbout 1,200 Alabamians on a long, exposed frontRobert E. Rodes reported that the Federal line overlapped both ends of his brigade. His regiments were separated by difficult ground, and no substantial reinforcement reached the exposed far left during the initial attack.
Stevens’s reinforcementAbout 550 South CaroliniansCol. Peter F. Stevens led the small contingent from Nathan G. Evans’s brigade toward Rodes. Together the two Confederate forces numbered about 1,800 against roughly 4,000 men in Meade’s division.
Seymour turns the flankThe Federal right gained the northern ridgeTruman Seymour’s brigade drove back the 6th Alabama and gained the commanding high ground. All three brigades of Meade’s division then pressed the Confederate retreat despite losing contact with one another in the rough terrain.
Meade’s lossesNearly 400 casualties at South MountainThe Pennsylvania Reserves carried the difficult northern ground. Darkness finally ended their pursuit after Rodes formed another line about 200 yards north of the National Road.
Hatch and GibbonHatch attacked the southern spur; Gibbon advanced on the National RoadHatch’s division moved alongside Meade while John Gibbon’s “Black Hat” brigade fought directly up the road. The combined pressure turned the Confederate left and threatened Turner’s Gap.
Hatch woundedShot through the right calfHatch was hit as his troops carried a stone-wall position. He left the field and transferred division command to Abner Doubleday.
Longstreet takes field commandNear 5 p.m. at the Mountain HouseBy seniority Longstreet superseded D. H. Hill. He warned Lee to prepare for disappointment and a retreat while committing exhausted brigades that had marched as much as 18 to 20 miles before entering the fight.
Position at nightfallThe United States held Fox’s Gap and the high ground north of Turner’s GapConfederates still occupied Turner’s Gap itself, but their right had been displaced at Fox’s Gap and their left driven back. The position could not be held safely the next morning, and Lee withdrew after dark.
McClellan’s dispatch“The corps of Hooker and Reno have carried the heights”At about 9:40 p.m. McClellan reported a “glorious victory” to Henry W. Halleck. He did not yet know the outcome of William B. Franklin’s separate fight at Crampton’s Gap six miles south.
Operational consequenceA Confederate retreat, but enough delay to help the Harpers Ferry operationThe mountain defense did not stop McClellan. It did consume September 14, while Jackson’s forces completed the investment of Harpers Ferry, whose garrison surrendered the next morning.
Recommended book“Unholy Sabbath: The Battle of South Mountain in History and Memory”The episode and the official podcast book list identify Brian Matthew Jordan’s study as the recommendation.
Transcript normalizationsRodes, Willcox, Nagle, Ferrero, Meade, Ricketts, Peter F. Stevens, Ashbel F. Hill, and BoonsboroThe automated transcript produces phonetic or simplified forms including “Rhodes,” “Wilcox,” “Nagel,” “Ferraro,” “Mead,” “Rickett,” and “Ashbilla.” Sources use both Frosttown and Frostown for the northern gap; this guide follows the Frosttown spelling on Sneden’s map and in the transcript. The earlier Second Manassas action mentioned in passing was Brawner’s Farm, not “Bronner’s Farm.”

What happened

The episode resumes at Fox’s Gap

Cox has defeated Garland’s brigade and consolidated instead of immediately pushing north toward Turner’s Gap.

Lee chooses to fight for South Mountain

The episode reviews Lee’s decision to delay McClellan rather than yield the passes and endanger the operation against Harpers Ferry.

Longstreet’s column starts from Hagerstown

Heat, dust, and exhaustion thin the Confederate reinforcements during the march toward Boonsboro and the mountain.

Lee reaches the foot of the mountain

With both hands in splints and unable to ride, Lee encourages passing troops while leaving immediate control to subordinates.

Hooker threatens the Confederate left

D. H. Hill sees I Corps maneuvering toward Frosttown Gap, where Rodes has only about 1,200 men.

Hill orders a counterattack at Fox’s Gap

Four brigades are assigned to retake the gap before the expected Federal attack falls on the opposite flank.

The Confederate plan breaks down

Boulders, steep slopes, laurel thickets, and separated units make coordinated movement nearly impossible.

Willcox reinforces the Federal line

His IX Corps division takes position beside Cox under Confederate artillery fire.

The afternoon attack begins

At about 4 p.m., Willcox moves forward to silence the Confederate guns after the long lull at Fox’s Gap.

Sturgis adds two brigades

Nagle and Ferrero advance to support Willcox, increasing the pressure on Drayton’s isolated brigade.

The 3rd South Carolina Battalion holds on

Only 24 of its 160 men emerge unhurt, and Lt. Col. George S. James is killed.

Drayton’s brigade is shattered

The failure of the planned Confederate deployment leaves Drayton to absorb the main attack; about half the brigade is lost.

Hood’s veterans approach

Hood’s two brigades reach the western base of the mountain after following D. R. Jones’s division from Hagerstown.

“Give us Hood”

Lee suspends Hood’s arrest when the soldiers call for their commander; Hood refuses to apologize but returns to duty for the battle.

Reno rides toward Fox’s Gap

At twilight the IX Corps commander moves forward from the high ground near Bolivar to congratulate his men.

Reno is mortally wounded

A bullet enters his chest below the heart after he approaches a Confederate position in the gathering darkness.

Reno speaks to Sturgis

The wounded corps commander recognizes his West Point classmate and friend, tells him he is dying, and dies soon afterward.

IX Corps holds Fox’s Gap

The Confederate counterattack has failed; Garland’s and Drayton’s brigades have suffered especially severe losses.

I Corps advances through Middletown

Hooker’s command follows the National Road toward the northern part of the South Mountain position.

Two division commands change

Meade replaces the detached Reynolds, and Hatch replaces the ailing King as the corps prepares to attack.

Burnside sets the double envelopment

With IX Corps at Fox’s Gap, Hooker’s I Corps is directed around the opposite flank north of the National Road.

Hooker assigns the divisions

Meade will attack the north ridge and ravine, Hatch the spur nearer Turner’s Gap, with Ricketts in reserve.

The attack steps off

Just before 5 p.m., the Federal formations begin climbing the steep, wooded ground.

Otis D. Smith remembers the ridge

The 6th Alabama private describes Rodes’s exposed position and a civilian who refuses to leave her mountain home.

Rodes’s regiments are spread thin

The difficult ground separates the Alabama units while Meade’s line extends well beyond the Confederate left.

Stevens brings a small reinforcement

About 550 South Carolinians join Rodes against roughly 4,000 Pennsylvania Reserves.

Seymour takes the north ridge

His brigade drives back the 6th Alabama and turns Rodes’s left, opening the way for Meade’s other brigades.

Ashbel F. Hill describes the pursuit

The 8th Pennsylvania Reserves sergeant recalls flanking South Carolinians and the close-range violence on the rocky slope.

Rodes’s first line breaks

Federal soldiers press the retreating Alabamians from tree to tree and boulder to boulder.

Rodes rallies another line

Survivors form around John B. Gordon’s 6th Alabama, but another flanking movement forces them toward Turner’s Gap.

Hatch attacks beside Meade

Hatch’s division advances toward the southern spur as the Pennsylvania Reserves fight on its right.

Longstreet takes command on the mountain

Arriving near 5 p.m., he warns Lee to prepare for retreat while committing brigades exhausted by their long march.

Gibbon attacks on the National Road

The “Black Hat” brigade advances directly toward Turner’s Gap as other I Corps troops press both ridges.

Hatch is wounded

A bullet passes through his right calf after his troops cross a defended stone wall; Doubleday assumes the division.

Darkness ends the fight

Confederates still occupy Turner’s Gap, but the Federal attacks have gained the heights and turned both flanks.

McClellan reports victory

At about 9:40 p.m. he tells Halleck that Hooker and Reno have carried the heights, while acknowledging uncertainty about the night.

The episode’s book is recommended

The hosts recommend Brian Matthew Jordan’s “Unholy Sabbath: The Battle of South Mountain in History and Memory.”
