What happened

The U.S. armies rested near Acworth

After the Dallas operations, soldiers camped south of Allatoona while engineers repaired the Etowah River railroad bridge.

The railroad reopened on June 11

Supplies again moved down the Western and Atlantic Railroad without interruption.

XVII Corps reinforcements arrived

About 9,000 men in two divisions joined the Army of the Tennessee on June 8.

High ground dominated the approach to Marietta

Lost, Pine, Brush, and Kennesaw mountains overlooked the roads and railroad leading south.

Kennesaw formed a three-part ridge

Big Kennesaw, Little Kennesaw, and Pigeon Hill extended for about two miles, with Confederate signal stations, artillery, and trenches.

The railroad fixed Sherman’s route

A movement east would expose the rail line and Allatoona depot. Sherman used Sandtown Road for his southward advance.

U.S. forces advanced on June 10

Cavalry found a fortified Confederate line from the railroad southwest to Lost Mountain.

Pine Mountain projected ahead of Johnston’s line

William B. Bate’s division held the salient, linked by trenches to the main Confederate works.

Thomas threatened to isolate Bate

By June 12, the Army of the Cumberland moved east of Pine Mountain and threatened the connection with Kennesaw.

Three Confederate generals inspected Pine Mountain

Johnston, Hardee, and Leonidas Polk climbed the exposed summit on June 14.

Sherman ordered artillery fire

U.S. gunners fired at the group after Sherman saw officers and soldiers on the summit.

Leonidas Polk was killed

Johnston and Hardee took cover. An artillery projectile passed through Polk’s body and killed him.

Loring took temporary corps command

William W. Loring replaced Polk, and Bate withdrew from Pine Mountain that night.

Schofield forced another Confederate adjustment

On June 16, U.S. troops gained ground that exposed Hardee’s line to enfilading artillery fire.

Hardee withdrew behind Mud Creek

The movement forced the Confederates to abandon Lost Mountain and created the second Kennesaw line.

Thomas exposed the second line

On June 17, U.S. artillery positions could enfilade Hardee again. Johnston ordered engineers to finish a stronger line.

The army occupied the final Kennesaw line

During the night of June 18–19, Hardee held the left, Loring the mountain ridge, and Hood the right, with cavalry on both flanks.

The U.S. right extended south

Hooker moved toward Powder Springs Road and Schofield moved from Lost Mountain toward Kolb’s Farm.

Johnston shifted Hood to the left

On the night of June 21–22, Hood’s corps moved south to block Hooker and Schofield.

U.S. prisoners identified Hood’s move

Captured men from Hood’s corps warned Hooker and Schofield, who halted and built fieldworks.

Hood attacked at Kolb’s Farm

Hood interpreted the retreat of two U.S. reconnaissance regiments as an attack and sent two divisions forward.

U.S. artillery and rough ground stopped the attack

The Confederate advance failed. Hood lost about 1,000 men; U.S. losses were about 300, mostly in the forward regiments.
