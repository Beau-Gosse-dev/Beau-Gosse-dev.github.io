Episode 117 · April 6, 1862, early afternoon; Johnston died at about 2:30 p.m. · Bell Field and the peach orchard on the eastern side of the Shiloh battlefield, Tennessee

SHILOH- Death of Albert Sidney Johnston

Albert Sidney Johnston personally led Statham’s brigade across Bell’s cotton field while Bowen advanced east of the Hamburg–Savannah Road. The attack forced Hurlbut’s troops out of the peach orchard. Johnston then ordered Statham to attack a United States battery west of the orchard. A projectile struck the back of Johnston’s right leg and damaged an artery. He did not recognize the wound’s severity, was moved into a ravine after collapsing in the saddle, and bled to death at about 2:30 p.m. The Confederate army lost its commander during the battle

What happened

Johnston directed the Confederate right from near the front

He left Beauregard in the rear to send reinforcements toward the firing while he supervised attacks on the eastern battlefield.

Johnston decided to lead from the firing line

After repeated attacks failed at the peach orchard, the army commander accepted the risk of personally leading the next assault.

Johnston rallied Statham’s brigade

The six regiments sheltered in a gully south of Bell Field. Johnston rode along the line and directed them to use their bayonets.

Johnston led the charge

He rode ahead of Statham’s brigade into the cotton field while Bowen’s brigade advanced on the other side of the Hamburg–Savannah Road.

Hurlbut’s line left the peach orchard

The United States infantry fired heavily but withdrew before the Confederate charge reached bayonet range.

Several projectiles struck Johnston and his horse

Two bullets hit his horse, one spent round struck his thigh, and another cut through the sole of his left boot without disabling him.

A United States battery opened west of the orchard

Johnston sent for Confederate artillery and ordered Isham Harris to have one of Statham’s regiments charge the battery.

Johnston was left with one aide

Other staff officers carried orders. W. Lee Wickham remained nearby and heard a projectile strike but did not see an obvious wound.

Johnston collapsed in the saddle

When Harris returned, Johnston sagged to the left and said that he feared he was seriously wounded.

Harris and Wickham moved Johnston into a ravine

They supported him on horseback, removed him from the saddle in sheltered ground, and sent for a surgeon.

Johnston’s surgeon was elsewhere

Earlier, Johnston had ordered Dr. David Yandell to remain with wounded United States and Confederate soldiers near Prentiss’s captured camps.

The staff could not locate the wound

Harris searched Johnston’s torso while Theodore O’Hara and other officers tried to find medical help.

Johnston died at about 2:30 p.m.

He stopped breathing roughly 25–30 minutes after reaching the ravine.

The leg wound caused fatal blood loss

A projectile entered behind and below the right knee and damaged an artery. Johnston had a tourniquet but apparently did not understand the injury in time to use it.

The charge succeeded at a major command cost

Statham and nearby formations advanced, but the Confederate army was left without its commanding general during the battle.
